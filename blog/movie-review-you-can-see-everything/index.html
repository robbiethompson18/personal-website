Movie Review: You Can See Everything

★★☆☆☆

Published November 27, 2026Last edited November 27, 2026

Exiting the cinema at the end of You Can See Everything, I felt a distinct sensation from high school. The crowd was chittering, finger-pointing, gleeful to have spent three hours watching Nathan Fielder cut down Elizabeth Holmes, Billy Evans, and every other tall, stupid, self-important poppy in the San Francisco Bay Area.⁠[1]

My main takeaway from You Can See Everything was: “Wow, I expected Elizabeth to be a better liar.” It took about 45 seconds for the first unconvincing bout of crocodile tears, at which point I lost a lot of interest.

It was also striking how dumb Elizabeth and Billy looked. Early in the movie there’s a shot of Billy on the couch listening to a YouTube video explaining biology on the level of “… the mitochondria, which you’ll recall is the powerhouse of the cell.”

I am confident that Elizabeth was clueless and scientifically unconvincing. A close family friend who is a biotech founder and doctor stated very confidently years before the blowup that Theranos was fraudulent. Theranos would have needed to make massive advances across four fields of diagnostics to be able to do what they were claiming, and this was implausible given that they had no peer-reviewed papers. You could get away with this in software, but not in medicine. The FDA wants papers.

I know startup founders like this, people who are clueless about their own business and focus exclusively on hoodwinking gullible investors. It is far too common. But I still bristle at the extent to which the crowd in that theatre drew conclusions about all of Silicon Valley. Trickery works for your seed round, but big public companies cannot operate in this way.⁠[2] They have to deliver value. Nothing about Elizabeth Holmes should make you change your opinion of Sergey Brin or Dario Amodei; they are not the same species.⁠[3]

The contrast to The Wolf of Wall Street is stark. Jordan Belfort made his fortune from deception. But he is a beloved character; people make raps in his honour. He is a powerful and impressive liar, not a bungling and tragicomic one. Powerful and impressive liars imply that the people they trick aren’t stupid. You Can See Everything, by highlighting just how clueless Elizabeth and Billy are, begs the question: “just how dumb is the rest of Silicon Valley?” I dislike this message. The Silicon Valley of my lived experience does include many VCs who don’t know much about technology,⁠[4] but genuinely brilliant engineers are much more common.⁠[5]

The Altruists was upsetting for different reasons. I confess I only watched half of the first episode.

There are many parallels between my life and SBF’s, which makes my family cackle. We both took a job at Jane Street right out of college. Both of us are effective altruists and polyamorists and former vegans.

The conclusions you’re meant to draw from that show:

  • Being calculating and rational inevitably leads to ruthless consequentialism.
  • Being calculating and rational means you’re socially inept.
  • Being calculating and rational leads you to take excessive risks, often on others’ behalf.

There’s a lot to dislike about SBF. He is a ruthless lying power-seeking consequentialist. Unlike Elizabeth Holmes, he’s smart enough to be extremely convincing to an educated interlocutor. His story merits cinema much more than Holmes’. But I dislike the implication that him being evil is downstream of his calculation and rationality. The world is full of calculating and rational people who truly are altruists. Even the title of the show implies otherwise.

This essay reads as: “why can’t Hollywood be pushing my particular agenda?!?” That’s too much to ask, I understand. Just know, ‘Hollywood,’ and friends who would ask me to see a movie with them: this is why I don’t want to watch.

Notes #

  1. This was partly selection. Tuesdays are half-price at AMC. The crowd has few rich young tech yuppies, and lots of students and (for lack of a better term, said in jest) future members of the permanent underclass. ↩︎

  2. There are many examples of big public companies committing accounting fraud and few examples of them faking their technology. (Though some exist, eg Dieselgate.) ↩︎

  3. I have a very different set of criticisms for Dario and Sergey! Possibly they are too ruthless and consequentialist, but they are not naked liars larping as technologists like Elizabeth. ↩︎

  4. My mental model of VCs: tremendously good at reading people, leveraging connections to discover things, and getting people to cooperate. Bad at futurism and engineering. They know which people they invest in are overconfident and slippery with the truth; both of these things are strongly positively correlated with being a successful founder. ↩︎

  5. In my gut I watch The Wolf of Wall Street and I laugh: the guy ripped off idiots who should’ve known better and then acted like a playboy. It’s a comedy. In my head I know I shouldn’t be happy that we’re glorifying fraud. Clearly this is bad; see the SBF section below. Yet somehow I don’t have an emotional response to this; I feel confident that we will keep catching and punishing fraud. Maybe the Trump presidency is a counterexample and I should be more upset. ↩︎
