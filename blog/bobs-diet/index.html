Bob's Diet: Much More Than You Wanted To Know

Published December 8, 2025Last edited December 8, 2025

Update 1/31/26: This ACX post does most of the data deep dive I have been postponing. They claim fish aren’t conscious. I can’t say whether or not I ‘believe’ this statement because I can’t even define consciousness, but I have updated down on the moral weight of fish. Today I am pescatarian and trying to kick my egg habit.

Epistemic Status: About once every two years God bestows upon me dietary wisdom which I am compelled to share with the world. Unfortunately, God’s new revelations tend to contradict his previous ones, just like in the real Bible. You are reading the gospel of Bob, circa 2025, predicted half life 2 years.

I: Bob’s Diet, A History #

This section is less interesting than the rest.

TLDR: #

2017: I read Natural Born Heroes by Christopher McDougall and become convinced that some combination of Paleo/Keto/Atkins diet is healthiest. I maintain that this is true for me: I lost 40 pounds in ~2 months after cutting carbs and never felt or looked better.

2020: Paul and Lucia, both of whom I met walking the Camino de Santiago, convince me that I am ethically obliged to become vegan. I become vegetarian in October 2020, pause for four months Jan-April 2021 while I live in Patagonia on a very restricted diet (eating meat < 10 times in those four months), then become fully vegan in August 2021.

2025: I get six separate fevers Jan - June 2025. I always felt that veganism was a health sacrifice, but these fevers are the straw (or the barbell) that breaks this camel’s back. I start eating eggs and dairy in June 2025.

Becoming Vegan #

Ryan and Oliver, two friends from college, convinced the rational part of my brain that factory farming is evil around 2019. I tried for quite a while to reason my way out of this. Here are some horrible arguments against began vegetarian that I knew deep down were pretty bad but repeated mentally in reflexive attempts to reduce cognitive dissonance:

  • Vegetarians annoyingly impose their values on others when they ask to eat somewhere with vegetarian options, and I’m not annoying. (Don’t meat-eaters impose their values too, but get away with it because they’re the majority?)
  • Being vegetarian is not on the Pareto frontier of lifestyle changes that help the environment while preserving quality of life. Thus vegetarians are virtual signalers, QED. (But veganism certainly is on the Pareto frontier for minimizing animal cruelty, and that’s what I was trying to do!)
  • Being vegetarian is blue-tribe, and in 2019 I was in the red tribe by Stanford standards, though that would change quickly.
  • Can’t you just eat meat, but not the factory farmed kind? (This question is very complicated - I still haven’t answered it).

During this time period I made a half-hearted attempt to only eat ‘happy meats,’ ie not factory farmed food. This didn’t work for several reasons:

  • Labels like ‘cage-free’ are in my estimation fairly meaningless. Even cage free chickens spend their diseased existence in a miserably crowded coop, constantly trodding through a swamp of feces and never seeing the light of day. Maybe there’s a meaningful food label out there that truly guarantees that the animal I’m eating lived a happy life, but I have not found it.
  • I wasn’t prepared to quiz friends and restaurants who cooked for me on the origin of the meat I was eating. Not eating meat ever is a thing; it’s called being vegetarian. Not eating meat that was produced in a factory farm isn’t a thing; (some) people call you a snob or an asshole.[1]

No rational discourse was going to change my meat-eating habits at this point in time. I could not become part of the soy-eating blue tribe. Luckily, in 2021 while walking the Camino de Santiago, I met several vegans I really liked. Most influential was Paul.

Paul was vegan for environmental reasons, which I think is dumb because it is not on the Pareto frontier of lifestyle changes that help the environment while preserving quality of life. But Paul was not dumb, he had just already made all the lifestyle changes on said Pareto frontier: he was vegan, never took planes, always used carpools or public transit, never bought new clothing, never consumed single-use plastic, etc. Paul was monk-like in his dedication, rational in his decisions, and presented normally to strangers; thus he was a vegan I was inspired to imitate. (I cared more about how I presented back then).

A Conflicted Vegan #

I always thought vegetarianism/veganism was a health sacrifice. I continue to believe that the two best dietary heuristics are 1) eat what a caveman would eat and 2) eat what makes you feel good. Veganism was a step backward on both fronts.[2]

I always craved meat. It never grossed me out. I was vegan because System II told me it was the right thing to do, not because System I felt guilty consuming flesh. My mouth has never stopped watering at the smell of a hamburger on a grill.

Initially I hated the aesthetics of veganism. I was living in a frat house. I got called a soy boy much more frequently than I was praised for my ethics. Eventually I stopped hanging out with the people who called me a soy boy, not literally because they called me that, but because they are exactly the type of person you think they are based on this one data point you have about their personality. Gradually veganism meshed well with the rest of my persona.

II: A First Principles Diet #

In June 2025 I decided that veganism was too damaging to my health. Vegetarianism was the default step backward. After realizing I had used this cognitive shortcut, I decided to use first principles to propose a diet; hence this post.

Why is Meat-Eating Bad? #

To some, it is because you are effectively killing animals. When you slaughter a cow or send a bullet through a moose’s skull, it suffers in the moments leading to its death. ChatGPT tells me this is on the order of seconds it both cases, which seems small relative to the total suffering experienced by any being. I think the real cost here, in the case of the moose at least, is that it can’t go on living its happy life, and that its moose family loses a loved one.

Both of these costs seem minuscule relative to the cost of factory farming, that is, keeping animals alive in horrific conditions. Pigs in factory farms live in pens so small they don’t have the room to turn around. The ammonia-laden air burns lungs and eyes. Baby pigs have their tails cut off, otherwise a pig in another pen will chew it off in distress. I won’t bore you with similar descriptions of other animals.

The Least Bad Metric #

Is there any good way to quantify the suffering of animals? I was hopeful that the rationalist blogging universe would help me find the answer, but it had few concrete suggestions. The best attempt at a first principles approach was something like “neuron count ≈ moral weight.” Everyone hates this solution (example), but no one proposes anything better.

I propose something better: suffering caused ≈ neuron count * time spent on a factory farm. This is of course oversimplified. I sit here awaiting your better metric, dear reader. In the meantime I will use my flawed-but-plausible one.

IV: Objections #

Free Range? #

The two obvious strategies arising from this chart are 1) don’t eat factory farmed meat, or 2) don’t eat factory farmed meat unless it’s a bivalve, a crustacean, or dairy.

I have deep apprehensions about eating wild animals and wiping away my guilt:

  • Buying more expensive food to wash away moral sin does not feel good. I worry it will lead to class resentment without much changing of dietary norms.
  • I suspect that average consumer eats a fairly fixed amount of, say, beef, and will pay $x more for free-range than factory farmed. I also suspect that the supply of free-range beef is fairly inelastic: beef production is very land-intensive. If you agree with these assumptions, then my buying 1lb of free-range beef brings into existence ~1lb of factory-farmed beef.
  • I worry that by buying free-range beef I’d be indirectly burning down the Amazon so that cattle can graze in Brazil. About free-range pork and fowl, I am uneducated.
  • The supply of basically all wild meat (moose, salmon, crab, etc.) is constrained by the size of wild populations, not humans’ ability to hunt. If I buy a wild salmon, the person who would’ve bought it will buy a farmed one.
  • It’s not easy to determine whether or not the animal on your plate lived a humane life. I have no trust in certifying labels, but could be convinced that certain specific farms are good.

I consider this objection unresolved. I’m still curious about:

  1. How fungible people’s meat consumption is across species.
  2. If it’s possible for me to buy animal products that I’m extremely confident are humanely raised.
  3. How land intensive various forms of human animal agriculture are, and how bad this is for the environment.

Don’t You Care About The Environment? #

Yes, I do. Another post to follow. But I suspect that emissions specifically are a rounding error when compared to the cost in animal suffering. As for land use, I’m not so sure.[4]

Farmed Shrimp Eat Fishmeal #

Correct. And fishmeal normally comes from anchovies, which are much smarter than shrimp. ChatGPT wants me to believe that 1 pound of shrimp production requires ~.2 pounds of fishmeal. These anchovies are caught wild. I don’t think this changes my conclusions much.

Horseflies Don’t Look So Good #

And as a former eater of horseflies and former believer in insect meats, I am distressed by this. Raw size is a bad heuristic.

Some Animals Don’t Have Brains At All #

Correct. Bivalves don’t have brain or a central nervous system, and they’re the first animals on my list! People with wrinklier brains than mine propose that total cortical neurons are a better heuristic for moral weight than total neurons. I am not convinced in either direction.

V. Conclusion #

I have decided to include animal protein in my diet, but I want to minimize the animal suffering that this choice causes. Being vegetarian is not the optimal solution to this problem. To determine what is, I will have to answer the open questions in the free range section. For now, I’ll be eating natant decapods, bivalves, dairy, and (guiltily) some eggs.
