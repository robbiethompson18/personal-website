How to Make People Feel Smart

(And thus ask more questions and learn quickly)

Published January 25, 2025Last edited January 25, 2025

I recently quit my job at a quant trading firm. Early on, we were told explicitly: “the number one mistake new traders make is not asking enough questions.” When I heard this, I thought: “what an easy piece of advice to follow. I’m a curious person, and I’m sociable enough that people won’t mind talking to me, so I bet I’ll ask a ton of thoughtful questions, and learn fast as a result.”

I was wrong. I fell very far behind in learning new things at work because I didn’t ask enough questions. The funny thing was, I knew I was making this mistake. And I couldn’t stop myself. Why is asking questions hard?

One reason you might not ask questions is because you don’t have any. You’re not curious about the thing you’re learning about, your mind is hearing things but not processing, and so you don’t register that there are things you don’t understand fully. This was to some extent true for me at my old job. I find finance more interesting than the average person, but not so interesting that I can spend 55 hours a week thinking about how bond ETFs work and be energized and stimulated the whole time. But this issue was insignificant, in relative terms. I still had tons of unanswered questions I wasn’t asking. Why?

The primary reason I didn’t ask questions at work is because I was embarrassed, ashamed, and self-conscious. For example:

  • Embarrassed when my mentor explained an accounting convention to me and two other new hires, they understood, and I did not.
  • Ashamed when I thought I understood how auctions worked, agreed to be responsible for one, and ended up realizing I didn’t know what was happening and was in the process of making a costly mistake.
  • Self-conscious that I wasn’t performing as well as my peers, and that every time I asked a question other people knew the answer to, I was reminding the room of this fact.

In each of these three scenarios, the correct thing to do next is ask for help:

  • Can you explain that convention one more time?
  • I didn’t realize this was a NASDAQ auction, can someone take over for me?
  • I know we talked about this last week, but can someone remind me what to think about when watching munis?

I knew that I was supposed to ask for help, but my embarrassment, shame, and self-consciousness stopped me dead in my tracks. A piece of advice I thought would be so easy to follow — “ask more questions” — turned out to be tremendously difficult emotionally. I felt like I was a smoker being told to just quit using cigarettes, a chubby kid being told to drop the snickers and have kale for lunch.[1]

Even worse, my employer didn’t do obviously hurtful or aggressive things that made me feel like my fragile emotional state was justified. I was almost never yelled at, never verbally abused or told I was stupid, almost never ranked or explicitly compared to my peers. Still, there are countless subtler signals, verbal and non-verbal, that you express whenever answering a question or explaining a concept. Despite the good intentions of my coworkers, and despite them not doing any of the egregious things listed above, I consistently felt stupid when asking questions and listening to explanations.

Below is the advice I would give to my former coworkers so that future new hires don’t feel the same way. These tips have worked well in my teaching and tutoring experience.[2] Most of the steps below are not actions to take to avoid making someone feel stupid. They are ways you can build people up, actively make them feel smart and valued, and validate that it was good of them to ask a question. Most of my advice is very simple, and yet in my experience ignored more often than not.

You might criticize me by saying that the following this advice will fish out some unnecessary questions. To which I would reply: in your organization, how often do people ask questions that you think are a waste of time? How often do they hold in questions that would have been helpful to ask? In most situations, the latter dwarfs the former, so you should absolutely try to fish out the marginal question that does not get asked.

When someone is asking you questions #

  • Compliment questions, especially good ones, and thank people for asking them.
  • When you don’t know the answer to all or part of a question, draw attention to this fact, so that the person asking thinks the question they’ve asked isn’t a trivial one.
  • Proactively point people to another person who can answer a question in more detail than you, making it easier for them to ask the next person the next question.
  • If someone asks a question that you think other trainees should have asked too, tell them to go share what they learned with the other people you think should hear. This strongly validates the question asked.
  • If you’re busy, don’t just respond to the question in a rushed way. First, communicate that you’re busy, ideally with an apology and some levity. Then respond how you see fit (maybe not at all). That way the person knows that it’s your busy-ness, not the them or their question, that is the issue. When you’re less busy, follow up and make sure that the question got answered; again, you’re demonstrating that the question asked of you was important.
  • If someone asks you a question that you think they could have or should have answered on their own, do not just respond with “I think you can figure that out.” The person asking the question will quickly feel dumb for not figuring out the answer on their own, and embarrassed because they exposed their self-perceived ineptitude by asking the question. If finding the answer really is trivial, you can instead say something like “Have you tried looking at xyz sql table? I think you should be able to get the answer there, let me know if you can’t and I’ll try.” Saying “I’ll try” is usually a lot better than “I’ll show you.” Trying implies that the task is hard and you might fail (even if it isn’t and you won’t), which prevents the asker from feeling like they asked a stupid question.[3]
  • If someone asks you a question that you want them to think about more, don’t just respond with “what do you think?” The person who asked the question could interpret this response as dismissive, thinking you really meant something like “You can answer that on your own, knucklehead. Think before you ask me a question.” Even if you think your tone conveys something else, it’s better to be explicit. Much better responses:
    • I’m not sure about the answer to this question and am curious what thoughts you have.
    • I think this question is an interesting exercise. What have you thought about so far? I think you should consider x. Let me know when you’ve thought more and we can discuss.
  • Show interest in a question and a person by trying to discover where it came from. After thanking or complimenting the question, and maybe also answering it, ask “is this related to xyz project you’re working on?” Or “I’m curious why you’re looking into that.”
  • Once you’ve answered the question, confirm that the person asking understands, patiently. In these final moments, your goal is to make the person you’re talking to you feel like they should have follow up questions (even if you’ve answered this question 100 times before and you think you explained everything perfectly). People always have something they could ask, but they respond to your cues on whether or not they should. Asking “do you have any questions?” is OK, but you can do a lot better. Try:
    • What questions do you have?
    • I glossed over some points there, is there anything I should come back to?
    • This concept is really challenging; I’m sure some of you have questions.

When You Are Giving a Presentation #

  • When people ask questions, thank them, and compliment the question asked.
  • Say at the beginning of the talk that you would like people to ask questions. Tell people that their ideas are welcome and encouraged even if they aren’t fully formed.
  • Be very cautious when describing things as easy, simple, trivial, or straightforward. Often you should just avoid these words entirely. You can call hard things hard without then saying that other things are easy. If you must, referring to a concept as ‘easier’ is better than calling it ‘easy.’ The former leaves room for the interpretation of “all these questions/concepts are hard, I know that, so I’m trying to start with something more manageable.”
  • When you get to a tricky section, acknowledge that the thing you’re about to talk about is complicated/tricky/difficult. Even better, tell the room that you personally struggled with it, or took a long time to figure it out yourself.
  • Stop frequently to ask for questions. After you ask, pause for five seconds and maintain eye contact with the audience. If someone is squirming a little bit like they have a question or are confused, you can say so: “you look like you have a question.” Or “it looks like people are confused.” You can also give people a full minute to digest some counterintuitive point or chunk of math, and then ask for questions. That way students can better formulate questions in their head and feel smarter while asking them.
  • If you suspect people are confused but are too nervous to ask a question or don’t know what to ask, ask the room if they understand. I like to do this by asking people to raise their hand if they feel like they understood.[4]
  • If you feel you’ve botched an explanation, acknowledge that you’ve botched it and try again, more slowly this time.
  • When someone asks a question that might cause you to skip ahead far beyond where most students’ understanding is, or is blatantly designed to make you think they’re clever, or is off topic, make very clear to the class that you do not expect them to have the same depth of understanding that the person who asked the question is displaying. Responding to a challenging, out-of-scope question in the same way that you respond to a normal question makes everyone else in the room think “wow, that other person is way ahead of me. I have a question too, but I don’t want to slow down the class, nor do I want to expose how far behind I am.” You should respond with “that’s an excellent question,” followed by…
    • In short, the answer has to do with x, but the reasoning is quite complicated. I’ll circle back to that if we have time at the end.
    • That’s beyond where I’m hoping to get to today, but come talk to me afterwards and I’m happy to chat more.

In Conclusion #

I think all of the advice I’ve written down is intuitive, maybe even common sense. Nonetheless, it takes practice and effort to do all of these things well. Hopefully reading this reminds you of ways you could be better at building up the self-confidence of those around you, and convinces you to spend a little more of your attention and energy on improving at it.

  2. To lend myself some credibility: I was a teaching assistant for five different courses in college and worked part time as a private tutor for five years. ↩︎

  3. If someone is actually asking you questions all the time because they’re too lazy to write a couple lines of code, that’s a separate story. But people normally ask these types of questions by accident and don’t realize how simple the solution is. If you show someone how easy it is to get the answer, they will realize (without you telling them!) and not ask questions like this in the future. Try to be very slow to come to the conclusion that someone is asking you questions because they’re lazy. ↩︎

Also, people might be sheepish and just keep doing nothing (ie keeping their hands down) as long as everyone else is doing nothing. I would rather falsely believe that people don’t understand than falsely believe that they do. ↩︎
