Learnings From Serving a Meditation Course

Published February 28, 2025Last edited February 28, 2025

Some context: I just returned from serving (volunteering) on a ten day silent meditation retreat as taught my S.N. Goenka (see dhamma.org). Students, or people who are sitting, perform seated meditatation for 12 hours every day. They take a vow of noble silence, and for ten days do not talk or use any form of non-verbal communication. This includes cell phone use and small acts of recognition like nods or eye contact. Journaling and reading are not permitted. Students can ask questions of the teacher at set times each day.

Servers meditate for four hours a day and spend the rest of their time taking care of the students’ needs, which primarily means cooking and washing dishes. You’re only allowed to serve after you have sat a course, which I did two years ago. As a server you can socialize with other servers while you’re cooking, cleaning, or eating, just not while you’re amongst the students. You’re still not allowed to have your phone or outside reading material or a journal.

Here are some of my learnings from serving and sitting on these courses.

Happiness is Contagious #

Servers watch one discourse as instruction, and the entire video could be paraphrased as: “Whatever you’re doing, do it with peace and good intention. If you find yourself washing the dishes with anger or resentment, go take a nap and let someone else wash them.”

I and the society around me values productivity. I have internalized the message that sometimes you have to keep going, even when you’re having a rough day. So much so that for the longest time I thought the expression “grin and bare it” was actually “grit and bare it,” as in, grit your teeth and get through it.

My role as a server was a dishwasher - for about six of my ten hours of service each day, I was scrubbing oats out of pots and sending plates through a commercial dishwasher (Champ, we affectionately called him). I thought it was inevitable that my patience would end, but day after day I surprised myself by waking up with a smile on my face. I chalked this up to the four hours of meditation I was doing, or to the fact that I was washing dishes for a good cause.

Only on day five did I realize why I was so cheerful: everyone else was so cheerful. And if they weren’t they were given the nap they needed. Adam got the flu and had to go home. Jerome had swollen feet and began to work only half the day. Kevin hurt his back, so we took over his heavy lifting. No one did their work begrudgingly, which was contagious.

I noticed this on day five because it briefly wasn’t true, and the contrast was jarring. Trevor, our kitchen manager, had by far the most responsibility of all of us, and was burnt out. On day five he said something passive aggressive. It was a small mishap, and in a normal work environment it would have been completely ignored. But in this pristine kitchen of calm and positivity, his mildly upset words stuck out strongly to each of us, like an olive dropped into rice pudding. After he did this, Trevor took a nap, and everyone’s cortisol levels returned to their extremely low levels.

Reflecting, it’s no wonder I felt stressed and unhappy at my old job. In my heart of hearts I didn’t care too much when my team made mistakes. But the stress of my coworkers was contagious, and despite my best efforts I couldn’t stop it from seeping into my subconscious.

Work and Trust #

Stealing this idea from Nassim Nicholas Taleb.

It’s no coincidence that the word trust has two meanings:

  1. Truthfulness: I trust her not to lie to me.
  2. Dependability: I trust her to perform the surgery, land the plane, deliver the letter, make the free throw.

These two phenomena sharing a word betrays that they’re closely linked mentally. Even though in theory someone could be truthful but not dependable, or vice versa, my brain shortcuts and merges these two traits into one.

Trust is foundational to friendship. And the first kind of trust, truthfulness, takes a long time to demonstrate to someone else. It’s hard to manufacture important situations in which you might be tempted to lie but don’t. But you can manufacture the situations which allow you to demonstrate dependability. Just do any kind of group work with the other person, and then do everything you say you will, ahead of schedule and under budget.

While serving, I grew to quickly trust that Kedar would clean pots thoroughly, Darin would cook fluffy rice, and Polly would get lunch cooked on time. My brain just shortcutted to I trust Kedar, Darin, and Polly, in all senses of the word trust.

I realize now that almost all of my lasting friendships began with working on something together. This explains the remarkable effectiveness of fraternity hazing: even if we have little else in common, I trust most of the guys in my pledge class to help me finish a keg or camp out in the sand pit. It also explains why my old job ruined my self-esteem. I was untrustworthy, not because I ever lied, but because I wasn’t dependable. I was incapable of completing well all the work that was assigned to me.

Old and Vulnerable #

It’s no coincidence that senior means both older and above you in the hierarchy. I’m 25, and when I interact with people above the age of 50 they’re almost always in a position of power over me. Not so while serving this course. Kedar, 40-something, washed dishes in the pit right next to me. I peeled potatoes right alongside Jerome, Lawrence, and Darin, all 70-something. Everyone listened to Trevor, 24, kitchen manager.

This egalitarian attitude persisted during lunchtime conversation. Often when a sixty year old tells me something about kids these days it feels like a lecture, but somehow with this group it never did. I don’t know if that’s because it takes a particularly humble sixty year old to agree to wash dishes for ten days, or because working together made us all trust each other. But it was beautiful. The men around me talked openly about their fears, anxieties, apprehensions, regrets. In the past this vulnerability was something I found to be rare in young men and non-existent in older men. I learned so much from the words and they examples of Lawrence, Jerome, and Darin.
