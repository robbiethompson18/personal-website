getting-humbled

Published September 20, 2026Last edited September 20, 2026

Everyone except John Von Neumann and Terence Tao will at some point in their life come into contact with an intellect far superior than their own.⁠[1] Let me tell you about when this happened to me.

I am really good at math. Not so good that I ever went to the IMO, or even the AIME. But good enough that I won basically every math contest any school I attended ever held up until college.⁠[2] Good enough that I got a job as a quant at Jane Street.

The trouble is that I never once sought out other people as good at math as me.⁠[3] I didn’t want to: if I never tested myself, then for all I knew I might actually be the best. I never thought in these terms explicitly, but I still think I was motivated by this subconsciously. This was obviously delusional: clearly some teenagers were going to the AIME and the IMO, even if I had never met them. If you asked me about this at the time I might’ve said something like “those people are nerds who spend all their time studying math; I’d rather be playing football and drinking with my friends.”⁠[4] In college I took only one or two hard proof-based math classes filled with mega-geniuses that properly challenged me, and steered far clear of the accelerated courses I would have failed.⁠[5] I still had to do hard math in courses like Algorithms, but I did it amongst engineers and, to flatter myself, did very well without trying very hard.

Writing all of this out, I realize that it took quite a large amount of self-delusion and manipulation of my environment to make it through college without ever truly humbling myself mathematically. For better or worse, it did happen soon after graduating.

My first job out of college I was a quant at Jane Street. Only half of interns get a job; I suspect I made the cut with not too much room to spare. Which meant that as an FTE, I was near the bottom of intellects amongst all traders.

It’s hard to put into words how demoralizing this was. I didn’t expect to be in trading my whole life; at that point in time I was a somewhat woo liberal and didn’t value money much. I planned to get rich for two years while I sorted out what was more important. But I did expect to be good at it: solving math puzzles quickly is my jam, and that’s what the job is.

For my whole life, I had been taking a handsaw into the forest and tirelessly cutting down tall mathematical trees, dumbfounding those around me who could never dream of such a feat. Then I got to Jane Street, pulled out my saw, and watched in awe as Kevin Ma chainsawed down a copse in an hour. I thought this was something, until Max Schindler showed all of new hires what a lightsaber can do to a forest.

I am surprised how different my actions and worldview were as a result of being very dumb relative to my peers and environment. Chesterton’s Fence might be summarized as “don’t remove institutions until you can explain their existence.” My feeble intellect could explain approximately nothing around me, so the correct choice was basically always to trust whatever system was in place and hope to God that whoever built it was correct. This feels spiritually akin to being a ‘conservative’ in a political sense: ‘I have no idea how anything works, it’s a miracle it all does, so don’t touch the institutions.’

I also found myself craving guidance and management. It’s remarkable to me how naturally this felt. No part of me felt compelled to manage anyone else. This is contra to most other work environments I’ve been in, where I’ve quickly found myself mentoring others.

I eventually quit this job to move back to San Francisco. My ego was in the absolute gutter, so the next job I took I think I was overqualified for, which at least helped me quickly regain my confidence.

These experiences give me a profound appreciation for the fact that I will never be truly the best at any sufficiently broad and important thing. The thing I was always best at is the type of quick mathematical thinking that makes one a good trader. Lots of people have told me cope like “but your coworkers all had bad social skills and you don’t.” A few of them did have bad social skills, though frankly I think people were pretty average socially and choose to relate to one another in a manner much different from what normies are used to. And my social skills are good but certainly not exceptional.

It also makes me more scared of AI. I’ve seen what Max Schindler can do with a lightsaber; none of my abilities approach the limit of what is physically possible. AI will do this to everyone all the time, protein folding…

In another life I might’ve been a trader on the floor somewhere, but because of Max I’m electronically automated…

I can spot an intellectual weapon across the room. People will hate this take, but to me they all look quite similar in fact. Most are very very good quantitatively. There are two skills: taking arguments seriously, and reasoning. You need both to be interesting. But I can spot the good reasoners from a mile away. The Elizers. Scott Alexander is mmostly a ‘take ideas seriously’ guy. He’s the best popularizer ever but idk of any new takes he’s convinced me of that aren’t just slightly repackaged EA dogma.

being around intellectual weapons sucks. You are forced into that bad mindset. I don’t know why thing works, but it does. Maybe there’s some parallel to religion too…

But you absolutely must do it. These people will be abrasive, they will be somewhat dismissive of you, they will propose things that make your hair stand up straight. And they will be right, and you should change your mind. Idk obviously you should try very hard to understand arguments yourself. But you gotta know who is persuasive and who is a weapon.

This didn’t really go anywhere very interesting… somewhat disappointed. I guess this essay mostly has emotional valence. And I should expand on the part where dumb person mindset and smart person mindset differ:

  • agency
  • respect for institutions
  • level of leadership/ownership (cheems mindset)
  • sense for how efficient the world is
