Mr. Mackey on Drugs

Published January 27, 2025Last edited January 30, 2025

I: Childhood Impressions #

Among the many American normative values that South Park instilled in me at an impressionable young age was this one: “Drugs are bad, m’kay” (from Mr. Mackey). I was taught in school and at home that regular drug use is unhealthy, destructive of personal relationships, a sign of bad character and low self-control. Except, of course, that one drug we use every day: coffee.

My childhood impressions about recreational drugs, almost entirely dictated by what those around me and society at large thought, was the following:

  • Drinking alcohol on the weekends is normal, but any more than that and you’re an alcoholic and lack self-control. Unless, of course, you’re in college, in which case anything goes.
  • Cigarettes are addictive and will give you cancer. People who smoke them lack discipline. But they won’t on their own stop you from being a high-functioning adult.
  • Stoners smoke pot. It makes you lazy. It will, on its own, stop you from being a high-functioning adult.
  • Some edgy but successful people do cocaine, but otherwise any drug not yet mentioned is extraordinarily dangerous and should be avoided at all costs. Only extremely reckless people would do ‘hard drugs.’

In case you couldn’t tell, my parents are conservative.

What I find most puzzling about this picture is that coffee just doesn’t count as a drug at all. It’s in its own special class. Something I’ll get to consume once I’ve finished growing, that will help me focus on my studies and stay up late. To my childhood brain coffee had more in common with a chamomile tea or the roller coasters only adults could ride than it did with a cigarette or a beer.

II: Settings #

Many settings can only be experienced pleasantly or properly with the assistance of a drug. A rave is designed to provide an extreme sensory experience, one that helps distance the mind and body from the ego. It’s a lot easier to get to this place on some type of psychedelic. Taking a brief respite from the party to go outside, get some fresh air, and gossip is always a pleasant experience, but it’s heightened by a cigarette. Blood rushes to the head, awakening the mind and stimulating conversation.[1] Taken in sober, a frat party is typically a miserable experience: the overly loud music swallows any attempt at conversation; the overcrowded room creates constant collisions between sweaty bodies; the air is humid and odorous; the beer warm, cheap, and insipid. But if you slug down a couple of those insipid beers, the music becomes an irresistible opportunity to dance, those sweaty collisions become pregnant with an exciting sexual tension, the humid air moves easily in and out of your lungs as you sing, and, like one of Pavlov’s dogs, you learn to love the taste of the cheap beer that creates so much excitement in your life.[2]

Now imagine going to work (at a corporate job) without coffee. Could you do it? Could you clear your email inbox, finish an excel spreadsheet, focus during a dull video conference, without the help of caffeine?

III: So is caffeine a drug? #

By any scientific definition, yes. Caffeine is psychoactive. But I guess maybe you don’t care about that fact — maybe you contend that, qualitatively, caffeine doesn’t behave much like a drug, or that humans don’t use it much like a drug. I disagree.

People use caffeine to be awake when they want to be. Now imagine the converse — you need to drink a psychoactive substance (or take a pill) to fall asleep every night. Society would certainly tell you you’re taking a drug in that situation, even if a doctor prescribed it to you.

People use caffeine to stay focused. Framed this way, caffeine is capitalism’s miracle drug. Give it to your employees, and they’ll be able to lock in on any task, no matter how repetitive. Nonetheless, you are altering your state of consciousness. If weed is a drug because it helps you relax and stimulates your appetite, then isn’t caffeine a drug for doing the opposite?

Hitler gave his soldiers meth.[3] The men manning his Panzers stayed awake and focused for days on end, their emotions dulled by drugs and sleep deprivation as they massacred their enemies. We wouldn’t condone this nowadays. But soldiers do receive MREs (Meal, Ready-to-eat) that contain all the calories and nutrients they need, in the densest, smallest, most easily prepared and digested form. It’s bare bones food. And instant coffee? I couldn’t spend a night in a foxhole without it.[4]

You could claim that you don’t drink coffee for any of the reasons I mentioned above. You just like the taste. I won’t argue with you. I don’t consume caffeine, and I’m sure this piece reads like one long judgmental tirade against people who do. But I promise, I don’t judge you. I think drugs are awesome! I use a bunch of different ones, with varying regularity. My point is not that caffeine is bad, it’s that drugs (to be more specific, psychoactive substances) are useful and beneficial, as long as they are used responsibly. If you want to paint psychoactive or addictive substances as harmful with one broad stroke, I insist that you hand over your Nespresso pods.

IV: The future #

American recreational drug use suffers from a boring monoculture. Thanks to our decades-long cultural and literal war on drugs, for a long time the only recreational ones accepted by mainstream society were alcohol, nicotine, and caffeine, with the last enjoying the broadest societal acceptance. To some extent, this is good — I like that the act of abusing Vicodin isn’t socially acceptable, even if I wish we spent more time helping people experiencing addiction instead of judging, incarcerating, and stigmatizing them. But our cultural immune system has been much too strong, causing us to unfairly judge people who use drugs outside the big three, and to miss out on enjoying these drugs ourselves.

If I had written this essay twenty years ago, today you would be patting me on the back for how smart I was. Much of society has accepted the notion that marijuana is a much safer painkiller than opiates (and a much safer recreational drug than alcohol); that psychedelics have tremendous therapeutic potential and research into this has been blocked for far too long. But this is just the tip of the iceberg. There are so many lesser-known drugs floating around in the world that will continue to enter mainstream culture. Kava is becoming in vogue in some of my social circles — for me it provides all the social lubrication that alcohol does without the the cognitive impairment or hangover. Lots of New York City bodegas now sell kratom, marketed variably as either a focusing stimulant or a pain-relieving depressant. (I do not recommend: I had a horribly disagreeable reaction the one time I tried it). Going to a sensory deprivation spa near me, I discovered countless mildly psychoactive plants I had never tried before, on all offer in their teas; things like ashwagandha, valerian root, skullcap leaf, mugwort, and catuaba bark.

From an individual perspective, trying an obscure drug for the first time rarely feels worth it. It’s hard to get the dosing right, there’s always a risk of an adverse reaction, and it’s a lot of effort to find stuff that’s pure and unadulterated. This essay is not your call to action to grow opium in your backyard or try DMT in your dorm room. But notice that those who are going around trying new drugs are performing a socially beneficial task: acting as human guinea pigs so that the rest of us can learn what the rare and long term effects of these drugs are. Of course in many ways it would be better if scientists in labs were doing these studies, but:

  1. There’s very little financial incentive to study the effects of plants that you can’t patent.
  2. Humans in the wild will do reckless and stupid things which ethical review boards would never or rarely allow in an actual study, like mixing drugs or consuming hundreds of grams of edibles at once. This is how we know how hard it is to overdose on weed.
  3. It’s hard for scientists to study substances that aren’t currently legal, which often has more to do with politics than science.
  4. There are just so many drugs out there; scientific studies are a limited resource.
  5. Studies can be doctored, buried, or p-hacked, if an unscrupulous company is behind them. I hope the Purdue Pharma saga of the past couple years convinces you that, sadly, this is still true in 2025.
  6. Clinical studies struggle to find rare, subtle, and long-term side effects. Sometimes the only way we learn is by seeing what happens in the wild, letting time draw out rare and long-term effects.

So give the people trying out new drugs a break. They are putting themselves at risk while the rest of us learn.

And take advantage of data sources beyond scientific studies. No medical paper will tell you what it feels like when you’ve smoked just the right amount, or will warn you about having sex on MDMA. And for any psychoactive plant, there will be indigenous communities who have used it for millennia and have traditions that regulate the dosing, regularity, and the environment in which a drug should be used.
