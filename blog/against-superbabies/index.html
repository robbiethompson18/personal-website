Against Superbabies

Published August 10, 2026Last edited August 10, 2026

By “superbabies” I mean the LessWrong-cluster project: rank IVF embryos by polygenic score and implant the best one, and eventually edit embryos directly, so that kids end up smarter, taller, and healthier than their parents’ natural draw. I’m not going to relitigate whether IQ is real or heritable here; I’ve already written that post, and if you need convincing go read Scott Alexander instead. The premise of this post is narrower and more personal: assume the whole IQ-realist package is true. Assume genetics matters enormously for how a life goes. I still don’t think I should spend my career on superbabies, and the pro-superbabies case is getting made in more detail elsewhere, so I’ll make mine plainly.

The Steelman #

If it works, this compounds in a way almost nothing else does. A better school compounds until the kid graduates, then resets for the next kid. A better embryo compounds for that kid’s entire life, and then half of it lands in their kids too. If you believe (I do) that most of what’s good about the world was produced by a small tail of unusually capable people, then shifting the whole distribution of capability right, even a little, is one of the few interventions with a genuinely exponential payoff instead of a linear one. This is the same instinct behind my tracking posts: sorted, high-ability people compound faster than mixed-ability groups drag each other down. Superbabies is that argument taken to its logical endpoint, before birth instead of before ninth grade.

I take this case seriously. It’s why I read the LessWrong posts on this instead of ignoring them. It’s just not, for me, a case for building it.

The Numbers Are Small #

The actual, peer-reviewed effect size of embryo selection today is nowhere near what “superbaby” implies. Simulating realistic IVF batches (5-10 viable embryos, which is what you actually get, not the 100-embryo fantasy scenario), selecting the top-scoring embryo for cognitive ability gets you an expected gain of about 2.5 IQ points, with prediction intervals wide enough that you often aren’t even picking the smartest embryo in the batch. That’s the honest number from the people who ran the simulation, not the marketing number. Heliospect Genomics, a startup that got caught on tape by an undercover researcher, was privately telling clients they could expect 6-plus points for $50,000. Even if you trust the more aggressive number, six IQ points is the difference between the 50th and 60th percentile. It is not the difference between your kid and von Neumann.

The deeper problem is what the polygenic score is actually measuring. The best available score for educational attainment explains 12-16% of the variance in a population sample, which sounds decent until you notice that embryo selection isn’t a population comparison, it’s a sibling comparison: you’re ranking full siblings against each other, all of whom share the same two parents and roughly the same in-utero and childhood environment. Under that comparison, direct genetic effects are about half the size of the population-level association, because a big chunk of what a polygenic score picks up in the wild is genetic nurture and assortative mating, not the within-family causal signal embryo selection is actually trying to exploit. You are selecting on a noisy proxy for a noisy proxy, from a menu of five embryos, for a trait that is itself only partially genetic. The confident public framing (“smarter kids”) is downstream of a much messier, much smaller technical reality.

The honest LW answer to “the numbers are small” is: fix the numbers, don’t abandon the project. The actual bottleneck isn’t the polygenic score, it’s the embryo count. In vitro gametogenesis (turning stem cells into eggs and sperm, so you can generate hundreds of embryos from a skin sample instead of five from a hormone cycle) is the real unlock, because ranking 1-in-1000 gets you a lot further than ranking 1-in-5. I buy that this is the correct next technical step. I also notice that IVG has been done in mice using methods that don’t obviously scale to humans, and has never been done in a human being. The whole “small numbers” objection resolves only if you also solve a second, harder, unsolved biology problem first. That’s not a rebuttal to the steelman. It’s a reason the steelman is a much longer bet than it first appears.

It Is Not Neglected #

I keep coming back to a question I asked myself about ed-tech in Track the Kids Pt. 2: is there more to this than the obvious wrapper? If not, you need to actually own and run the thing, and your odds of success collapse. Superbabies fails this test even harder than personalized learning did, because the field is already stacked with capital and talent, much of it drawn from my own social circles.

Orchid has raised over $16M and become the default whole-genome embryo screen in Silicon Valley. Nucleus Genomics has raised $32M from Founders Fund and is already partnered with fertility clinic networks across India and the Middle East. Bootstrap Bio, started about eighteen months ago, is out raising a seed round specifically to do germline editing. Genomic Prediction has been selling embryo screening since 2019. Gene Smith wrote the canonical LessWrong methods post on germline engineering years before I thought about this topic for the first time. The rationalist-adjacent pronatalist movement (the Collinses and their circle) has been publicly embryo-selecting and evangelizing for it since before it was cool. This is arguably the single most talent-dense, most funded weird cause area within a five-degree radius of my actual social graph. Whatever gap I think I’m filling by showing up now, someone smarter than me with a two-year head start and a biology PhD is already filling it.

Contrast this with tracking in schools, which I looked into last week and found almost nobody seriously building. Superbabies has the opposite problem: too many well-capitalized people, chasing too small a technical edge, in too crowded a market, for me to expect my marginal contribution to matter.

The Regulatory Wall Is Worse Than Bad Vibes #

When I wrote about tracking, my conclusion was that the technology and the market were basically solved problems, and the only real blocker was vibes: tracking makes people uncomfortable, so no one does it, even though the upside is well understood and the downside is mostly reputational. I assumed superbabies would be the same kind of problem, just with worse vibes. It’s actually a categorically different kind of blocker, because part of it is a felony.

Since 2015, a rider attached annually to the FDA’s appropriations bill has barred the agency from even reviewing an application for a clinical trial “in which a human embryo is intentionally created or modified to include a heritable genetic modification.” That’s not a slow approval process you can route around with a good regulatory strategy. It’s Congress affirmatively forbidding the FDA from looking at the paperwork. Germline editing in humans is not legal-but-slow in the US, it is closed by statute, renewed every year without controversy, by both parties. He Jiankui, the scientist who actually did this in China in 2018, served three years in prison for it.

Embryo selection (no editing, just picking which existing embryo to implant) is legal in the US, and that’s the part companies like Orchid and Nucleus actually sell. But even that softer version draws a much more visceral backlash than tracking ever will, because tracking sorts kids who are already born and it’s reversible if it goes badly. Selecting an embryo is a one-time, irreversible decision made about a person who can’t consent and doesn’t exist yet to object. When an undercover researcher recorded Heliospect’s sales calls, the resulting story wasn’t “controversial ed-tech startup,” it was a scandal tying the company’s staff to eugenics history and scientific racism, covered by everyone from the Guardian to Fox News. Nobody got a scandal like that for suggesting we teach algebra to precocious eight-year-olds.

I don’t think tracking’s “bad vibes” problem and superbabies’ “bad vibes” problem are the same magnitude of thing. One is an unfashionable opinion. The other is a felony statute plus the single most efficient trigger for the word “eugenics” in the English language.

Twenty-Year Feedback Loops #

Here’s the argument I actually find most disqualifying for me personally, separate from whether the cause is good. I like building things I can test. Every project I’ve written up on this blog, the tracking wrapper, the browser agent startup, the OpenClaw hosting business, lived or died on a feedback loop measured in weeks or months. I built the personalized-learning wrapper on a Sunday and was using it by Monday. When browser agents didn’t work, I knew within a month, because customers either paid or they didn’t.

Superbabies has none of that. If I select an embryo for a marginally higher polygenic score today, I find out whether it worked around 2046, once that kid is old enough for the trait to have fully expressed and been measured, and only in a statistical sense across many other people’s kids, never in a way that tells me anything about the one decision I actually made. By the time any given cohort of embryo-selected kids grows up enough to validate or falsify the whole approach, the underlying technology (better GWAS panels, IVG, editing) will have moved on so far that the thing you’d be validating won’t be the thing anyone is still doing. You can’t loop on this. You can’t ship a rough version and see who complains. You place a bet in the dark and wait two decades to see if the die had the number you guessed painted on it, and even then the sample size is one.

The Wrong Kind of Edge #

Polygenic scoring is statistics and ML, which is nominally in my wheelhouse, so I don’t get to use “wrong field” as an excuse the way I could for, say, oncology. But the actual bottlenecks in this space right now aren’t modeling bottlenecks. They’re: (1) access to enormous genotype-and-outcome datasets, which live inside UK Biobank, 23andMe, and deCODE and are not available to a solo founder vibe-coding on a Sunday; (2) relationships with IVF clinics, which are medical institutions bound by liability, insurance, and licensing in a way no software distribution channel is; and (3) navigating the exact regulatory and PR minefield described above, which rewards patient, credentialed, media-trained operators, not fast movers. My actual edge, demonstrated across every project I’ve written up here, is shipping a rough version fast and finding out if anyone wants it. None of the three real bottlenecks in superbabies reward that at all.

The Math Doesn’t Clear #

I did a version of this Fermi estimate for tracking and got a number I found convincing enough to half-believe in the project. Let me do the same one here, honestly, and see where it lands.

IVF accounts for about 2.8% of US births, roughly 100,000 babies a year. Assume, generously, that embryo selection for cognitive ability reaches full saturation of that population, which it is nowhere near today, and delivers the peer-reviewed 2.5 IQ points on average. Compare this to tracking, which touches nearly all 3.6 million kids born in the US each year and, per the MAP data I cited last week, could plausibly close closer to a full grade level of gap for a meaningful slice of students. The reach is roughly forty times smaller and the per-kid effect is smaller too. On top of that, IVF families already skew wealthier and more educated than the general population, meaning the marginal kid being selected for is disproportionately already going to land in a good school, with engaged parents, near the top of the distribution. The population you’re intervening on is the population that needs the intervention least. Every knob I can turn on this Fermi estimate points the same direction: smaller reach, smaller effect, smaller marginal value than the tracking version of the same underlying belief (sorted, high-ability people compound faster). If I only have the attention for one of these two bets, tracking wins the estimate by a wide margin, and I already talked myself out of tracking too.

Don’t Poison Your Own Well #

This is the argument I actually care most about, and it’s specific to me. I think tracking kids by ability is one of the highest-leverage, most tractable, most under-invested ideas I’ve come across, and I already believe the only thing standing in its way is a vibes problem: people flinch at “sorting kids,” full stop. That flinch is exactly the thing that gets triggered, harder, faster, and with better headlines, by “startup screens embryos for IQ, is tied to scientific racism.” If I spend credibility on the superbabies case, I am not diversifying my portfolio of IQ-realist arguments, I am handing ammunition to the exact people who already conflate “smart kids should get harder math earlier” with “designer baby eugenics program,” a conflation that is false but rhetorically convenient for them and that I have zero interest in making easier to draw. The tracking case is winnable precisely because it’s boring: it’s about a school schedule, not a genome. I’d rather keep those two arguments as far apart in the public’s mind as I can, which means I stay away from the one that makes for a much better cable news segment.

What I’d Actually Do #

None of this is an argument that embryo selection is bad, or that nobody should build it, or that Orchid and Nucleus and Bootstrap Bio are wrong to try. Somebody solving the IVG bottleneck for real would change my Fermi estimate completely, and I’d be delighted to be a customer of whatever they build by the time I’m choosing embryos myself. That’s the actual shape of my position: I’ll take the product, I won’t build the company. The effect sizes are small, the field isn’t neglected, the legal regime for the interesting version is a federal crime, the feedback loop is twenty years long, my actual edge doesn’t apply, the expected value doesn’t clear a much better use of the same year, and the association risk actively damages the cause I’d rather spend that year on instead. Any one of these I might talk myself past. All seven together is not a coincidence, it’s a field telling me to go build something else.
