I know little about medicine. I am not trained in it, or even in biology or chemistry or some other
related field. I am unable to debate someone at a complex biological level, ie “this drug affects
that organelle which secrets the following hormone.” Yet I feel comfortable ignoring certain generic
medical advice (much to the annoyance and bewilderment of some of my loved ones). Why?

## The questions I ask myself before accepting a medical intervention:

1. Is there a good story for why cavemen were fine without this medical intervention? Is this
   intervention intended for everyone, or just me?
2. Is this intervention designed to cure me, or to resolve my symptoms?
3. How have financial incentives impacted research?
4. How are my doctor’s incentivizes different from my own?
5. Do the studies of this treatment have hard outcomes or surrogate outcomes? Are they RCTs or
   observational studies?

## I: Is there a good story for why cavemen were fine without this medical intervention?

Does it strike you as a little odd that American dentists recommend that almost everyone get their
wisdom teeth removed? I find this weird. Like, what happened to cavepeople? Did they all have
horrible jaw pain in their twenties and thirties?[^1] Did they knock each others’ teeth out with
rocks? What gives?

If you ask an anthropologist, they’ll tell you: the human jaws we’ve found from before the
agricultural revolution have perfect teeth. Well, not entirely perfect; I’m sure some of the
skeletons show teeth knocked out from blows. But the problems that modern orthodontia aims to fix —
malocclusion, crowded and crooked teeth, overbites — are almost nonexistent in prehistoric skulls.

So why is my dentist telling me I have shit genes and need braces, wisdom teeth removal, and an
Herbst appliance? It’s implausible that the human dental genome degraded so rapidly over the past
10,000 years. There’s a growing [movement](https://www.youtube.com/@orthotropics) and body of
literature which suggests that the real problem is that we aren’t chewing enough as kids. The
muscles and bones of our jaw aren’t stimulated, don’t grow properly, and give us a host of problems
included crowded jaws, impacted wisdom teeth, and breathing issues.

I’m certainly not a dentist, so if your dentist wants to give your kid braces or take out their
wisdom teeth, you should take their advice (or the advice of another dentist you trust), not mine.
It’s probably too late for your kid to start chewing more. The point is not to overrule your medical
professional, the point is to notice this weird phenomenon and ask the question. A little googling
unearths (what I believe to be) the real story, and a very valuable action item, if your kids are
young enough. Let them gnaw on some bones. Or get special dental gum, if that’s more your speed. I
wish my parents had done that for me, and saved me the pain and embarrassment of braces.

You should also aim to find medical professionals that are asking these types questions, not just
drilling teeth like the pictures in the textbook. If you ask your dentist why crowded jaws are so
common, and she just contends that most people have shit teeth because the human jaw and genome are
flawed, that is to me a red flag.

Modern Americans have a weird physiological inferiority complex: wild animals are by nature
vigorous, strong, healthy, robust. They can sleep outside in the cold, fight off infections without
drugs, skip meals to escape predators. Humans, thanks to millennia of civilized life, have become
wimps, dependent on central heating and antibiotics, incapable of extended exercise without Clif
bars and electrolyte goo. Again, let me remind you: your genome is virtually identical to the humans
who, ten thousand years ago, slept outside in the cold, fought off infections on their own, and
remained strong even when they could not find much food. So when someone claims that you and
everyone else need some medical intervention,[^2] because human beings are the wimps of the animal
kingdom, be skeptical. For example: is it really true that we need \$200 Nikes to for a jog?

There are a couple arguments you could make in favor of Nike, none of them very good:

1. _We run on pavement, cavemen ran on dirt._ Ignore for a moment that there are plenty of rocky
   surfaces in nature. If we just need a slightly softer surface, shouldn’t a thin rubber slipper be
   enough? Why do we need motion control, custom orthotics, a raised heel, rubber half an inch
   think?
2. _Cavemen didn’t run very often, and those that did got hurt frequently_. This one is disproven by
   modern anthropological findings. Countless modern hunter-gather societies continue to exist
   today, walking and running the bush every day, some of them routinely covering fifty or a hundred
   miles without stopping. They experience vanishingly few running injuries. Meanwhile, something
   like half of American runners got injured last year.
3. _I could be running around barefoot if I had grown up that way, but I’ve worn shoes all my life,
   so I’ll never be able to go without them_. This claim I have some sympathy for. If you’re 70,
   it’s probably safer to keep using the same old shoes. But if you’re 20, your body is capable of
   adapting. If you start walking and running barefoot, your arches, ankles, toes, all the
   musculature in your feet will strengthen and become more flexible. You whole foot will grow wider
   and taller. And your technique will transform. Instead taking long rolling strides and striking
   with your heel, you’ll bounce around on the ball of your foot, keep your legs beneath you with
   short strides, and allow your heels to snap back towards your butt to make your motion more
   efficient. I know from experience: I went cold turkey on Nike and started barefoot running at 19.
   I have had no running injuries or pain since, excluding the occasional foot blister.

(In Chapter 25 of _Born to Run_ Christopher McDougall argues far more eloquently than I ever could
that your body was made to run barefoot. If you’re curious I strongly recommend you buy a copy; this
is my favorite book ever.)

In general, I try to let my body suffer at least a watered down version of all the things a wild
human animal would. I swim in icy water, run for miles in the heat without much water, fast for a
day or two every so often, fight off less severe infections without the help of antibiotics or
NSAIDs, expose my skin to a reasonable amount of sun for someone of my pale complexion. You can find
mountains of studies and commentary on the internet telling you that any of these things is helpful
or harmful. It’s quite difficult to read enough to make a _scientifically_ informed decision. For me
the easiest way to make a _decent_ decision is to use a heuristic, namely: _if every prehistoric
body experienced this, it’s probably good for mine to experience it too_.[^3] This is a heuristic,
not a rule; I’m not going out of my way to walk through thorn bushes or let mosquitos bite me. But
for me this heuristic has served very well.

## II: Is this intervention designed to cure me or to resolve my symptoms?

Icing or using an NSAID (ie Advil) after an injury is an intuitive intervention. Put the ice pack
on, and you can see the swelling go down, feel your pain fade away.

If your goal is just to reduce pain and inflammation, then icing works. But after I sprain my ankle,
reducing pain is not my goal. It’s a useful guide in how far I’ve progressed in my recovery. Nor is
reducing inflammation. Inflammation is an immune response, bringing resources to the site of the
injury so that your body recovers.

Dr. Gabe Mirkin coined the acronym RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) for treating soft tissue
injuries. Yet even he now admits on his
[website](https://drmirkin.com/fitness/why-ice-delays-recovery.html) that this approach, while
succeeding in reducing pain and inflammation, and _slows recovery._ He says himself:

Anything That Reduces Inflammation Also Delays Healing

There is a common pattern here:

1. The body has an ailment, and the immune system responds.
2. The immune response is often the most unpleasant part of being sick, so we associate our sickness
   with the immune response.
3. If an intervention reduces our immune response, we assume that we are getting better.
4. We are actually just eliminating or delaying our immune response, sometimes at the expense of
   curing the disease itself.

Sometimes, suppressing our immune response is exactly the correct intervention. For example, imagine
you have the flu (root ailment) and it’s giving you a 105 degree fever (immune response). The fever
is going to kill you way before the flu will, so you should take NSAIDs and do whatever else you
need to do to reduce your fever. If you have a 101 degree fever, you have to weigh decide whether
short term pain reduction is the reduction in your body’s ability to fight the infection (see
[study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7812885/#:~:text=a%20febrile%20response.-,A%20mild%20fever%20appears%20to%20improve%20outcome%3B%20it%20appears%20to,the%20host%20outweigh%20the%20benefit)).

Don’t confuse alleviating symptoms with treating an illness. It always surprises me how common this
mistake is; here are some of my ideas why:

1. Alleviating symptoms happens on a short time scale, and is thus very visible and high-feedback.
   Consider the twisted ankle again: if I put the icepack on, I can see my ankle reduce in size, and
   feel the pain reducing. If a week later my ankle is healing slower than expected, I could blame
   the ice pack, but I could also blame so many other things (being active again too quickly,
   drinking last night, spraining my ankle worse than last time). A clear cause-effect relationship
   pleases patients, because they feel that they know what’s happening. It pleases doctors, because
   they will easily be able to make studies showing that their interventions have statistically
   significant effects.
2. Even if doctors can’t cure your ailment, they do know how to cure your symptoms. As discussed
   previously, doctors are incentivized to do something instead of nothing so that their patients
   feel satisfied.
3. Lessening immune response often just is the correct medical intervention. This was true in the
   105˚ fever case discussed previously, but let’s consider another. An allergic reaction is your
   immune system lighting up in a disproportionately aggressive way to some small external stimulus.
   Suppressing immune response is synonymous with curing that episode of the disease. Because
   suppressing the immune response is correct in some cases, it makes it harder to notice that it’s
   a misguided approach in others.

## III: How have financial incentives impacted research?

Companies and doctors are financially incentivized to tell you that their drugs and treatments are
safe and effective. I’m not telling you that you can’t trust any study. Unscrupulous pharma
companies exist, but scrupulous ones do too. The point is that some heuristics which at first seem
like hippie-dippie earth-loving platitudes turn out to be quite reasonable. For example, plenty of
people are happy to put plants in their bodies (ostensibly because they’re unprocessed, or because
our earth mother has graciously bestowed them upon us), but steer far away from pharmaceuticals. I’m
sure you can imagine someone in your life who, if they had the flu and were offered aspirin or a
healing tea made from black birch, would take the tea every time.[^4] You might feel that you’re
firmly in the aspirin camp. It turns out that the two have the same active ingredient.

The heuristic at work here is _plants are safe, synthetic drugs are not_. It’s a heuristic; it’s not
always true. But it’s helpful! A working synthetic drug is patentable. You can bill insurance
companies for it. It’s potentially worth billions to a pharma company. Whether it’s through
marketing, or funding studies, or comping tickets for the right doctors to speak at conferences, all
that money is going to increase the likelihood that the drug is deemed safe and effective. People
are still selling the unpatentable plants, and they are also financially incentivized, but usually
much more weakly. And in some cases the pharma companies actively try to paint the plant as
insufficient or harmful so that they can sell their synthetic alternative.[^5]

You might subscribe to the opposite heuristic. _Synthetic drugs are safe, plants are not_. And you
could tell a good story, too. All prescription drugs have to be approved by the FDA, deemed safe and
effective. This heuristic is useful too. Let’s see how we can use both.

Imagine you have a rare disease that is giving you yellow bumps on your leg, _bumpitis_. Your
allopathic doctor tells you to take _synthobump_, a new pharmaceutical that treats the illness. Your
naturopathic doctor tells you to drink the tea of _papaver sanus_, a healing poppy. You go on PubMed
and there are hundreds of papers studying each treatment’s effect on _bumpitis_. You read a couple
studies, and it seems like _synthobump_ is roughly as effective as _papaver sanus_, and the studies
for each are of similar rigor. A systematic review recommends each equally. What do you do? It would
be pretty fair to guess that there’s more money behind _synthobump_ than _papaver_ and decide to use
_papaver._

Now imagine that your cousin Cecilia is backpacking in Bhutan when she gets _bumpitis_. It will be
days before she can see a doctor or get access to the internet. Her friend Maple darts into the
forest to pick a bunch of _papaver_. “Last time I had _bumpitis_ these flowers cured me overnight,”
she explains. Cecilia’s other friend Preston digs some _synthobump_ out of his medkit. “My doctor
prescribed this to me in case any of us got _bumpitis_ out here,” he tells her. What should Cecilia
do? The information she has is that _synthobump_ has been approved by the FDA[^6] and thus has
scientific data suggesting that it works, while _papaver_ was helpful to her friend once. Cecilia
should take the _synthobump_.

There’s an easy trap to fall into here. The internet is a cesspool overrun with chatbots. If you
cast a wide enough net when looking for evidence, everything looks ambiguous. If your
decision-making like this, you’re in trouble:

- Check to see if _synthobump_ works. There are three studies on PubMed saying that it does, and one
  with null results.
- Check to see if _papaver sanus_ works. There are five people on Reddit saying that it works, one
  of whom who had a horrible adverse reaction to _synthobump._
- Conclude that the evidence is mixed, and use _papaver_.

You have to be able and willing to read and evaluate a study, and to apply any other heuristics you
might use in a logically consistent way, if you want to stop deferring to generic medical advice and
the wisdom of the crowd. It’s pretty easy to publish a bad study, and even easier to fall for one
(see [replication crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis)). The mistake people
make is that it’s not _uniformly difficult_. A study that sticks 50 people in minimalist shoes and
50 people in Nikes, then makes them run every day and measures who gets injured and how? I can read
that one. I know what an ACL tear is, I’m good enough at statistics to tell whether or not they’re
p-hacking. A study on how cold water immersion affects biochemistry? I don’t deeply understand how
dopamine re-uptake affects my moods. If five studies said good things about dopamine and five
studies said bad things about serotonin, and my doctor told me one side or the other was BS, I would
take her word for it.

I think this is the source of a lot of the tension between the anti-establishment, plant-medicine
crowd and those who defer to modern medicine. The pro-establishment crowd sees the other side
falling for not-very-convincing conspiracy theories on the internet that denigrate really helpful
therapies, and they (I think correctly) assert that a lot of people would be a lot healthier if they
stopped trying to be their own doctor and just did what an average physician would tell them to,
every time. To which I and the rest of the crunchy crowd would holler back with indignation: “don’t
tell me what to do!”

Finding the right balance between doing your research and trusting the system is really difficult,
and I don’t claim to be doing it perfectly. You might be convinced that your best strategy is to
always follow generic medical wisdom. The goal of this essay is to convince that you can do a little
better, or at least that you could if you were willing to put in the effort.

## IV: How are my doctor’s incentivizes different from my own?

#### Doctors make money when you visit them

But you know this, so I won’t belabor the point. Doctors are humans who feel empathy, and they are
many mechanism to punish them for acting greedily, so be skeptical, but not excessively so. Let’s
move on to some subtler effects.

#### Companies and doctors don’t want to get sued

So they will (a) defer to giving you standard care, even if that isn’t optimal in your specific
circumstance, and (b) avoid a small chance of a catastrophic outcome that could end in a lawsuit,
even if that means you must accept a high chance of serious-but-not-suably-bad side effects. This is
known as [defensive medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defensive_medicine).

To give a concrete example, imagine that you see your son fall and hit his head at a tennis match.
It hurts and is bleeding a little, but he looks OK. The EMTs arrive and tell you the prudent thing
to do is to take your son to the hospital to evaluate for a concussion and get an MRI for a brain
bleed. You don’t have insurance and know that this will cost you thousands. But the EMTs are being
just a little pushy; maybe they even make you sign a document refusing treatment. The EMTs have
different incentives from you: if your son does have a brain bleed and they don’t take him to the
ER, they will get fired, or worse, sued. They certainly won’t be on the hook for your ER bill.

As an EMT myself (I am certified but never actually held a job as one) I can tell you that all the
EMTs I interacted with were excellent people who put their patients’ well-being first. They never
let a frustrating external incentive like the threat of getting sued stop them from giving the best
medical advice they could. But this is a lot to expect from everyone all the time, when people have
bills to pay and families to feed. Furthermore, any push to be overly conservative might not be
coming from your EMT herself. It could be protocol from the company she works her, which she is
obliged to follow.

The specific situation of a head injury is scary. Unless you’re certain you know what you’re doing
and have a strong reason to not seek further medical care, you should probably just take the EMTs’
advice.

#### Doctors want you to think they did a good job

Doctors are incentivized to have their patients _think_ they did a good job (so that patients leave
a good review on Zocdoc, refer their friends, etc). Typically when you go to the doctor, you are
asking your doctor for help, for them to _do something_. If your doctor tells you that none of the
treatments for your ailment are worth the risks, you’ll be disappointed, and you’ll probably find a
new doctor who can do something. Thus many medical professionals have a bias in favor of _action_.

Felicity and Dolores each have a respiratory infection that’s given them a fever for the past three
days. Felicity goes to an urgent care in Greenpoint. Her doctor sees that she’s sick and wants
treatment, and gives her antibiotics. She gets better quickly and leaves a good Zocdoc review.
Dolores goes to an urgent care in Williamsburg, which tells her how horrible antibiotics are for her
gut. She gets better slowly and maybe leaves a not-so-nice review.

Now you have a respiratory infection, and you need to pick which urgent care to go to. You know how
dangerous antibiotics are, so you want to go to a doctor who will weigh the risks against the harms.
But you still end going to the urgent care in Greenpoint and being given antibiotics, because that
clinic had good reviews.

Doctors are incentivized to be activist, and the most popular doctors tend to be the most
activist.[^7]

## V: Do the studies of this treatment have hard outcomes or surrogate outcomes? Are they RCTs or observational studies?

Some jargon: a _mortality endpoint study_ or a study with _hard outcomes_ measures an intervention’s
effects on disease or death, while a _biomarker endpoint study_ or a study with _surrogate outcomes_
measures an intervention’s effects on a biomarker, ie blood pressure, weight, cholesterol levels,
etc. I use RCT to abbreviate
[randomized controlled trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial), as opposed
to an [observational study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_study)

As early as the 1940s, doctors began to postulate that dietary cholesterol increases cholesterol in
the blood (particularly LDL, the bad kind), which in turn increases the risk of heart attack. The
first evidence that came out showed a link between cholesterol in the blood and heart disease (ie
[Framingham Heart Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framingham_Heart_Study),
[Seven Countries Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Countries_Study)). An observational
study like this is relatively cheap and easy to put together; you just have to measure people’s
cholesterol and then see if they die. Similarly, it’s quick to do an RCT that links diet to
cholesterol levels; you have to make people eat something different for months or even years, but
you don’t have to wait for anyone to die before you can publish results.

If all the information you have is that high cholesterol causes heart attacks, and a diet high in
fat (or in saturated fat) causes high cholesterol, then the evidence, though far from perfect,
should nudge people to eat less fat. But you haven’t fully proven your theory until you have an RCT
that shows both things at once — that if you make people eat less meat they both have lower
cholesterol and get fewer heart attacks. Trials like these are expensive and often take decades.
When the RCTs about diet, cholesterol, and heart disease did come out, they _did not_ show that
eating less fat or less saturated fat reduces the risk of heart attack
([one paper](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/202339),
[another paper](https://www.bmj.com/content/353/bmj.i1246)).

But null results don’t make headlines in the same way that positive results do, and medical advice
is often stickier than it should be. A simple piece of advice (eat less fat, or less saturdated fat,
both of which can be read off any food label) was replaced with more complicated advice about
omega-3s and omega-6s. I geek out about this stuff, and I can’t remember which one is the good one.
So I have a lot of sympathy for people who are confused by the new advice and keep implicitly
following the old guidelines, even though the old advice doesn’t have much scientific basis anymore.

I’m not trying to say that observational studies and biomarker endpoint studies are useless. They
can be very useful, and often they’re the only hard data we have. For example, we still have no RCT
in which we make people smoke cigarettes and then observe if they die more frequently (for obvious
reasons).[^8] Cigarettes haven’t been around for very long, and they physically burn your throat, so
before seeing any medical evidence it would be extremely plausible to believe that they’re harmful.
By contrast, Humans have been eating red meat for eons, and it tastes great, so a study that says
that red meat is bad is just more surprising, less in line with my prior beliefs. Even though the
evidence is observational in both cases, I’m much more confident that cigarettes are harmful than I
am that meat is.

You can often tell that a study will be observational knowing only the one-sentence version of it
someone gives you in conversation. Imagine my friend tells me that there is a new study linking
cigarette alcohol use to weight loss. I am already quite confident that this study is observational;
no review board would allow you to encourage people to drink. I know to take this evidence with a
grain of salt, but I’m very wary of telling my friend this. They’ll be pissed that I’m discounting a
study I haven’t even read.

## Conclusion

If you’re not scared every time you disagree with what a large majority of people believe, you
should be. We live in a free society filled with smart people, where good ideas tend to replace bad
ones. But in order for that to happen, people have to keep poking at the bad ideas, and having new
good ones.

My defiant spirit compels me to keep questioning widely accepted wisdom, in arenas like medicine
where I am not a subject matter expert. I acknowledge that this is frustrating for my loved ones,
that I’m often wrong, and that there are plenty of real medical doctors in the world more qualified
to be doing this than me. I hope this essay convinces you that I’ve thought hard about my
controversial opinions, and maybe even that I’m right about one or two of them.
