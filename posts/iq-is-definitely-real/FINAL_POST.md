title: IQ Is Definitely Real

This is an argument I've had too many times, so I'm going to write it down here once and for all. If
you already think IQ tests do an imperfect but decent job measuring the thing we casually refer to
as 'being smart' then you can skip this post. I claim zero novel contributions to this topic.

B: IQ only measures one thing. How could that possibly be useful?

A: Scientists first proposed IQ (back then 'g') because they found that mathematical reasoning
correlates with verbal reasoning correlates with reaction time, etc. Given this, IQ is a useful
metric, and 'smart' is a useful linguistic concept. See
[Neural Categories](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yFDKvfN6D87Tf5J9f/neural-categories).

B: But there are other ways of being smart. There are so many ways to be smart.

A: Agreed. One way to be big is to be tall. Another way is to be short and bulky. Big is still a
useful word. Name your way of being 'smart' that doesn't correlate positively with IQ.

B: Being emotionally intelligent. Being wise. Being virtuous.

A: Emotional intelligence is [positively correlated with IQ](@maccann-2014). $r=.8$.

I suspect you would define wise in a way that isn't measurable. If this argument ends in us agreeing
"all measurable traits which one would describe as 'smart' are positively correlated with IQ" then
one should have the prior that the non-measurable ones correlate positively too.

Being virtuous is definitely not the same thing as being smart. I accuse you of trying to paint me
into the corner of 'all good human traits are positively correlated,' which I did not claim.

B: IQ only measures your upbringing and your education.

A: IQ is most strongly correlated with your environment when you're younger and gradually decouples.
Reasonable estimates for what % of IQ variance in adults is caused by education / upbringing vary
from [0% to 20%](@plomin-deary-2015). Reasonable estimates for what % is genetically determined
range from [60% to 80%](@plomin-deary-2015).

Words can be useful descriptors even if they entirely result from our environment.

B: IQ is a test that can be gamed.

A: If you take a second IQ test, your score improves by [about 5 points](@scharfen-2018). After
[three tests](@scharfen-2018), your score stops improving with practice, for a total boost of
[7-8 points](@hausknecht-2007). An extra year of schooling improves your score by
[1-5 points](@ritchie-2018). It is weakly gamable.

Even if it was totally gamable: who cares! One can still make apples-to-apples comparisons, eg two
kids who take the test with similar amounts of prep. In practice, one make reasonable comparisons
between different test-takers because the phenomenon is so well-studied.

B: If IQ is real, you've wrecked growth mindset.

A: Growth mindset works with [effect size $\approx .1$](@sisk-2018). To the extent that believing IQ
is heritable inhibits growth mindset, I'm sad.

Believing certain true things is sometimes detrimental. IQ does not fall in this bucket. Knowing
that IQ is real lets one make better choices, by, eg,
[acknowledging that tracking makes sense and implementing it](/blog/track-the-kids/).[^effect_size]

[^effect_size]: And tracking has a ~5x bigger effect size than growth mindset interventions!

B: If IQ is real, then some people are better than others.

A: I disagree. Some humans are taller than others. Heightists value tall humans more than short
humans, even though height is genetic. Morality issue on behalf of the heightists. I choose to value
everyone equally, regardless of their IQ or height.

B: My IQ is low, and I feel inferior.

A: You shouldn't! Read
[The Parable of the Talents (SSC)](https://slatestarcodex.com/2015/01/31/the-parable-of-the-talents/).
In short: we are compassionate to people who are overweight or balding or unathletic, because these
things are largely genetically determined and there's no point in shaming people for things outside
their control. The same should apply to intelligence.

{@maccann-2014}: MacCann, Joseph, Newman & Roberts, "Emotional Intelligence Is a Second-Stratum
Factor of Intelligence: Evidence From Hierarchical and Bifactor Models," _Emotion_ 14(2) (2014):
358–374 — ability-measured EI loads on general intelligence at 0.80 in hierarchical models.
<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24341786/>

{@plomin-deary-2015}: Plomin & Deary, "Genetics and Intelligence Differences: Five Special
Findings," _Molecular Psychiatry_ 20 (2015): 98–108 — heritability of intelligence rises linearly
from ~20% in infancy to ~60% in adulthood (up to ~80% in later adulthood), with shared-environment
effects shrinking correspondingly. <https://www.nature.com/articles/mp2014105>

{@sisk-2018}: Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara, "To What Extent and Under Which Circumstances
Are Growth Mind-Sets Important to Academic Achievement? Two Meta-Analyses," _Psychological Science_
29(4) (2018): 549–571 — mindset–achievement correlation r ≈ 0.10; average intervention effect d ≈
0.08, near zero for typical students.
<https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797617739704>

{@scharfen-2018}: Scharfen, Peters & Holling, "Retest Effects in Cognitive Ability Tests: A
Meta-Analysis," _Intelligence_ 67 (2018): 44–66 — scores rise ~0.33 SD (about 5 IQ points) from
first to second administration and ~0.5 SD by the third, then plateau.
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289617303276>

{@hausknecht-2007}: Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard, "Retesting in Selection: A
Meta-Analysis of Coaching and Practice Effects for Tests of Cognitive Ability," _Journal of Applied
Psychology_ 92(2) (2007): 373–385 — d = 0.24 by the second administration, 0.51 by the third; gains
are larger with coaching and identical test forms. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17371085/>

{@ritchie-2018}: Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, "How Much Does Education Improve Intelligence? A
Meta-Analysis," _Psychological Science_ 29(8) (2018): 1358–1369 — 1–5 IQ points per additional year
of education, from compulsory-schooling law changes, school-entry regression discontinuities, and
prior-IQ-controlled longitudinal designs; effects persist across the lifespan.
<https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797618774253>
