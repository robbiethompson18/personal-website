# Would Lie Detectors Be Good: the "natural experiments within drugs" paragraph

Compiled 2026-11-11 by one research agent, for the paragraph in `FINAL_POST.md` that starts "Are
there natural experiments within 'drugs'?". The general enforcement argument (cannabis arrest
counts, social-media policing, Prohibition agent counts, Stuntz 2001, Marlinspike 2013, red-light
cameras, Xinjiang) is in the last two sections of `POST_RESEARCH_THOUGHTS.md` and is not repeated
here; I point to it where it matters.

Four candidate explanations, as in the brief: **H** actual harm, **E** how hard the drug is to
regulate (ease of production and smuggling), **C** cultural embedding, **X** historical accident and
politics. I add a fifth, **R** (tax revenue and an incumbent legal industry), because the literature
does. Two outcome variables, kept apart throughout: **what the law says** and **whether the law
works**.

Marking:

- **No mark**: I opened the page or PDF and the claim is on it. Where the page came through a fetch
  tool that summarises (PMC, NBER and EH.net pages), numbers are as the tool reported them and
  quotes are short.
- **(snippet)**: from a search engine's summary of the linked page; page not opened.
- **(secondhand)**: a source I opened, reporting someone else's claim.
- **(unverified)**: my recollection or the brief's; no page found. Treat as a lead.
- **(mine)**: my inference from the evidence, not a sourced claim.

No synthesis or extraction procedure appears anywhere in this file. Production difficulty is
described only as "what is the bottleneck".

## Where the draft paragraph is wrong

1. **"LBJ banning weed because hippies smoked."** Wrong president, wrong decade, wrong drug.
   - Cannabis was federally restricted by the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, drafted by Harry Anslinger
     of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics and signed by Roosevelt on 2 August 1937, over the American
     Medical Association's objection
     ([Wikipedia, "Marihuana Tax Act of 1937"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marihuana_Tax_Act_of_1937)).
     The Supreme Court struck it down in _Leary v. United States_ (1969) and Congress replaced it
     with the Controlled Substances Act, Pub. L. 91-513, 27 October 1970, under Nixon (same page).
   - What Johnson did sign was the federal LSD possession ban: Pub. L. 90-639, approved 24 October
     1968 (date from a search summary; the statement is dated the 25th). His signing statement: "I
     am signing into law a bill to increase the penalty for the sale or manufacture of LSD, and to
     impose, for the first time, a penalty for its possession", with sale "a felony, punishable by 5
     years in prison and a $10,000 fine" and possession "a misdemeanor punishable by up to 1 year in
     prison and a $1,000 fine"
     ([American Presidency Project, 25 October 1968](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/statement-the-president-upon-signing-bill-relating-traffic-or-possession-drugs-such-lsd)).
     So "LBJ banned acid in 1968" is true. The brief's name for it, "Staggers–Dodd", I could not
     confirm.
   - Cannabis went into Schedule I in 1970 as a placeholder: Assistant Secretary of Health Roger
     Egeberg "recommended that marijuana temporarily be placed in Schedule I... pending the
     Commission's report"
     ([Wikipedia, "Shafer Commission"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafer_Commission)). The Shafer
     Commission, nine of whose members Nixon picked, reported on 22 March 1972 and recommended that
     "possession of marijuana for personal use no longer be an offense"
     ([NORML, 2022](https://norml.org/blog/2022/03/20/50-years-ago-today-congress-own-expert-commission-demanded-they-repeal-marijuana-prohibition/),
     quoting the report). Nixon had said beforehand: "Even if the commission does recommend that it
     be legalized, I will not follow that recommendation" (Wikipedia, quoting the New York Times, 13
     February 1972). The placeholder is still there for non-medical cannabis 54 years later. That is
     the real "historical accident" in this story and it is better than the one in the draft.
   - The "because hippies" story rests on one quotation. Dan Baum, "Legalize It All"
     ([Harper's, April 2016](https://harpers.org/archive/2016/04/legalize-it-all/)), reports John
     Ehrlichman telling him in 1994: "The Nixon campaign in 1968, and the Nixon White House after
     that, had two enemies: the antiwar left and black people... by getting the public to associate
     the hippies with marijuana and blacks with heroin, and then criminalizing both heavily, we
     could disrupt those communities... Did we know we were lying about the drugs? Of course we
     did."
   - The dispute: (a) Ehrlichman died in 1999 and the quote surfaced 22 years after the interview;
     Baum left it out of the 1996 book the interview was for, saying it did not fit the book's style
     ([CNN, 2016](https://amp.cnn.com/cnn/2016/03/23/politics/john-ehrlichman-richard-nixon-drug-war-blacks-hippie)).
     (b) Ehrlichman's five children: "The 1994 alleged 'quote' we saw repeated in social media for
     the first time today does not square with what we know of our father" (same). (c) Three Nixon
     drug-policy officials (Jaffe, DuPont, Donfeld) said the remark, if made, might have been
     "biting sarcasm" (snippet, from a Wikipedia mirror). (d) The quote's history is off: Nixon did
     not criminalise either drug. Marijuana had been federally restricted since 1937 and heroin
     longer (mine, from the dates above). (e) The usual further point, that most of Nixon's drug
     budget went to treatment, I could not open a source for (Vox blocked); unverified.
   - Net: "hippies" is defensible for LSD under Johnson and for Nixon's refusal to follow Shafer. It
     is not the reason cannabis is illegal.

2. **"DMT is... hard to make."** Backwards. DMT is one of the easiest controlled drugs to obtain
   without a chemist. The DEA's own fact sheet: "DMT is extracted from various plant material or
   synthetically produced in clandestine labs. Like other hallucinogens, Internet sales and
   distribution have served as the source of drug supply in this country"
   ([DEA Diversion, DMT fact sheet, 2025](https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/dmt.pdf)).
   It "occurs naturally in many plants", is "more often extracted from plant-sources" than
   synthesised, and "The chemicals involved in the extraction are commonly available"
   ([Wikipedia, "Dimethyltryptamine"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimethyltryptamine); the middle
   claim carries a citation-needed tag there). The source plant is not itself scheduled in the US
   and the bark is sold openly, though buying it to obtain DMT is illegal (snippet, low-quality
   sources). The bottleneck is not chemistry or materials. It is demand: US forensic labs logged 589
   DMT reports in 2016 and 1,061 in 2021 (DEA fact sheet), against hundreds of thousands a year for
   the major drugs (unverified). DMT is a case of an easy-to-make, low-harm drug in the strictest
   schedule, which makes it evidence against both H and E as explanations of legal status.

3. **"Meth is very hard to make."** No. Small-batch production from over-the-counter cold medicine
   was common enough that Congress regulated the cold medicine. The Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic
   Act of 2005 (signed 9 March 2006) put pseudoephedrine behind the counter with a logbook, ID
   check, and limits of 3.6 g a day and 9 g a month
   ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_Methamphetamine_Epidemic_Act_of_2005)). US lab
   seizures were 17,562 in 2004 and 12,185 in 2005 on one count (snippet, EPIC via wire report) and
   "23,700" in 2004 on DEA's later count, against 60 in 2023 (snippet,
   [DEA 2024](https://www.dea.gov/press-releases/2024/05/09/dea-releases-2024-national-drug-threat-assessment)).
   The right description: meth is easy to make and has a precursor chokepoint, because the
   precursors come from a handful of large chemical manufacturers (Frontline, below). That is more
   useful to the post than "hard": it is the one mass-market drug where the state found a lever,
   pulled it repeatedly, and the results were measured (section 4).

4. **"Alcohol can be made anywhere, so it's damn hard to regulate, so regulating it didn't work."**
   Each link is weaker than it looks.
   - Prohibition cut drinking. Miron and Zwiebel, "Alcohol Consumption During Prohibition"
     ([1991](https://www.nber.org/papers/w3675)): consumption "fell sharply at the beginning of
     Prohibition, to approximately 30 percent of its pre-Prohibition level", then recovered to about
     60–70%. Dills and Miron, "Alcohol Prohibition and Cirrhosis"
     ([2003](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9681)): "Constitutional prohibition reduced cirrhosis by
     about 10-20 percent", against earlier claims of up to 50%. Blocker, "Did Prohibition Really
     Work?"
     ([American Journal of Public Health, 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1470475/)):
     2.6 US gallons of pure alcohol per drinking-age adult before, 1.2 in the years after repeal,
     "less than half the level of the pre-Prohibition period", and consumption "did not surpass the
     pre-Prohibition peak until the early 1970s". Mark Moore's op-ed "Actually, Prohibition Was a
     Success"
     ([New York Times, 16 October 1989](https://www.nytimes.com/1989/10/16/opinion/actually-prohibition-was-a-success.html)):
     male cirrhosis deaths 29.5 per 100,000 in 1911 and 10.7 in 1929 (snippet; I could not open the
     op-ed).
   - The sceptic's side is the same economist. Miron's encyclopedia entry
     ([EH.net, 2001](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/alcohol-prohibition/)) says cirrhosis "began
     declining from its pre-1920 peak by as early as 1908" and that Prohibition "had little impact
     on the path of alcohol consumption". The range these papers support is a 10–40% sustained
     reduction, with the low end from the most careful one (mine).
   - It failed politically, not operationally. Blocker attributes repeal chiefly to the Depression
     and the argument that repeal would restore tax revenue and jobs; he calls enforcement the
     Volstead Act's "Achilles' heel" because the Bureau was "chronically underfunded by Congress".
     That is a will failure producing a capability failure, which is exactly the post's distinction.
   - "Can be made anywhere" does not separate alcohol from the banned drugs. Cannabis and psilocybin
     mushrooms can be grown at home and DMT extracted at home, and all three are in the strictest US
     schedule. Home-producibility predicts neither which drugs are legal nor whether a ban cuts
     consumption (Gorbachev, Bihar, below).
   - Okrent's _Last Call_ (2010) is the brief's other source for "it cut consumption". I did not
     open it.

5. **A fifth premise, from the previous paragraph of the draft: "we don't bring overwhelming force
   on people smoking weed in their home. We do on dealers."** Half right. Arrests fall on users:
   86.7% of US drug arrests in 2019 (1,351,533 of 1,558,862) were for possession (snippet,
   [Drug War Facts](https://drugwarfacts.org/node/234) citing FBI). Prison falls on sellers: 27% of
   people in prison for drugs in 2019 were there for possession only, 73% for sales and other
   commercial offences (snippet,
   [Pew, 2022](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/issue-briefs/2022/02/drug-arrests-stayed-high-even-as-imprisonment-fell-from-2009-to-2019)).
   What is left alone is use _at home by people police do not otherwise encounter_. See section 6.

## Read these first

1. **Caulkins, Reuter and Coulson, "Basing drug scheduling decisions on scientific ranking of
   harmfulness: false promise from false premises" (Addiction, 2011).**
   [PDF](https://reuter.it-prod-webhosting.aws.umd.edu/sites/default/files/reuter/files/basing_drug_scheduling_decisions_on_scientific_ranking_of_harmfulness.pdf).
   5 pages. The best short statement of why harm rank and legal status need not match, and it
   contains the sentence that answers the draft's question (section 5).
2. **Nutt, King and Phillips, "Drug harms in the UK: a multicriteria decision analysis" (Lancet,
   2010).** [PDF copy](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2010-nutt.pdf),
   [DOI](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61462-6>). 8 pages; Figure 2 is the ranking everyone
   cites. Read with item 1.
3. **Andrew Barr's review of Courtwright, _Forces of Habit_ (2001), "Altered States" (Wilson
   Quarterly, 2001).** [Article](https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/_/altered-states/). About
   1,500 words. The quickest route to Courtwright's answer to "why are some drugs more legal than
   others". The book itself:
   [Harvard University Press, 2001](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.4159/9780674029903/html)
   (publisher page, not opened), 277 pp.; the relevant chapters are the last three, on taxes and
   smuggling, the turn to prohibition, and licit versus illicit drugs (chapter topics from memory,
   unverified).
4. **Frontline, "The Quaalude Lesson" (PBS, 2006).**
   [Page](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/meth/faqs/quaaludes.html). One page. The same DEA
   official tried the same chokepoint strategy on two drugs; it eliminated one and failed on the
   other.
5. **Dobkin and Nicosia, "The War on Drugs: Methamphetamine, Public Health, and Crime" (American
   Economic Review, 2009).**
   [RAND abstract page](https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP20090319.html). 26 pages.
   The best-measured supply shock in the literature. I read the abstract only.
6. **Bhattacharya, Gathmann and Miller, "The Gorbachev Anti-Alcohol Campaign and Russia's Mortality
   Crisis" (American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2013).**
   [NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18589.pdf). Read the introduction, about 4
   pages. A ban on the most home-producible, most embedded drug, in the hardest-drinking country,
   that worked on mortality and was abandoned.
7. **Blocker, "Did Prohibition Really Work? Alcohol Prohibition as a Public Health Innovation"
   (American Journal of Public Health, 2006).**
   [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1470475/). About 10 pages. The
   will-versus-capability reading of Prohibition, by a historian.
8. **Home Office, "Review of the Psychoactive Substances Act 2016" (2018).**
   [PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5beec777e5274a2b0fd695db/Review_of_the_Psychoactive_Substances_Act__2016___web_.pdf).
   Executive summary, 4 pages. The one modern law that banned every psychoactive substance by
   default and had to list the exceptions.

### What the table shows

**Harm does not predict legal severity.** I computed rank correlations on the 20 drugs Nutt scored.
Codings: UK class today A=3 (heroin, crack, methamphetamine, cocaine, methadone, ecstasy, LSD,
mushrooms), B=2 (amphetamine, cannabis, GHB, ketamine, mephedrone), C=1 (benzodiazepines, khat,
steroids, buprenorphine), uncontrolled=0 (alcohol, tobacco, butane). US schedule I=5 (heroin,
cannabis, GHB, mephedrone, khat via cathinone, ecstasy, LSD, mushrooms), II=4 (crack, cocaine,
methamphetamine, amphetamine, methadone), III=3 (ketamine, steroids, buprenorphine), IV=2
(benzodiazepines), unscheduled=0 (alcohol, tobacco, butane).

| Nutt score vs US schedule, scheduled drugs only     | 17  | −0.13    |
| Dutch individual score vs UK class today            | 18  | +0.02    |
| Dutch individual score vs US schedule               | 18  | −0.38    |
| Nutt score vs Dutch individual score                | 18  | +0.89    |

Nutt's paper reports the same thing for the UK: the correlation between its scores and the Misuse of
Drugs Act classification "is 0·04, showing that there is effectively no relation". My Pearson figure
on the 2010 classes is 0.03, so the coding reproduces theirs. With n = 20 none of the
harm-versus-law figures is distinguishable from zero. The two expert rankings agree with each other
(+0.89 here; Nutt reports a Pearson correlation of 0.80) and with neither legal system. The 2010 UK
classes are from memory: GHB and ketamine were Class C then and khat was uncontrolled.

Two cautions about reading that as "the law is irrational":

- The US schedule is not built as a harm ranking. Schedule I versus II turns on "no currently
  accepted medical use" (Wikipedia, quoting the statute), which is why cocaine, methamphetamine and
  fentanyl sit below psilocybin. The negative US correlation is mostly that rule. Penalties are set
  elsewhere, by quantity thresholds in sentencing law, which would be a better severity measure; I
  did not assemble those (lead).
- Caulkins, Reuter and Coulson (2011) on the rankings themselves. The overall score mixes units:
  harm to users is scored per user and harm to others in aggregate, so it "measure[s] neither harm
  per unit of use nor aggregate harm, but an awkward weighted average of each". Their example:
  tobacco 26 against GHB 19, when the UK had 8.5 million smokers and about 50,000 GHB users. The
  weights are value judgements, not measurements. Harms depend on the legal regime:
  "Methamphetamines create environmental problems when produced in small, technologically primitive
  laboratories; this would disappear if they were legal." Drugs cannot be scheduled one at a time:
  any threshold between 27 and 54 "would imply banning crack but allowing cocaine", and "it is easy
  to make crack from powder cocaine". And the comparison that matters for policy is the same drug
  under two regimes, not two drugs under one: "it is wrong to argue that legal substance A causes
  more total harm than does illegal substance B; ergo, B should be legal and/or A should be
  illegal".

**Which of H/E/C/X predicts legal status** (mine, from the table):

- C, defined as mass customary use by the enfranchised majority of the regulating society, predicts
  the three permanent exemptions (alcohol, tobacco, caffeine) and nothing else gets that treatment.
  Every drug whose embedding was in someone else's culture (khat, coca, opium smoking, kratom in the
  West) was banned or nearly so by the society that did not use it. So C is not "part of human
  culture"; it is "part of the lawmakers' culture", which is hard to distinguish from who the users
  are.
- R predicts the middle tier: everything in US Schedules II–IV in the table is there because a
  medical industry uses it.
- X predicts timing and tier for the rest: each ban follows a panic about a visible group or a
  salient death (LSD 1968, MDMA 1985, steroids 1990, GHB 2000, mephedrone 2010, khat 2014, nitrous
  oxide 2023), and three of those went against the regulator's own expert advice (MDMA, khat,
  nitrous oxide). X is also needed to explain what is _not_ banned: kava, betel and kratom are
  unembedded in the US and legal there, because no panic has yet stuck.
- H matters through salience, not magnitude. Visible deaths trigger bans; aggregate harm does not.
- E predicts almost nothing about status. The easiest drugs to make (cannabis, mushrooms, DMT) and
  the hardest (LSD) share Schedule I. E shows up at the margin: precursor laws exist only where
  there is a precursor chokepoint, and the UK khat ban was justified by trafficking logistics.

## Natural experiments

### Supply shocks at production chokepoints

1. **Methaqualone, 1981–84. Verified.** What changed: the DEA's Gene Haislip went to "the chemical
   manufacturers of methaqualone powder in West Germany, Austria, Hungary, and China" and persuaded
   each government to stop the trade; Congress then banned the one US manufacturer's product
   in 1984. Before: in 1981 DEA "ranked Quaalude use second only to marijuana and estimated that 80
   to 90 percent of world production went into the illegal drug trade". After: "By 1984, Quaaludes
   had all but disappeared from the U.S. marketplace." Haislip: "the Colombians could no longer get
   their drug powder... We eliminated the problem"
   ([Frontline, 2006](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/meth/faqs/quaaludes.html)). No
   quantitative series opened. Bears on E: the only clean case of a popular drug eliminated from a
   national market, and it took a precursor made in a few factories. Haislip's memoir traces the
   trail through a Hamburg broker to Hungary and China (snippet).
2. **US methamphetamine precursor controls, 1989–2006. Verified in outline; the cleanest numbers in
   this file.** Same official, same strategy, different chemistry: ephedrine and pseudoephedrine are
   also made by a few large manufacturers (Frontline), but they are also legal cold medicine.
   - 1995 intervention (Dobkin and Nicosia,
     [2009](https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP20090319.html)): "The price of
     methamphetamine tripled and purity declined from 90 percent to 20 percent"; amphetamine-related
     hospital admissions fell 50%, treatment admissions 35%, use among arrestees 55%. "The impact
     was largely temporary. The price returned to its original level within four months; purity,
     hospital admissions, treatment admissions, and arrests approached preintervention levels within
     eighteen months." No measurable fall in property or violent crime.
   - Cunningham and Liu
     ([Addiction, 2003](https://scilit.com/publications/61d731e6a693ff1e1c1855acbbfefbd3), snippet):
     the 1989 bulk-powder rule cut California meth hospital admissions 35%; the 1995 ephedrine rule
     cut them 48% in California, 71% in Arizona, 52% in Nevada; the 1997 pseudoephedrine rule 38%,
     41%, 61%. Each rebounded within two to four years.
   - 2005–06 retail controls: domestic labs fell to 6,951 incidents in 2007, rebounded to 15,314 in
     2010 as buyers pooled purchases, and are near zero now (snippets,
     [GAO-13-204](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-13-204), DEA 2024). Supply moved to Mexico;
     average purity above 90% in 2019 (snippet, DEA).
   - Bears on E: a chokepoint works for exactly as long as there is one route. Small domestic
     producers were eventually eliminated. Use was not.
3. **Australian heroin shortage, 2001. Verified.** What changed: supply fell abruptly in early 2001
   across all states. Measured: opioid deaths among 15–54-year-olds "declined from 1116 in 1999 to
   386 in 2001" and stayed there for at least three years; "a 67% reduction in fatal and nonfatal
   opioid overdoses". Cause: "probably due to a confluence of factors", with high-level law
   enforcement against a small number of importing syndicates "probably a contributory cause".
   Substitution: more cocaine injection in New South Wales, more amphetamines and benzodiazepines in
   Victoria (Degenhardt, Day, Gilmour and Hall,
   [2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1524737)). Bears on E and on capability: an
   island with few import routes. The persistence of the death reduction is the unusual part.
4. **LSD after the Pickard bust, November 2000. Partly verified; the DEA's number is not.**
   Measured: past-year LSD use among US high-school seniors was 6.6% in 2000 and 2001, 3.5% in 2002,
   1.9% in 2003; the survey's lead researcher, in Slate's words, says the group "has never seen such
   a dramatic drop in the use of an established illicit drug"
   ([Slate, 2004](https://www.slate.com/id/2098109/)). The DEA "claims it reduced the LSD supply by
   '95 percent'". Slate's own addendum says the 91 pounds of LSD the government claimed to have
   seized "appears to be a myth of the government's making", and it offers a second cause: the end
   of Grateful Dead touring in 1995, which had kept "LSD users and dealers connected", and of Phish
   touring in 2000 (the Phish part from a search summary). Pickard denies the claim (snippet). Bears
   on E: LSD has the narrowest production base of any major drug, so one bust plausibly mattered;
   the 95% figure should not be quoted as fact.
5. **Safrole seizures and the MDMA drought, 2008–10. Weakly verified; treat as a lead.** Cambodian
   authorities destroyed large stocks of safrole-rich oil in 2008–09 (snippets give 1,278 drums in
   June 2008 and 5.2 and 5.7 tonnes in 2009; the brief's 33 tonnes I could not confirm). In the
   Netherlands the share of ecstasy tablets containing MDMA fell by more than half in 2009,
   mephedrone turned up in tablets sold as ecstasy, and the market was back near normal in 2010
   (snippet, Brunt et al., "Instability of the ecstasy market and a new kid on the block:
   mephedrone", Journal of Psychopharmacology, 2011). In the UK, 154 of 1,848 seized tablets
   contained MDMA in one year (snippet, undated magazine report). Producers who switched precursor
   made a more dangerous drug, PMA, and people died
   ([Vice, 2020](https://www.vice.com/en/article/mdma-drought-safrole-oil-pma-deaths/)). Bears on E,
   with the usual two-year expiry, and on substitution.
6. **Taliban opium bans, 2000–01 and 2022–23. Verified.** 2001: cultivation 7,606 hectares, "a 91
   per cent decrease from last year's estimate of 82,172"; production 185 tonnes, down 94%; Helmand
   went from 42,853 hectares to none
   ([UN News, 2001](https://news.un.org/en/story/2001/10/18962-un-drug-control-agency-reports-sharp-drop-afghanistans-poppy-cultivation)).
   2023: "from 233,000 hectares at the end of 2022 to 10,800 in 2023", production from 6,200 to 333
   tonnes
   ([Al Jazeera, 2023](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2023/11/5/afghan-opium-poppy-cultivation-plunges-by-95-percent-under-taliban-un),
   citing UNODC). Farmer income fell 92% (snippet). Bears on will: a field crop is the easiest drug
   supply to destroy if the state will impoverish the growers. No Western-backed Afghan government
   achieved anything comparable (unverified).
7. **Fentanyl replacing heroin. Verified as description; the causal "law" is contested.** Richard
   Cowan's 1986 formulation: "the harder the enforcement, the harder the drugs" (secondhand,
   [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_law_of_prohibition), citing National Review, 5
   December 1986). Beletsky and Davis, "Today's fentanyl crisis: Prohibition's Iron Law, revisited"
   ([International Journal of Drug Policy, 2017](https://idpc.net/publications/2017/08/today-s-fentanyl-crisis-prohibition-s-iron-law-revisited)):
   supply suppression produces "economic and logistical pressures favouring evermore compact
   substitutes". Evidence for the law is described as "mixed" (snippet,
   [Undark, 2023](https://undark.org/2023/10/16/iron-law-drugs/)). The chokepoint story repeats:
   China's 2019 class-wide control "curtailed almost all direct shipments of fentanyl from China to
   the United States", and production moved to Mexico using Chinese precursors; US synthetic-opioid
   deaths then fell from 77,695 (year to June 2023) to about 37,393 in 2025, down 52%, with DEA
   saying Mexican producers "may be struggling to obtain some key precursors"
   ([CRS, October 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_IF10890_a90986132d374b6ff4ae21e90db1000cb3cfc131.html)).
   Bears on E in both directions: enforcement selects for the drug with the weakest chokepoint, and
   precursor control still moves the numbers.

### Bans on home-producible drugs

8. **US Prohibition, 1920–33.** Numbers in section 1. Consumption fell 30–70% at first and settled
   perhaps 10–40% lower; the law was repealed for revenue and legitimacy reasons. E did not stop the
   ban working. Bears on will.
9. **Gorbachev's anti-alcohol campaign, 1985–88. Verified.** "At the height of the campaign,
   official alcohol sales had fallen by as much as two-thirds (Russians responded by increasing
   home-production of alcohol called samogon – although our estimates suggest not by nearly enough
   to offset the reduction in state supply)." Effect: "about 400,000 fewer deaths per year, a
   reduction of 24% relative to the pre-campaign crude death rate". The mortality surge of the early
   1990s, when crude death rates "soared by 40% between 1990 and 1994", is attributed in large part
   to the campaign's end (Bhattacharya, Gathmann and Miller,
   [2013](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18589.pdf)). Why it was abandoned (lost state revenue,
   unpopularity) I did not verify in the paper. Bears on E and C together: the hardest case on both,
   and state supply control still dominated home production.
10. **Bihar, 2016. Snippet only.** Chakrabarti, Christopher, Scott, Kishore and Nguyen (Lancet
    Regional Health – Southeast Asia,
    [2024](https://doaj.org/article/38515570c6db460ea5789073cbeebabd)), difference-in-differences
    against neighbouring states: frequent drinking among Bihar men had been rising (9.7% to 15%) and
    fell to 7.8% after the ban while neighbours rose to 10.4%; an estimated 2.4 million cases of
    frequent drinking and 2.1 million cases of intimate-partner violence prevented. The authors do
    not recommend bans. Cost side: 348,170 cases and 401,855 arrests by October 2021, and the Patna
    High Court swamped with bail petitions (snippets). The brief's "Chaudhuri" is Chakrabarti.
    Hooch-death counts not verified.
11. **Kentucky dry counties. Verified.** Fernandez, Gohmann and Pinkston, "Breaking Bad in Bourbon
    Country" ([working-paper version](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/75544/1/MPRA_paper_75544.pdf);
    published in the Southern Economic Journal, 2018), instrumenting with 1930s religious
    composition: "the number of meth lab seizures in Kentucky would decrease by 34.5 percent if all
    counties became wet". Bears on substitution: banning the embedded drug raises production of a
    worse one.
12. **Japan's methamphetamine epidemic, 1951–58. Verified; not in the brief and one of the best
    cases.** In 1954 there were "approximately 550,000 chronic users and 2 million ex-users".
    Arrests: 17,528 in 1951, 55,000 in 1954, 32,000 in 1955, 5,000 in 1956, 271 in 1958. Penalties
    were raised in 1954 to five years for a first offence. In October 1954, 46% of arrests were for
    possession, 28% for buying or selling and 22% for use (Tamura, "Japan: stimulant epidemics past
    and present",
    [UN Bulletin on Narcotics, 1989](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/119829NCJRS.pdf)).
    Bears on will: a democracy arrested users in bulk and the epidemic ended in four years. A second
    epidemic began in 1970.
13. **Iceland's beer ban, 1915–89. Verified; a weak test.** Total prohibition from 1915; wine legal
    from 1922 because "Spain refused to buy Iceland's main export, fish, unless Iceland bought
    Spanish wines"; spirits legal from 1935; beer above 2.25% banned until 1 March 1989
    ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_Iceland)). It lasted 74 years because
    a legal substitute existed, so nobody's will was tested. Its only use here is the wine
    exemption, which is R.
14. Cannabis home-grow and mushrooms: not researched. See Leads.

### Embedded drugs that were banned, unembedded drugs that were not

15. **China and opium. Verified as outline; numbers not obtained.** 1906–17: "a highly effective
    intervention to suppress the production of opium", by means that included rural populations
    having "their property destroyed, their land confiscated and/or [being] publically tortured,
    humiliated and executed" (Windle, "Harms caused by China's 1906–17 opium suppression
    intervention",
    [International Journal of Drug Policy, 2013](https://idpc.net/publications/2013/09/harms-caused-by-china-s-1906-17-opium-suppression-intervention),
    abstract). It collapsed with central authority after 1916 (snippet, Britannica). 1950–52: the
    Communist campaign "successfully eliminated the centuries-long epidemic of opium abuse" (Zhou
    Yongming, "Anti-Drug Campaigns and State Building: China's Experiences in the 1950s",
    [2001](https://journals.openedition.org/cemoti/695), abstract; the same abstract says
    suppression in Yi and Tibetan areas ran later and differently). The brief's "20 million addicts"
    I could not source. Bears on will: the most embedded drug problem in history, in a field crop,
    ended twice by states willing to use mass coercion and revived once when the state weakened.
16. **Seventeenth-century tobacco bans. Snippets only.** Murad IV made smoking a capital offence
    around 1633; his successor lifted the ban and taxed it. Russia banned tobacco in 1634 and by
    1697 ran it as a state monopoly. James I wrote _A Counterblaste to Tobacco_ (1604) and taxed it.
    This is Courtwright's pattern (R beats H once a drug is commercialised). I did not open a
    scholarly source.
17. **Bhutan's tobacco sales ban, 2004. Verified in part.** Sale banned in 2004. Youth tobacco use
    rose from 24% in 2006 to 30% in 2013; seized smuggled tobacco rose from 130,000 ngultrum in 2006
    to 1.4 million in 2008; during COVID, Bhutan "temporarily lifted the decade-old ban to temper
    demand"
    ([TalkingDrugs, 2022](https://www.talkingdrugs.org/what-can-new-zealand-learn-from-bhutans-tobacco-ban)).
    The legal detail of the 2021 change I did not verify. Bears on C and E: a small state with an
    open border to India could not hold a ban on an embedded drug.
18. **New Zealand's generational smoking ban. Verified.** The law would have banned sale "to anyone
    born after 1 January 2009", cut outlets "from 6000 to 600" and removed 95% of nicotine. It was
    "repealed under urgency" on 28 February 2024 before any of it took effect. Opposition leader:
    "an acceptable way to fund tax cuts"
    ([RNZ, 2024](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/political/510439/smokefree-generation-law-scrapped-by-coalition-government)).
    The UK has since passed the same policy: Royal Assent 29 April 2026, in force 1 January 2027
    (snippet). Bears on will and R: no capability was ever tested.
19. **Khat in the UK. Verified.** Legal until June 2014, used almost entirely by East African
    immigrants (unverified; a 2005 Home Office figure has more than half of Somali men in the UK
    using it, snippet). The Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs "recommended that khat should
    not be controlled"; the Home Secretary banned it anyway. Stated reasons: "The whole of northern
    Europe – most recently the Netherlands – and the majority of other EU member states have
    controlled khat", and without a ban the UK risked "becoming a single, regional hub for the
    illegal onward trafficking of khat"
    ([Home Office written statement, 2013](https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/khat)). Nutt
    score 9. Bears on who-the-users-are: an embedded drug, low harm, banned without difficulty
    because the people it was embedded among were few and not the electorate.
20. **Coca in Bolivia. Verified.** Bolivia left the 1961 Single Convention on 1 January 2012 and
    rejoined on 10 February 2013 with a reservation permitting coca chewing. Blocking it needed 61
    objecting states; 15 objected
    ([UNODC, 2013](https://unodc.org/unodc/en/frontpage/2013/January/bolivia-to-re-accede-to-un-drug-convention-while-making-exception-on-coca-leaf-chewing.html)).
    Bears on C: the mirror image of khat. Where the users are the electorate, the embedded drug gets
    its exemption even against a treaty.
21. **Kratom in Thailand, 1943. Verified.** Thailand banned kratom because "Revenues collected by
    the State on opium, legal at that time under a State monopoly, started to decline when a growing
    number of users switched from opium to kratom... The Kratom Act was then introduced... to stem
    that movement and suppress competition in the opium market"
    ([Transnational Institute](https://tni.org/es/node/13546)). Bears on R: a traditional, milder
    drug banned to protect the tax base of a harder one.
22. **Kratom in the US, 2016. Verified.** DEA announced emergency Schedule I placement in August and
    on 12 October, "in response to public concern... withdrew its notice of intent"
    ([CRS, 2016](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN10593.html)). Not federally scheduled
    since, as far as I know (unverified). Bears on will: a small organised user constituency was
    enough, once.
23. Kava, betel, alcohol under Islamic law: not researched. Caulkins et al. (2011) note in passing
    that "alcohol prohibition in contemporary Saudi Arabia" does not produce American-style gang
    violence.

### Same drug, different legal treatment

24. **Crack versus powder cocaine. Verified.** From 1986 to 2010 five grams of crack triggered the
    five-year federal mandatory minimum that took 500 grams of powder; the Fair Sentencing Act of
    2010 cut the ratio from 100:1 to 18:1
    ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Sentencing_Act)). The US Sentencing Commission
    said in 2002 that the ratio rested on "a misperception of the dangers of crack cocaine"
    (secondhand, same page). Same molecule, so H, E and C are held fixed; what differs is who used
    it and how visibly. Caveat: Black members of Congress supported the 1986 Act (unverified), and
    Stuntz's "Race, Class, and Drugs" (Columbia Law Review, 1998;
    [unofficial copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/408984510/Stunt-z-1998)) argues the mechanism
    is class and policing cost, not animus: "Downscale markets tend to be much easier for the police
    to penetrate than upscale markets" (snippet).
25. **Portugal, 2001. Verified from the abstract.** Decriminalised possession of all drugs on 1
    July 2001. "contrary to predictions, the Portuguese decriminalization did not lead to major
    increases in drug use. Indeed, evidence indicates reductions in problematic use, drug-related
    harms and criminal justice overcrowding" (Hughes and Stevens,
    [British Journal of Criminology, 2010](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/29910/)). Bears on the user side:
    removing criminal penalties for users changed use little.
26. **Dutch coffeeshops, 1976 onward. Verified from the abstract.** Use "rose and fell as the number
    of coffeeshops increased and later declined, but only modestly"; no evidence of escalation;
    Dutch use "very typical for Europe" (MacCoun, "What can we learn from the Dutch cannabis
    coffeeshop system?",
    [Addiction, 2011](https://www.tni.org/en/publication/what-can-we-learn-from-the-dutch-cannabis-coffeeshop-system)).
    Prices stayed high because production remained illegal (snippet). Bears on the same point from
    the retail side.
27. **US state cannabis legalisation.** Washington's retail price fell 67% in three years (snippet,
    RAND-linked analysis). Not otherwise researched. Bears on capability: prohibition was holding
    the price up, so enforcement was doing something even while failing to stop use.
28. **Oregon Measure 110, February 2021 to September 2024. Snippet only.** Zoorob et al.
    ([JAMA Network Open, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11378001)): after adjusting
    for the arrival of fentanyl, "no association between M110 and fatal overdose rates was
    observed"; an earlier Journal of Health Economics paper had estimated +23%. The legislature
    recriminalised possession in March 2024 regardless. Bears on will: repeal tracked visible
    disorder, not measured effect.

### Novel substances with no culture and no history

29. **UK Psychoactive Substances Act 2016. Verified.** The Act bans supply of anything psychoactive,
    then exempts by name: "Food, alcohol, nicotine, caffeine, medicine and illegal drugs do not
    count" ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/penalties-drug-possession-dealing)). Possession is not an
    offence outside prison. Results from the
    [2018 review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5beec777e5274a2b0fd695db/Review_of_the_Psychoactive_Substances_Act__2016___web_.pdf):
    "31 head shops had been shut down, and 332 retailers had been identified as no longer selling";
    use among 16–24-year-olds fell from 2.6% (162,000 people) in 2015/16 to 1.2% (75,000) in
    2016/17; ending the "cat and mouse" of new compounds "does not appear to have been achieved";
    "street dealers" became the main source for synthetic cannabinoids, with "continued high
    levels... among the homeless and prison populations". Bears on all four at once: with C and X
    stripped out, a ban on open retail roughly halved casual use at trivial enforcement cost (492
    arrests), and did nothing for the heaviest users. The exemption list is the cleanest statement
    anywhere of what C means in law.
30. **MDMA, 1985–88. Verified.** DEA emergency-scheduled MDMA on 28 May 1985, "the first time that
    DEA had attempted to emergency schedule any drug". Its administrative law judge, Francis Young,
    recommended Schedule III in May 1986. The Administrator "overruled the judge's recommendations,
    and placed MDMA in Schedule I". A federal appeals court ruled for Lester Grinspoon in 1987,
    finding DEA's definition of accepted medical use too narrow; DEA rescheduled it in Schedule I on
    22 February 1988 with a new rationale
    ([MAPS](https://maps.org/research-archive/dea-mdma/introhearings.html), an interested party).
    Bears on X: the agency's own fact-finder lost.
31. **Nitrous oxide, UK, 2023. Verified.** "While the ACMD's review concluded that the overall harms
    were insufficient to merit control, the government felt that the considerations outlined above
    justified the control" (Home Office circular). The ban was announced in an Anti-Social Behaviour
    Action Plan. Bears on X.
32. Mephedrone, synthetic cannabinoids, analogue-law cat-and-mouse, the poppers exemption: covered
    only as far as items 5 and 29. See Leads.

## Existing theories of where the line sits

- **Courtwright, _Forces of Habit_ (2001).** The closest thing to a direct answer. As summarised by
  Barr (2001), whom I read, not the book: alcohol "has survived all attempts at prohibition...
  principally because of the economic importance of the drinks industry and the essential
  contribution of alcohol taxes to national economies"; Prohibition "was repealed in 1933 because,
  in the depths of the depression, the government needed the revenues". Beneath that: "Alcohol is
  legal because it is consumed, on the whole, by respectable, law-abiding citizens. Opiates,
  cocaine, and marijuana are illegal because their users are not". And Courtwright's own sentence,
  quoted by Barr: "prejudice alone did not cause the bans", but "the smaller and lower-status the
  target population, the easier it is to enact such legislation—and the easier it is to keep it in
  place." Mapping: R and C for the legal three; who-the-users-are for the rest; H as the stated
  reason; E hardly at all. The brief's further elements (timing of global commercialisation, elite
  use) I could not confirm from the review.
- **Musto, _The American Disease_ (1973).** Early US drug law as a product of "narcotic abuse and
  addiction, diplomatic maneuvering, muckraking journalism, racial anxieties, pharmaceutical and
  medical lobbying, and moral entrepreneurship"
  ([Points, 2013](https://www.pointshistory.org/post/the-american-disease-turns-forty), a
  retrospective). Also a cycle thesis: alternating periods of tolerance and intolerance (snippet).
  Mapping: mostly X, with H real at the start (there was an addiction problem). Not opened.
- **The disliked-minority account** (Chinese and opium, Black Americans and cocaine, Mexicans and
  cannabis). For: San Francisco's 1875 opium-den ordinance is where Baum starts American drug law;
  Courtwright accepts the association for all three (Barr); khat in 2014 and crack in 1986 fit
  without strain; the pattern extends to alcohol, banned when it was associated with "wine-drinking
  immigrants from southern and eastern Europe" (Barr). Against: Courtwright's "prejudice alone did
  not cause the bans"; Musto's account is multi-causal; Mexico banned cannabis itself in 1920, and
  Campos, _Home Grown_
  ([2012](https://revista.drclas.harvard.edu/a-review-of-home-grown-marijuana-and-the-origins-of-mexicos-war-on-drugs)),
  argues "reefer madness" ideas originated in Mexico and travelled north (snippet); China ran the
  harshest anti-opium campaigns of all, against its own majority; and countries with none of these
  minorities adopted the same bans by treaty. Mapping: it is a theory about C seen from the other
  side. It explains _which_ drugs were easy to ban better than _why_ anyone wanted to.
- **Caulkins, Reuter and Coulson (2011); Kleiman, "Enforcement swamping" (1993).** The
  existing-prevalence theory, and the one that matters most for the post. Verbatim: "it is much
  easier to maintain a prohibition against a rarely used substance than to impose a new prohibition
  on a substance that is already widely used. Enforcing prohibition of phencyclidine (PCP), GHB, and
  lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) is not overly onerous for the simple reason that there are
  relatively few sellers or users of those drugs. In contrast, the tens of millions of existing
  alcohol users would make establishing a prohibition on alcohol tremendously costly." The mechanism
  is Kleiman's: "increases in market size reduc[e] the individual's risk of arrests", so "black
  markets have multiple stable equilibria" and "illegal use can stabilize at low or high levels".
  Mapping: this turns C into arithmetic. Enforcement capacity is roughly fixed, so capability per
  violator is the number that matters.
- **MacCoun and Reuter, _Drug War Heresies_ (2001).** Compares regimes across vices, times and
  places; total harm is prevalence times intensity times harm per unit, and regimes trade these off;
  commercialisation, not depenalisation, is what raises use
  ([Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511754272), link and summary
  unverified; book not opened). The Dutch paper above is the same author applying it.
- **Kleiman, _Against Excess_ (1992).** Drug-by-drug policy between prohibition and free markets;
  alcohol and tobacco under-controlled, cannabis over-controlled (unverified; not opened, no link
  checked).
- **Becker, Murphy and Grossman, "The Market for Illegal Goods: The Case of Drugs" (Journal of
  Political Economy, 2006).**
  [Working paper, 2004](https://mgrossman.ws.gc.cuny.edu/files/2017/06/w10976.pdf): "when demand is
  inelastic, it does not pay to enforce any prohibition unless the social value is negative and not
  merely less than the private value", and "a monetary tax on a legal good could cause a greater
  reduction in output and increase in price than would optimal enforcement". Mapping: a normative
  theory, but it explains R positively. A state that can tax a drug has a cheaper instrument than a
  state that bans it, which is why commercialised drugs end up taxed.
- **A fifth factor.** Yes, the literature supports R. Courtwright puts it first for alcohol. The
  cases in this file where revenue or an incumbent industry decided the outcome: US repeal in 1933;
  the tobacco monopolies; Thailand banning kratom to protect opium revenue; Iceland's wine exemption
  for the fish trade; New Zealand's repeal; every Schedule II–IV drug with a pharmaceutical maker;
  and methaqualone, bannable because its industry was one firm.

## Bearing on the post

Everything here is mine, from the evidence above.

1. **The brief's guess is mostly right, with two amendments.** E explains almost none of what the
   law says and a good deal of whether it works. Amendment one: E's effect on outcomes is real but
   short. Every measured chokepoint shock decayed in 4 to 24 months once producers found another
   precursor or another country (methamphetamine three times, MDMA, LSD partly), and the permanent
   wins needed a supply base of a few factories (methaqualone) or an island (Australia). Amendment
   two: E is not the main determinant of whether a ban works either. The bans that worked against
   easy-to-make, deeply embedded drugs (China 1950–52, Japan 1954–58, the Taliban twice, Gorbachev
   for three years, Prohibition partially) worked through mass coercion of users and growers, not
   through chokepoints.
2. **Cases where the state had the capability and lacked the will.** Prohibition (cut drinking,
   repealed for revenue and legitimacy, enforcement arm never funded). Gorbachev (about 400,000
   fewer deaths a year, abandoned). New Zealand (repealed before it started). Kratom 2016 (withdrawn
   after public comment). Federal cannabis (in the other file). Oregon in reverse: the will to
   decriminalise lasted three and a half years. These support the draft.
3. **Cases where capability decided it.** Methaqualone, the 1995 methamphetamine shock, the
   Australian heroin shortage, China's 2019 fentanyl scheduling and the 2025 fall in deaths. Here
   will was constant and the outcome moved when a lever appeared. And the reverse: fentanyl exists
   as a street drug because it has no lever. These cut against the draft's sentence as written.
4. **The distinction that reconciles them.** The state has two different capabilities and the draft
   treats them as one. The capability to _punish_ is abundant: about 1.5 million US drug arrests
   in 2019. The capability to _suppress a market_ is scarce and exists only at chokepoints, or where
   the state will coerce a large share of users. "Not bottlenecked on enforcement capability" is
   true of the first and false of the second. A lie detector or a mind reader is a tool of the first
   kind. It finds violators; it does not close markets.
5. **"Will of the people" versus "who the users are".** Who the users are predicts better. Stated
   precisely, using Caulkins and Kleiman: what protects a law-breaker is the number of fellow
   law-breakers and their standing. Alcohol in 1933, cannabis after 2012, kratom in 2016 and coca in
   Bolivia were protected because the users were numerous, or organised, or the electorate. Khat
   users in 2014, crack users in 1986, opium smokers in 1909 and synthetic-cannabinoid users in 2016
   were not. "The will of the people" protects majorities. The draft's own red-light-camera and
   cannabis examples are majority cases, and the other research file already found that split (its
   section "Where the evidence disagrees with the draft", point 2). The drug evidence confirms it
   from a second direction, and Courtwright's sentence is the one-line version.
6. **Users at home versus dealers.** The premise needs adjusting first: users are who gets arrested
   (86.7% possession). What users at home are spared is prison and being found. Three reasons fit
   the evidence better than "we only let power go after power". (a) Cost: police find drug crime
   where it is cheap to see, in public and in street markets (Stuntz 1998; the New York "public
   view" arrests in the other file). The home is expensive to enter, legally and practically. (b)
   Numbers: users outnumber dealers by orders of magnitude, so the same enforcement budget is a real
   threat to dealers and a negligible one to each user (Kleiman). (c) Standing: sellers are few and
   easy to condemn, so legislatures set the long sentences there. Japan in 1954 shows this is a
   choice. Two-thirds of its arrests were for possession and use, and the epidemic ended. Western
   democracies make that choice only against small or low-status user populations.
7. **What this does to the lie-detector argument.** The drug record says the binding constraint on
   enforcing an unpopular law against a _majority_ is political, which helps the draft. It also says
   that for laws against _minorities_ the constraint has been the cost of finding and processing
   violators, and that when that cost fell (street markets, stop-and-frisk, precursor logbooks)
   enforcement rose without anyone voting for it. A cheap detector lowers exactly that cost. I found
   no example in the drug record of a detection capability that went unused against a group too
   small to object.
