# Would Lie Detectors Be Good: the "What if my mom could do it whenever?" section

Compiled 2026-11-11 by one research agent, for the section of `FINAL_POST.md` headed "What is my mom
could do it whenever?" and its six bullets. Mainly US teenagers; other rich countries where the data
is better (England for independent mobility, the Netherlands and Sweden for the monitoring
literature).

Marking:

- **No mark**: I opened the page, PDF or abstract and the claim is on it. For PubMed and OpenAlex
  records I read the abstract only, not the paper, and say "abstract". Where the page came through a
  fetch tool that summarises (Pew 2016, Pew 2020, Pew March 2024, the Mott 2026 report, NCHS, Child
  Trends, Kannan and Veazie, Kontou et al., MMWR 2002, the Life360 SEC filing, Travelwest, Bisnow),
  numbers are as the tool reported them and quotes are short.
- **(snippet)**: from a search engine's summary of the linked page; page not opened.
- **(secondhand)**: a source I opened, reporting someone else's claim.
- **(unverified)**: my recollection or the brief's; no page found. Treat as a lead.
- **(mine)**: my inference from the evidence, not a sourced claim.

## Where the bullets are wrong or unsupported

1. **"I do feel bad for the kids whose parents track their location, but not that many do it?"**
   True in 2014–15, false by 2018. About half of US parents of teenagers track location now, and
   half of parents keep doing it after the kid turns 18.
   - 2014–15, national: "16% use monitoring tools on their teen's cellphone to track their location"
     (parents of 13–17-year-olds;
     [Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2016/01/07/parents-teens-and-digital-monitoring/)).
   - 2018, one US state: 51% of parents and 50% of adolescents (mean age 15) reported digital
     location tracking ([Burnell et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10238636/)).
   - February 2026, national: "About half (52%) of parents report tracking their young adult child's
     location using a cell phone or similar device", for children aged 18–25
     ([Mott Poll, 2026](https://mottpoll.org/reports/safety-or-surveillance-tracking-young-adults)).
   - There is no national figure for 13–17-year-olds after Pew's 2016 report. Section 3 has the full
     series.

2. **"parents are well less surveilling than one would expect?"** Depends on the tool, and the split
   is the useful finding.
   - Reading the phone has not moved in a decade: 48% of parents of teens had looked through call
     records or messages in 2014–15 (Pew 2016), 50% had looked through the teen's phone in 2023
     ([Pew, 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/03/11/how-teens-and-parents-approach-screen-time/)).
   - Location tracking roughly tripled over the same decade (bullet 1).
   - Schools do more of it than parents: 88% of teachers said their school monitors students' online
     activity in 2022–23
     ([CDT, 2023](https://cdt.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/091823-CDT-Off-Task-Summary-web.pdf)).
   - So "less than one would expect" holds for content snooping and fails for location and for
     schools (mine).

3. **"they have sex less than they used to"** True on the headline survey, with three caveats that
   change how it can be used.
   - It happened in two steps with a flat decade between: 54% of high schoolers had ever had sex in
     1991, a 16% relative fall by 2001, 47% in 2013, 32% in 2023 (section 1).
   - The post-2019 part is partly a survey artefact. YRBS overall response fell from 71% (2011) to
     35% (2023), missing data on the sex item rose from 7.0% to 19.8–29.5%, and the 2021 sample was
     younger; an estimated "50% of the change in sexual experience among female students between
     2019 and 2021 and 30% of the change for male students was due to a change in the age
     distribution"
     ([Santelli et al., 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13178-025-01223-3),
     abstract).
   - The other national survey (NSFG, ages 15–19, 2006–2019) finds "declines in male adolescent
     sexual experience" only
     ([Lindberg, Firestein and Beavin, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conx.2021.100064), abstract).
     The teen birth rate is not a clean cross-check, because its fall since 2007 is attributed to
     contraception (section 1).

4. **"This is the phones"** is stated as settled. It is the live dispute in the field, and most of
   what the first two bullets describe started before teenagers had smartphones.
   - 37% of US teens had a smartphone in 2012
     ([Pew, 2013](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2013/03/13/teens-and-technology-2013/)), 73%
     in 2014–15 and 95% in 2018
     ([Pew, 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2018/05/31/teens-social-media-technology-2018/)).
   - Licensing at 16 fell from 46.2% (1983) to 31.1% (2008). Teen summer employment fell from over
     half (2000) to 29.6% (2010–11). "Ever had sex" fell from 54% (1991) to about 46% (2001). Teen
     births have fallen since 1991. All before 2012 (section 6).
   - What does line up with 2012 is loneliness, and a faster fall in seeing friends in person
     (section 2).

5. **"more lonely?"** Yes after 2012, but teen loneliness was falling before that, so it is a
   reversal and not a long trend. Twenge on the 12th-grade item, asked since 1977: "loneliness
   declining among teens before suddenly increasing"
   ([The Conversation, 2019](https://theconversation.com/teens-have-less-face-time-with-their-friends-and-are-lonelier-than-ever-113240)).
   "Party less" is a different shape: in-person time "has declined since the 1970s, the drop
   accelerated after 2010" (same). The two halves of the bullet have different start dates.

6. **"Not in tech-enabled ways mostly in raw rules!"** Half supported, and the supported half is
   about younger children.
   - The loss of physical freedom is real, large and old, but nearly all the trend data is for
     under-12s (walking to school, going out alone).
   - For high schoolers I found no trend data at all on parental rules (curfew times, distance
     limits). CDC first asked its one "parental monitoring" question in 2021 (section 4).
   - What is documented for teens is new legal and institutional restriction (drinking age,
     graduated licences, city curfews, school cameras, mall escort policies), plus things nobody
     forbids that teens stopped doing (section 5).
   - "Not tech" is also wrong on its face now, given bullet 1.

## Read these first

1. Davis, Thornburg, Patel and Pelham,
   ["Digital Location Tracking of Children and Adolescents: A Theoretical Framework and Review"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11843666/),
   _Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review_, 2024, 23 pages, free. The only review of location
   tracking. Has the prevalence series, the list of ways teens get round it, and the admission that
   no study has followed tracked kids over time.
2. Stattin and Kerr,
   ["Parental Monitoring: A Reinterpretation"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11016567/), _Child
   Development_, 2000, 14 pages; with Kerr, Stattin and Burk,
   ["A Reinterpretation of Parental Monitoring in Longitudinal Perspective"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1532-7795.2009.00623.x),
   _Journal of Research on Adolescence_, 2010, 26 pages. The disclosure-versus-surveillance result,
   cross-sectional and then longitudinal. I read abstracts only.
3. Hawk et al.,
   ["'I still haven't found what I'm looking for': parental privacy invasion predicts reduced parental knowledge"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22889388/),
   _Developmental Psychology_, 2013, 13 pages. The closest thing to data on non-consensual mind
   reading: snooping leaves parents knowing less a year later.
4. Mott Poll,
   ["Safety or surveillance: Tracking of young adults"](https://mottpoll.org/reports/safety-or-surveillance-tracking-young-adults),
   June 2026, a two-page report. The newest national number, and the one with the consent details
   (who was given an opt-out).
5. Pew,
   ["Parents, Teens and Digital Monitoring"](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2016/01/07/parents-teens-and-digital-monitoring/),
   2016, web report. The baseline for every monitoring behaviour, from before tracking apps spread.
6. Twenge and Park,
   ["The Decline in Adult Activities Among U.S. Adolescents, 1976–2016"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925063/),
   _Child Development_, 2019 (online 2017), 17 pages. Shows the fall covers things parents want
   (jobs, driving) as well as things they forbid, which is the best evidence on "rules or just

   [Scientific American write-up (2017)](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/extended-adolescence-when-25-is-the-new-181/).

   ["The great rewiring: is social media really behind an epidemic of teenage mental illness?"](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00902-2),
   _Nature_, 2024, a short book review. The standard statement of the case against "it's the
   phones". I read a
   [course excerpt](https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-11/Odgers_Nature_2024.pdf)
   of about 450 words, not the full review.
8. Shaw et al.,
   ["Children's independent mobility: a comparative study in England and Germany (1971–2010)"](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:89103856-a239-489a-8e7e-b6c1bad43a0f),
   Policy Studies Institute, 2013, report (length not checked). The 40-year series on what children
   are allowed to do alone. I read the abstract.

| 2013 | 47%          | 34%                       | [CDC, YRBS Data Summary & Trends 2013–2023 (2024)](https://www.cdc.gov/yrbs/dstr/pdf/YRBS-2023-Data-Summary-Trend-Report.pdf) |

| 2023 | 32%          | 21%                       | same                                                                                                                          |

- 1991–2001: "the prevalence of sexual experience decreased 16% among high school students" and "the
  overall prevalence of current sexual activity did not change"
  ([CDC, MMWR, 2002](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm5138a2.htm)). A 16% relative fall
  from 54% is about 46% (mine). The year-by-year table on that page is an image and I could not read
  it.
- So the shape is: down eight points in the 1990s, flat from 2001 to 2013 (46% to 47%), down fifteen
  points from 2013 to 2023 (mine, from the rows above). The second drop is the one that coincides
  with smartphones.
- CDC's own summary of the last two years: "There were no significant changes in any of the sexual
  behaviors included in this report between 2021 and 2023" (YRBS 2013–2023 report).
- Four or more lifetime partners: 15% (2013), 6% (2023). Condom use at last sex among the sexually
  active: 59% (2013), 52% (2023) (same report).

**Why the recent YRBS numbers are shaky.** Santelli, Malden, Moss, Finkelstein and Lindberg, "Age
Bias, Missing Data, and Declining Response Rates in the National Youth Risk Behavior Survey",
_Sexuality Research and Social Policy_,
[2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13178-025-01223-3) (abstract):

- "From 2011 to 2023, school and student survey response rates declined (school 81% to 40%, student
  87% to 71%, and overall, 71% to 35%)."
- "Missing data on ever had sex increased over time from 7.0% in 2011 to 29.5% in 2019 and 19.8% in
  2023."
- They conclude the changes "raise serious concerns about the validity of trends in the YRBS after
  2019".

**The other national survey disagrees in part.** National Survey of Family Growth, ages 15–19,
periods 2006–2010, 2011–2015 and 2015–2019: "Males reported declines in sexual behaviors with a
partner of a different sex", and the conclusions list "declines in male adolescent sexual
experience, increased contraceptive use overall, and declines in consistent condom use"
([Lindberg, Firestein and Beavin, _Contraception: X_, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conx.2021.100064),
abstract). No decline is reported for females.

**Teen birth rate (births per 1,000 females aged 15–19).** From the Congressional Research Service,
["Teen Births in the United States: Overview and Recent Trends", R45184, 2 September 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-02_R45184_622c4af2dbd649b2fb763ba222b7cdc26b5a06be.pdf):

- 96.3 in 1957 (peak), 50.2 in 1986, 61.8 in 1991, 12.6 in 2024. "From 1992 onward, the teen birth
  rate declined except in two years, 2006 and 2007. From 2007 to 2024, the rate declined by 69.6%,
  to a historical low in 2024 of 12.6."
- NCHS confirms the last step: "The birth rate for teenagers ages 15–19 declined 4% to 12.6 births
  per 1,000 females from 13.1 in 2023"
  ([NCHS Data Brief 535, 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db535.htm)). The 2025
  provisional rate is 11.7 (snippet).
- Why it fell, per CRS: delayed first sex "accounted for 23% of the decline in teen pregnancy among
  younger teens" in 1995–2002, but "More recent data indicate that trends in adolescent sexual
  activity have not changed significantly, but rather that decreases in the teen birth rate are
  attributed to an increased use of contraception."
- So the birth rate confirms less sex for the 1990s and says little about it after 2007 (mine).

## 2. "they party less and are more lonely?" and "This is the phones"

**Seeing friends, parties, going out.**

- Twenge, Spitzberg and Campbell, "Less in-person social interaction with peers among U.S.
  adolescents in the 21st century and links to loneliness", _Journal of Social and Personal
  Relationships_, [2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407519836170) (abstract): Monitoring the Future
  and American Freshman surveys, 1976–2017, N = 8.2 million. Adolescents in the 2010s "spent less
  time on in-person (face-to-face) social interaction with peers, including getting together or
  socializing with friends, going to parties, going out, dating, going to movies, and riding in cars
  for fun." "College-bound high school seniors in 2016 (vs. the late 1980s) spent an hour less a day
  engaging in in-person social interaction, despite declines in paid work and little change in
  homework or extracurricular activity time."
- Twenge's own summary of the same paper
  ([The Conversation, 2019](https://theconversation.com/teens-have-less-face-time-with-their-friends-and-are-lonelier-than-ever-113240)):
  "In the late 1970s, 52 percent of 12th-graders got together with their friends almost every day.
  By 2017, only 28 percent did. The drop was especially pronounced after 2010." "Today's
  10th-graders go to about 17 fewer parties a year than 10th-graders in the 1980s did."
- The same abstract has a finding that cuts against the simple phone story: displacement "occurs at
  the cohort level, with in-person social interaction declining as digital media use increased, but
  not at the individual level, where in-person social interaction and social media use are
  positively correlated." The teens who use social media most also see friends most.
- American Time Use Survey, Kannan and Veazie, "US trends in social isolation, social engagement,
  and companionship", _SSM – Population Health_,
  [2023 (online 2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9811250): "Social engagement with
  friends, others, and in companionship plummeted for young Americans." All ages, time with friends
  fell from 60 minutes a day (2003) to 34 (2019) and 20 (2020). For ages 15–24 the 2003–2019 mean
  was 115 minutes a day and the change across the period was −62 minutes a day; time alone rose 31
  minutes a day. National breakpoints at 2007, 2013 and 2019 (as the fetch tool reported the tables;
  the age-group year values are in a supplement I did not open).

**Alcohol.**

- YRBS, high schoolers who currently drank alcohol: 35% (2013), 33%, 30%, 29%, 23%, 22% (2023) (YRBS
  2013–2023 report).
- Monitoring the Future, 12th graders, any alcohol in the past 12 months: 64% in 2011
  ([Patrick and Schulenberg, _Alcohol Research: Current Reviews_, c. 2013](https://arcr.niaaa.nih.gov/media/816/download)),
  45.7% in 2023 and 41.7% in 2024
  ([NIDA, 2024](https://nida.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/2024/12/reported-use-of-most-drugs-among-adolescents-remained-low-in-2024)).
  75% in 1997 (snippet). 51% of 12th graders in 2024 had never drunk alcohol (snippet).
- Patrick and Schulenberg date the decline to the survey's start: use "among these age groups
  overall has been declining since 1975", and they credit "changes in the minimum legal drinking
  age" and "perceived social norms". The year-by-year MTF tables were blocked; I could not confirm
  the usual "peaked around 1979" figure (unverified).

**Adult activities generally.** Twenge and Park, [2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925063/)
(abstract): seven national surveys, 1976–2016, N = 8.44 million, ages 13–19. "fewer adolescents in
recent years engaged in adult activities such as having sex, dating, drinking alcohol, working for
pay, going out without their parents, and driving". "The trends are unlikely to be due to homework
and extracurricular time, which stayed steady or declined, and may or may not be linked to increased
Internet use." The press release: "In terms of adult activities, 18-year-olds now look like
15-year-olds once did." Scientific American (2017): "since around 2000, teens have become
considerably less likely to drive, have an after-school job and date", and "the dip in adult
activities began before internet usage became common."

- Dating figures (about 85% of 12th graders ever dated for earlier cohorts, 56–63% by 2015) are from
  Twenge's popular writing; I could not open a source (unverified).

**Driving.** Share of the age group holding a licence, from Federal Highway Administration data
([Sivak and Schoettle, UMTRI-2016-4, 2016](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~umtriswt/PDF/UMTRI-2016-4.pdf)):

| Age | 1983  | 2008  | 2011  | 2014  |
| --- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| 16  | 46.2% | 31.1% | 27.5% | 24.5% |
| 17  | 68.9% | 50.0% | 45.0% | 44.9% |
| 18  | 80.4% | 65.4% | 60.3% | 60.1% |

Two thirds of the fall for 16-year-olds had happened by 2008. I found nothing later than 2014.

**Jobs.** Share of 16–19-year-olds employed for at least part of the summer
([Pew, August 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/08/19/after-a-long-fade-is-teen-summer-employment-in-the-us-rebounding/)):
"At the peak in 1978, 58% of teens worked for at least part of the summer." "As recently as 2000,
more than half of 16- to 19-year-olds worked for at least part of the summer." "The low came during
the summers of 2010 and 2011, when just 29.6% of teens". 30.8% in 2020, 33.8% in 2025, 35.5%
in 2026. The whole fall happened between 2000 and 2010; it has been flat to slightly up in the
smartphone era.

**Loneliness.**

- Monitoring the Future, 12th graders: "39 percent said they often felt lonely in 2017, up from 26
  percent in 2012. Thirty-eight percent said they often felt left out in 2017, up from 30 percent
  in 2012. In both cases, the 2017 numbers were all-time highs since the questions were first asked
  in 1977" (Twenge, The Conversation, 2019). The paper's abstract: "Adolescents' feelings of
  loneliness increased sharply after 2011."
- PISA, 15- and 16-year-olds, 37 countries, n = 1,049,784: "School loneliness increased 2012-2018 in
  36 out of 37 countries. Worldwide, nearly twice as many adolescents in 2018 (vs. 2012) had
  elevated levels of school loneliness." "school loneliness was high when smartphone access and
  internet use were high. In contrast, higher unemployment rates predicted lower school loneliness."
  Conclusion: "though causation cannot be proven"
  ([Twenge, Haidt, Blake, McAllister, Lemon and Le Roy, _Journal of Adolescence_, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34294429/),
  abstract).

**Where the phones debate stands.**

- For. Twenge, Martin and Campbell, _Emotion_, [2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000403)
  (abstract): US adolescents' "psychological well-being (measured by self-esteem, life satisfaction,
  and happiness) suddenly decreased after 2012", "which may instead be at least partially due to the
  rapid adoption of smartphones and the subsequent shift in adolescents' time use." This is the
  argument of Twenge's _iGen_ (2017), which I did not open.
- For. Haidt, _The Anxious Generation_ (2024). The book's own page says it "documents how the rise
  of smartphones and social media, as well as the decline of independence and free play, rapidly
  rewired childhood and caused a global mental health crisis for kids and teens"
  ([anxiousgeneration.com](https://www.anxiousgeneration.com/book)). Haidt's thesis has two causes,
  and the second is the one in bullet 6. I did not open the book.
- Against. Odgers, _Nature_, 2024: "the book's repeated suggestion that digital technologies are
  rewiring our children's brains and causing an epidemic of mental illness is not supported by
  science." "Hundreds of researchers, myself included, have searched for the kind of large effects
  suggested by Haidt. Our efforts have produced a mix of no, small and mixed associations. Most data
  are correlative." Where effects over time are found, "they suggest not that social-media use
  predicts or causes depression, but that young people who already have mental-health problems use
  such platforms more often". "An analysis done in 72 countries shows no consistent or measurable
  associations between well-being and the roll-out of social media globally".
- Against. Orben and Przybylski, _Nature Human Behaviour_,
  [2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30944443/) (abstract): three datasets, n = 355,358. "The
  association we find between digital technology use and adolescent well-being is negative but
  small, explaining at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being."
- State of play (mine): both sides accept the post-2012 turn in loneliness and mood, and both accept
  that teens see each other less. They disagree on whether phones caused it. Neither side attributes
  anything to parental surveillance; Haidt's second cause is parental restriction of physical
  freedom, which is a rule and not a capability.

| Feb 2026     | US parents of 18–25s, national (n = 1,542)      | 52%                                        | [Mott Poll, 2026](https://mottpoll.org/reports/safety-or-surveillance-tracking-young-adults)                                            |

- The review's summary: "DLT use has increased over time and DLT is now a common parenting tool.
  Data collected in 2008 and 2011 found only 2-12% of parents were using DLT... Between 2018 and
  2020, 33%-69% of parents of children and teens reported using DLT"
  ([Davis et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11843666/); DLT is their term for
  digital location tracking).
- The Dutch study has its own 2019 sample (n = 175) and finds parents in 2024 tracked location more
  often (β = 1.47, p < 0.001), and that "Adolescents in 2024 viewed tracking of location (β = 2.61,
  p < 0.001) and finances (β = 0.73, p = 0.010) as more acceptable than adolescents in 2019."
- Pew's young-adult wording: "One-in-four parents (25%) say they track their young adult child's
  location with GPS apps, even if only rarely. Most of the tracking is being done of 18- to
  24-year-olds. Among parents answering about a child in this age range, 42% say they track their
  location. In addition, parents are more likely to say they track their daughters than their sons
  (31% vs. 21%)."
- Caveats (mine). The Pew 2016 question asks about "monitoring tools on their teen's cellphone";
  Burnell's asks "How often does your parent monitor your location using technology or an app (e.g.,
  Find My iPhone, Find My Friends, mSpy, Life360)?" The wider wording explains some of the jump from
  16% to 51%, not most of it. Burnell's sample is one state and had drifted from representative. The
  national teen number for the 2020s does not exist; the 18–25 number (52%) is a floor for it only
  by assumption.
- Intensity. In the 2018 sample, of all parents: "15% used it only once in a while, 13% some of the
  time, 10% most of the time, and 13% always" (Davis et al., on Burnell). So about a quarter tracked
  heavily. In the 2026 Mott poll, 71% of tracking parents "say the tracking feature is always on".
- Burnell's parent–teen agreement: of 574 pairs, 36% agreed tracking happened, 37% agreed it did
  not, and in 28% one said yes and the other no (14% each way).

**Life360.** A company figure, counting every family member and not only teens. Q2 2026: 102.4
million monthly active users worldwide, 54.0 million in the US, up from 47.5 million a year earlier;
3.2 million paying "circles"
([Life360 8-K, 10 August 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001581760/000158176026000142/life360incq226mediarelea.htm)).
Earlier US figures: 20.3 million in mid-2021 and 36.8 million at the end of 2023 (snippet). 54
million is about one US resident in six (mine; population figure unverified). Life360's own surveys
(91% of "Gen Alpha" parents whose child has a phone use location sharing, February 2024) are
marketing and came only as a snippet.

**Is the tracking covert?** Mostly not.

- Mott 2026: "Nearly all parents who track their young adult child's location (96%) indicate that
  their child is aware". But "only 54% of parents say they gave their young adult child the option
  to not have them track their location" (61% for 21–25-year-olds, 47% for 18–20-year-olds).
- An Australian convenience sample (112 parents): "Of parents who used DLT, 95% reported children
  were aware of being tracked" (Mavoa et al. 2023, in Davis et al. 2024, secondhand).
- Reasons given in Mott 2026: peace of mind 68%, emergencies 64%, knowing when to call 21%, "To make
  sure the child is in approved places" 9%. And 23% of tracking parents say it "sometimes makes them
  more anxious than reassured".
- In the Dutch sample location tracking was "viewed as 'not okay' by approximately one-quarter" of
  adolescents, against large majorities accepting grade and bank tracking.

**Reading the phone, messages, browsing.** Parents of 13–17s.

- 2014–15 (Pew 2016): "61% of parents say they have ever checked which websites their teen visits."
  "60% have ever checked their teen's social media profiles." "48% have ever looked through their
  teen's phone call records or text messages."
- Sept–Oct 2023 (Pew 2024, 1,453 teens and their parents): "Half of parents say they have looked
  through their teen's phone." 64% for parents of 13–14s, 41% for 15–17s. 43% of teens think it has
  happened. 47% of parents limit phone time (62% for 13–14s, 37% for 15–17s).
- No increase in nine years.

**Passwords.** 2014–15 only: "nearly half (48%) of parents know the password to their teen's email
account", "43% know the password to their teen's cellphone", "35% know the password to at least one
of their teen's social media accounts" (Pew 2016). I found no later figure.

**Parental-control software.**

- 2014–15: "39% of parents report using parental controls for blocking, filtering or monitoring
  their teen's online activities." "16% use parental controls to restrict their teen's use of his or
  her cellphone" (Pew 2016).
- Feb–Mar 2025, 1,000 US parents of 10–17s: "51% on tablets, 47% on smartphones, 46% on desktops,
  43% on laptops, 38% on Smart TVs, and 35% on video game consoles"
  ([Family Online Safety Institute, 2025](https://fosi.org/parental-controls-for-online-safety-are-underutilized-new-study-finds/)).
  FOSI's headline is that controls are "underutilized"; the survey was run with member companies
  including TikTok.
- Phone controls went from 16% to 47% across the two surveys, with different age ranges and wording
  (mine).
- For younger children the numbers are higher: parents of 5–11s in March 2020 reported limiting
  screen time (86%), checking websites or apps (75%), parental controls (72%), looking at call
  records or texts (49%) (Pew 2020).

**School-issued devices and school monitoring.** Center for Democracy and Technology surveys of
teachers, parents and students.

- 2022–23: "the overall percentage of teachers reporting that their school monitors students' online
  activities (88 percent) is consistent with the 2021–22 school year", with "40 percent reporting
  use of the technology on personal devices". "Thirty-eight percent of teachers whose school uses
  student activity monitoring software indicate that a student was contacted by law enforcement."
  19% of students at monitored schools say they or someone they know was "outed" by it. "only 27
  percent of parents have been asked for their input on what their school filters, blocks, or
  monitors"
  ([CDT, "Off Task", 2023](https://cdt.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/091823-CDT-Off-Task-Summary-web.pdf)).
- CDT's 2022 report calls monitoring "the nearly ubiquitous practice of schools using technology to
  monitor students' activities online", which "frequently occurs outside of" school hours
  ([CDT, "Hidden Harms", 2022](https://cdt.org/insights/report-hidden-harms-the-misleading-promise-of-monitoring-students-online/)).
- Vendor scale: GoGuardian in "roughly half" of US K-12 public schools; Bark in "more than 1,100"
  school districts (both snippet). I found no opened count for Gaggle.
- This is the largest surveillance system teenagers live under, and parents neither run it nor,
  mostly, were asked (mine).

**Real-time grade portals.**

- US parents: no survey found. Portals "allow parents and often students, especially middle and high
  schoolers, to access grades and attendance data in real time"
  ([Education Week, January 2026](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/online-portals-offer-instant-access-to-grades-thats-not-always-a-good-thing/2026/01)).
- US students, 2024–25, about 21,000 high schoolers surveyed by Challenge Success: "24% do so more
  than once a day; 30%, daily; 28% said weekly; 9%, monthly; 6%, less than once a month; and 4%
  never do" (same article). The kids surveil their own grades more than daily.
- Dutch parents, 2024: "Tracking school performance was most common (96.4%), followed by financial
  activities (90.1%) and location (65.7%)"; "nearly half of the parents checked the online school
  portal several times a week" (Boele et al. 2026).

**Cameras.**

- Schools: public schools using security cameras rose from 19.4% (1999–2000) to 36.0%, 42.8%, 55.0%,
  61.1%, 75.1%, 80.6%, 83.5% and 91.1% (2019–20)
  ([NCES Digest table 233.50, 2021](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d22/tables/dt22_233.50.asp)).
- Homes: about 39% of US households have a video doorbell and 24% an indoor camera in one 2026
  survey of 1,000 adults; other surveys range from 35% to 56% for doorbells (all snippet). I found
  nothing on parents using them to watch teenagers.

**Summary of the trend (mine).** Over roughly 2015–2025: location tracking of teens tripled (16% to
about half); phone parental controls rose (16% to 47%); reading the phone stayed at half; school
monitoring reached nine schools in ten. The capability that became free and passive got used. The
capability that takes effort and feels like snooping did not grow.

## 4. "This is the phones, but it's probalby not the location tracking?"

Does parental surveillance change what kids do? The developmental literature says: mostly no, and
the old evidence that it did was measuring something else.

**The original finding.**

- Stattin and Kerr, _Child Development_, [2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11016567/)
  (abstract): "monitoring measures typically assess parents' knowledge but not its source". In 703
  14-year-olds in central Sweden and their parents, "parental knowledge came mainly from child
  disclosure, and child disclosure was the source of knowledge that was most closely linked to broad
  and narrow measures of delinquency (normbreaking and police contact)." "We conclude that tracking
  and surveillance is not the best prescription for parental behavior".
- Kerr and Stattin, _Developmental Psychology_, [2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10830980/)
  (abstract): 1,186 14-year-olds. "children's spontaneous disclosure of information explained more
  of these relations than parents' tracking and surveillance efforts did. Parents' control efforts
  were related to good adjustment only after the child's feelings of being controlled, which were
  linked to poor adjustment, were partialed out."

**Followed forward.**

- Kerr, Stattin and Burk, _Journal of Research on Adolescence_,
  [2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1532-7795.2009.00623.x) (abstract): two years, 938 seventh and
  eighth graders. "Youth disclosure was a significant longitudinal predictor of parental knowledge
  in single- and cross-rater models. Neither measure of parents' monitoring efforts—control or
  solicitation—was a significant predictor. In analyses involving delinquency, parental monitoring
  efforts did not predict changes in delinquency over time, but youth disclosure did."
- Keijsers, _International Journal of Behavioral Development_,
  [2016 (online 2015)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0165025415592515) (abstract): separating
  within-family change from differences between families, "Previously detected correlations between
  parental monitoring and adolescent delinquency were not present at the within-family level.
  However, there were significant associations between within-person fluctuations in disclosure and
  delinquency." Monitoring's apparent effect "can be explained by stable between-person differences
  rather than causal processes operating within families."
- Racz and McMahon, _Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review_,
  [2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22086648/) (abstract): a review of 47 studies from
  2000–2010, organised around "'parental monitoring' as 'parental knowledge'" and "parental
  knowledge as driven by child disclosure". It calls the 2000 papers "seminal". The abstract does
  not state the review's verdict and I did not open the paper.
- The rebuttal. Fletcher, Steinberg and Williams-Wheeler, "Parental Influences on Adolescent Problem
  Behavior: Revisiting Stattin and Kerr", _Child Development_,
  [2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2004.00706.x) (abstract): 2,568 US high school
  students. "Problem behavior was best predicted by a model that included indirect effects of
  warmth, control, and monitoring (all by way of parental knowledge), as well as direct effects of
  control and monitoring." Cross-sectional, so it does not answer the 2010 and 2016 longitudinal
  results (mine).

**Snooping specifically.**

- Hawk, Keijsers, Frijns, Hale, Branje and Meeus, _Developmental Psychology_,
  [2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22889388/) (abstract): three years, 497 Dutch adolescents
  (age 13 at start) and both parents. "Higher youth-reported invasion predicted lower father- and
  mother-reported knowledge 1 year later." "The results suggest that privacy invasion is
  counterproductive to parents' efforts to remain knowledgeable about youths, due to increased
  adolescent secrecy."
- Hawk, Becht and Branje, _Journal of Research on Adolescence_,
  [2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28581659/) (abstract): "Snooping is a relatively infrequent
  monitoring behavior, compared to solicitation and control, but appears to be a stronger indicator
  of problems in adolescent and family functioning."
- Hawk, _Journal of Adolescence_, [2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28012399/) (abstract): 455
  Chinese high school students, 60.40% reporting covert monitoring. "Solicitation positively
  predicted disclosure, while covert monitoring negatively predicted disclosure and positively
  predicted secrecy."
- Peng, Hawk and Wang, _Journal of Youth and Adolescence_,
  [2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37043036/) (abstract): 289 Chinese youth over a year,
  within-person. "stronger perceptions of parental invasion predicted later within-person decreases
  in adolescents' disclosure and increases in secrecy. Disclosure and secrecy did not predict later
  perceptions of parental invasion". The arrow runs from invasion to secrecy, not the reverse.

**Location tracking and outcomes.**

- Burnell et al. 2023 (abstract): "tracking was associated with greater externalizing problems and
  alcohol consumption; however, these associations were not robust across multiple informants and
  sensitivity analyses." Davis et al. summarise the same data as: "Frequency of DLT use had small
  and non-significant correlations with internalizing problems, externalizing problems, and
  substance use (rs = −0.01 to +0.07)".
- Boele et al. 2026 (abstract): "Digital tracking was unrelated to adolescent well‐being (e.g.,
  anxiety symptoms) but positively associated with parental control‐ and fear‐related factors (e.g.,
  overprotection, dangerous worldviews)." Tracking tells you about the parent.
- Chase et al. 2024 (abstract), 706 college students: those currently tracked "tended to perceive
  their primary parent/caregiver as engaging in more helicopter parenting and as less supportive of
  their autonomy."
- Davis et al. 2024 on the whole field: "All reviewed studies were cross-sectional". "No study has
  linked parent DLT use to prospective measurements of youth adjustment or family functioning in
  order to appraise the impacts of DLT." Twenty-four articles in total, seven of them quantitative.

**Evasion.** No prevalence data; the review lists the methods.

- "youth can leave the device somewhere they are not, such as at home before leaving for the night,
  or turn the device off and claim the battery died. If the device is a phone, youth could
  temporarily disable location sharing on their phone or within the app, delete the app, or log into
  another decoy device to share the wrong location" (Davis et al. 2024).
- The review's worry runs the other way too: "Incorrectly believing that DLT cannot be tricked or
  circumvented, the parent may miss signs that the teen is in fact spending more time in risky
  situations."
- Online, 2012: "Seventy-one percent had done something to hide their online behavior, such as
  clearing their Web browser history (53.3 percent) and minimizing the browser when mom or dad
  walked by (45.9 percent). The total number of kids hiding online behavior is up from 45 percent in
  a similar survey in 2010." A McAfee-commissioned survey of 1,004 teens aged 13–17, "not nationally
  representative"
  ([LiveScience, 2012](https://www.livescience.com/21156-parents-teens-online-activity.html)).
- I found no survey on second accounts ("finstas") or on leaving the phone at a friend's house
  (unverified that any exists).

**Current standing (mine, from the above).** The claim that parents' knowledge comes mostly from
what the child volunteers has held up for 25 years and across designs. The stronger claim that
parental control does nothing is contested (Fletcher et al. 2004). Practice has not caught up with
either:

- Clinical manuals still treat monitoring as "a well-established protective factor"
  ([Schwarz-Torres et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40115905/), abstract), and the same
  review notes they offer "little advice" on monitoring with technology.
- CDC's national statistic on "parental monitoring" is the knowledge question Stattin and Kerr
  criticised: students whose parents "know where they are going or with whom they will be all or
  most of the time", 86.4% in 2021
  ([CDC, MMWR, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10156152/)) and 84% in 2023 (YRBS
  2013–2023 report). The 2021 report calls it "the first national assessment of parental monitoring
  among U.S. high school students". There is no trend.

**For the consensual-versus-mind-reading distinction (mine).** This literature is the same
distinction with data: disclosure (the child chooses what to reveal) predicts good outcomes and
parental knowledge; solicitation and rules predict little; covert snooping predicts less knowledge
later. Location tracking as actually practised sits in between. It is known to the child (95–96%),
often not optional (only 54% of parents in the Mott poll gave an 18–25-year-old the option to
refuse), and passive, so it needs no disclosure at all. Whether it crowds out disclosure is the open
question the review poses: "parents could end up with a net of less knowledge when youth react
especially negatively."

## 5. "What ways do parents monitor their kids way more? ... what can high schoolers not do anymore tbh? Are there rules or are they just lame?"

**Independent mobility, England.** Policy Studies Institute surveys of 1971, 1990 and 2010.

- "In 1971, 80 per cent of seven and eight year old English children surveyed were allowed to go to
  school without adult supervision. By 1990, the figure had fallen to 9 per cent. Over the same
  period, in Britain, although the volume of traffic nearly doubled, child fatalities on the roads
  nearly halved"
  ([Shaw et al., 2013](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:89103856-a239-489a-8e7e-b6c1bad43a0f),
  abstract, reporting Hillman, Adams and Whitelegg, _One False Move_, 1990).
- The 1990 book's argument, as the 2013 abstract puts it: "children had been removed from the source
  of danger", and "parents restricted their children's independent mobility because of a fear of
  danger from traffic."
- Primary school children allowed to travel home from school alone: 86% (1971), 35% (1990), 25%
  (2010). Non-school journeys accompanied by an adult: 41% (1971), 62% (2010)
  ([Travelwest evidence summary, 2013](https://travelwest.info/essential-evidence/96-declining-independent-mobility-among-children/),
  secondhand from the 2013 PSI report).
- "in 1971 in England, 55 per cent of children under 10 were allowed to travel alone to places other
  than school that were within walking distance; by 2010, almost no children under 10 were allowed
  to do so"
  ([Shaw et al., 2015](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/98xyq/children-s-independent-mobility-an-international-comparison-and-recommendations-for-action),
  abstract). That report covers 16 countries and children aged 7 to 15; England ranked seventh
  (snippet).
- The big fall is 1971–1990. Mobile phones are irrelevant to it.

**Walking to school, US.** National Household Travel Survey.

- "13 percent of children five to 14 years old usually walked or biked to school compared with 48
  percent of students in 1969", and the share "has remained stable at about 12 percent over the last
  15 years"
  ([Safe Routes to School National Partnership, 2010](https://www.saferoutespartnership.org/sites/default/files/pdf/NHTS-SRTS-Press-Release-04082010.pdf),
  on the 2009 survey). Among children living within a mile of school, 88% walked or biked in 1969
  and 38% in 2009; the share living within a mile fell from 41% to 31%.
- K-8: 47.7% (1969), 12.7% (2009), 11.1% (2017). In 2017, ages 15–17: 8.0% walked and 0.8% biked
  ([Kontou, McDonald, Brookshire, Pullen-Seufert and LaJeunesse, _Preventive Medicine Reports_, 2020, online 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6948264/)).
  The paper warns that survey methods changed in 2017.
- "Roughly half to low teens" is right, and the fall was over by the mid-1990s.

**What parents allow now, US.**

- Parents of 9–11-year-olds, August 2023: "84% agree that children benefit from having free time
  without adult supervision. Fewer parents report their child does things without an adult present,
  including staying home for 30-60 minutes (58%), finding an item at the store while the parent is
  in another aisle (50%), staying in the car while the parent runs a quick errand (44%),
  walking/biking to a friend's house (33%) or playing at the park with a friend (29%), or
  trick-or-treating with friends (15%)." The top reason is "worry that someone might scare or follow
  their child (54%); however, only 17% say their neighborhood is not safe". "Some parents believe
  state or local laws don't allow children that age to be alone (17%), that someone might call the
  police (14%)"
  ([Mott Poll, 2023](https://mottpoll.org/reports/promoting-childrens-independence-what-parents-say-vs-do),
  n = 1,044 parents of 5–11s).
- 2015: "On average, parents say children should be at least 10 years old before they should be
  allowed to play in front of their house unsupervised while an adult is inside", 12 to stay home
  alone for about an hour, 14 "to spend time at a public park unsupervised". 62% of parents "say
  they can sometimes be overprotective"
  ([Pew, "Parenting in America", 2015](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2015/12/17/parenting-in-america/),
  1,807 parents).
- Fourteen for a park reaches the first year of high school (mine). It is the only physical-freedom
  rule touching teenagers that I found a number for.

**Law on unsupervised children.** "Utah passed the first 'Reasonable Childhood Independence' law in
2018." "As of April, 2026, 13 states have passed 'Reasonable Childhood Independence' laws: Utah,
Texas, Oklahoma, Colorado, Virginia, Connecticut, Illinois, Montana, Georgia, Florida, Missouri,
Indiana and Kansas" ([Let Grow, 2026](https://letgrow.org/states/)). The laws narrow neglect
statutes so that letting a child walk or play alone is not grounds for investigation. That 13
legislatures thought this necessary is the evidence that it was being treated as neglect; I found no
count of investigations (unverified that one exists).

**The counter-evidence: parents supervise more hours.** This does not depend on any technology.

- US: "After three decades of decline, the amount of time spent by parents on childcare in the
  United States began to rise dramatically in the mid-1990s... Less educated mothers increased their
  childcare time by over 4 hours per week, and college-educated mothers increased theirs by over 9
  hours per week." The authors' explanation is "increased competition for college admissions"
  ([Ramey and Ramey, "The Rug Rat Race", _Brookings Papers on Economic Activity_, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1353/eca.2010.0003),
  abstract).
- Eleven Western countries, 1965–2012: "For both mothers and fathers, results indicated a widespread
  educational gradient and an increase in child‐care time"
  ([Dotti Sani and Treas, _Journal of Marriage and Family_, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12305),
  abstract).
- The rise starts in the mid-1990s, a decade before smartphones and two before tracking apps.

**What a 16-year-old could do in about 1985 and cannot, or does not, now.**

Legal rules:

- Drive alone at any hour with friends in the car. "A few states had elements of GDL prior to the
  mid-1990s, and between 1996 and 2006 all other states adopted a learner period of 2 months or
  more, a minimum supervised practice hours requirement for the learner period, or a night or
  passenger restriction once initially licensed." "Fifteen states raised the minimum age for a
  license allowing any unsupervised driving"
  ([Williams, McCartt and Sims, _Journal of Safety Research_, 2016](https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/bibliography/ref/2091),
  abstract; GDL is graduated driver licensing). How many states now have night and passenger limits:
  not checked.
- Get alcohol through 18–20-year-old friends who bought it legally. The National Minimum Drinking
  Age Act was signed on 17 July 1984 and "indirectly raised the nationwide minimum drinking age to
  21" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Minimum_Drinking_Age_Act)). Which states
  were still at 18 or 19 in 1985: not checked.
- Buy cigarettes at 18. "On December 20, 2019, President Donald Trump signed the 2020 United States
  federal budget which raised the federal smoking age to 21"
  ([Wikipedia, "Tobacco 21"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_21)). State ages in 1985: not
  checked.
- Be out late. Juvenile curfew ordinances rose from 93 of the 200 largest US cities (47%) in 1990 to
  146 (73%) in 1995 (snippet,
  [Ruefle and Reynolds, 1996](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/keep-them-home-juvenile-curfew-ordinances-200-american-cities)).
  Current coverage and enforcement: not found.

Institutional rules:

- Be at school unrecorded. Security cameras in 19.4% of public schools in 1999–2000, 91.1% in
  2019–20; staff ID badges 25.4% to 76.8%; student ID badges 3.9% to 10.1%. Random sweeps and dog
  sniffs barely moved (25.3% to 28.7%) (NCES table 233.50).
- Use a school computer unwatched (88% of teachers report monitoring, CDT 2023), or get a bad grade
  without a parent seeing it the same day (portals).
- Hang around a mall. Named policies as of April 2023: Garden State Plaza (under-18s need an adult
  after 5 p.m. Friday and Saturday), Fashion District Philadelphia (from 2 p.m. daily), Lenox Square
  Atlanta (3 p.m. curfew since 2021), Atlantic Station Atlanta (2022), American Dream (under-16s
  after 4 p.m.)
  ([Bisnow, 2023](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/retail/nj-mall-latest-to-restrict-teen-visitors-because-of-disruptive-behavior-118564)).
  No national count exists that I could find; the only number is an informal poll of 110 people, 48%
  of whom said their local mall bans unaccompanied minors at some hours
  ([Let Grow, undated](https://letgrow.org/malls-no-minors/)). That Mall of America started this in
  1996 is unverified.
- Leave campus for lunch. No national figure found (unverified). Cinema and hotel age policies: not
  checked.

Parental rules:

- Have a phone the parent has not looked through (half of parents have), or be somewhere the parent
  cannot see on a map (about half).
- For under-12s, go to a friend's house or the park alone (a third or fewer at ages 9–11).
- Curfew times, distance limits, dating rules for teenagers: no data, then or now.

Things nobody forbids that teens mostly stopped doing:

- Hold a licence at 16: 46.2% (1983), 24.5% (2014).
- Work in the summer: 58% (1978), 35.5% (2026).
- See friends almost every day: 52% of 12th graders (late 1970s), 28% (2017).
- Go to parties: 10th graders about 17 fewer a year than in the 1980s.
- Have had sex: 54% (1991), 32% (2023).
- Drink: 12th-grade past-year 64% (2011), 41.7% (2024).
- Walk to school at 15–17: 8.0% (2017).

**"Are there rules or are they just lame?" (mine).** Both, in different places. For children under
12 it is rules, and the rules arrived between 1970 and 1995. For 16-year-olds the legal and
institutional rules did tighten (driving, drinking, curfews, school), mostly between 1984 and 2006.
But the biggest falls are in things that are allowed and that parents want (jobs, licences), which
rules cannot explain. Twenge and Park's reading is a "slow life strategy" tied to smaller families,
higher income and later milestones. A plausible link between the two age groups, which no source I
found tests, is that children who were never allowed out alone at 9 do not start going out at 16.

## 6. The inference (my analysis)

**(a) Did teen behaviour change?** Yes, on every measure in the bullets, but on four different
clocks.

| Behaviour                             | Decline starts                        | Most of the fall happened           |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Children going places alone (England) | after 1971                            | 1971–1990                           |
| Walking or biking to school (US)      | after 1969                            | by the mid-1990s; flat since        |
| Teen drinking                         | 1975–80 (per Patrick and Schulenberg) | continuous                          |
| Licence at 16                         | after 1983 (first data point I have)  | 1983–2008 (46% to 31%), then to 25% |
| Teen births                           | 1992                                  | continuous; steepest after 2007     |
| Ever had sex                          | 1991                                  | 1991–2001, then again 2013–2021     |
| Teen summer jobs                      | 2000                                  | 2000–2010; flat since               |
| Seeing friends in person              | late 1970s                            | accelerates after 2010              |
| Loneliness                            | 2012 (falling before)                 | 2012–2018                           |

**(b) Did parental surveillance capability and use rise?** Capability, enormously: teen smartphone
ownership went from 23% (2011) to 95% (2018), and every phone can report its location. Use,
unevenly: location tracking 2–12% (2008–11), 16% (2014–15), about 50% (2018), 52% of parents of
18–25s (2026). Reading the phone flat at about half. Phone parental controls 16% to 47%. School
monitoring to 88%.

**(c) Did (b) cause (a)?** No evidence that it did, and three reasons to think it did not.

- Timing. Everything in table (a) except loneliness began before tracking was common, and most of it
  before teens had smartphones. **The most useful single fact: when Pew found 16% of parents
  tracking their teen's location in 2014–15, licensing at 16 had already fallen from 46% (1983) to
  25% (2014), teen summer employment from 58% (1978) to 29.6% (2010–11), "ever had sex" from 54%
  (1991) to 41% (2015), and the share of children walking or biking to school from 48% (1969) to 13%
  (2009).** Tracking reached half of families around 2018, after the changes it would have to
  explain.
- Direct tests. The one study correlating tracking with teen outcomes found correlations of −0.01 to
  +0.07. The Dutch study found tracking "unrelated to adolescent well‐being" and related to parental
  fear. No longitudinal study exists.
- The older literature. Twenty-five years of monitoring research says parents' surveillance effort
  does not predict changes in delinquency; the child's own disclosure does.

**Against the two alternatives.**

- Phones as entertainment. Timing fits the post-2010 acceleration in not seeing friends and fits
  loneliness exactly. It cannot explain anything that began before 2010, which is most of the list.
  Causation is disputed (Odgers; Orben and Przybylski's 0.4%), and Twenge's own data show heavy
  social media users see friends more, not less.
- Physical-world restriction. It predates everything and its timing fits the cohort story: children
  who lost independent mobility in the 1980s and 1990s were the teenagers who stopped driving and
  working in the 2000s. But the trend data is for under-12s, the best of it is English, and nobody
  has measured parental rules for teenagers over time. It is the best-timed explanation and the
  worst-measured one.

**What this gives the post's argument, and what it does not.**

- For "capability is rarely the binding constraint": parents have been able to read every message
  for fifteen years and the share who do has not moved from half. The surveillance that makes a
  parent a snoop did not grow when it got cheap.
- Against it: location tracking did go from rare to normal within about five years of becoming free
  and passive, and it now continues into adulthood for half of families. Where using a capability
  costs nothing and can be framed as safety, it gets used. A mind-reading mom is closer to the
  second case than the first if it is passive and framed as care.
- The consent point: nearly all tracked kids know (95–96%), only 54% of parents gave an
  18–25-year-old the option to refuse, and Dutch teens' acceptance of being tracked rose between
  2019 and 2024. Known-but-not-optional is a third category between his consensual lie detector and
  covert mind reading, and it is the one that spread.
- What surveillance tracks, on the evidence, is the parent: trackers are more overprotective and
  more fearful (Boele et al. 2026), and 23% say tracking sometimes makes them more anxious than
  reassured (Mott 2026). There is no evidence yet that it changes the kid.
- Not established by anything here: that tracked teens get more physical freedom in exchange (the "I
  let her go because I can see where she is" story). No study tests it.
