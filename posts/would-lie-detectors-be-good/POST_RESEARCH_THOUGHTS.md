## Where the evidence disagrees with the draft

1. **"No one cares that I smoke pot even though I post pictures... You get in trouble if someone
   complains."** Police do read social media and do arrest off it: a New Jersey man in 2015 from the
   GPS data in his Instagram grow photos, seven people in Indiana after a Facebook post captioned
   "Come get it", a Texas 17-year-old in 2022 from Instagram posts; the NYPD's Operation Crew Cut
   (2012) built conspiracy cases on teenagers' Facebook activity; Geofeedia sold police a feed of
   public posts until 2016. And marijuana enforcement has not mostly run on complaints. US police
   made over 870,000 marijuana arrests at the 2007 peak and still 217,150 in 2023, 84% for
   possession only. New York City made about 50,700 possession arrests in 2011, 86% of them of Black
   or Latino people, generated by stop-and-frisk. Black Americans are arrested for possession at
   3.73 times the white rate at similar usage. What I did not find is any agency running a dragnet
   for photos of personal use. The narrow premise survives; "you get in trouble if someone
   complains" describes his demographic, not the system.
2. **"The state is not bottlenecked on enforcement capability... It is bottlenecked on the will of
   the people."** True for some cases and false for others, and the split is clean. The supporting
   cases (red-light cameras voted out, the 1974 seatbelt interlock, IRS audits, federal cannabis
   possession, the polygraph ban itself) all have targets who are a voting majority. The opposing
   cases (Xinjiang, ICE's use of data brokers, genetic-genealogy cold cases, Singapore's
   contact-tracing data, sex-offender polygraphs) all have targets who are not, and in each of those
   enforcement rose when capability rose, with no vote. Prohibition, which sounds like a will story,
   was also a capability story: 1,520 federal agents in 1920.
3. **"Reliable lie detector: possibly exists today, and we have social reasons why we don't use
   it?"** No such method exists. The best candidate, the concealed information test, only tests
   whether you recognise a crime detail the examiner already knows; it cannot certify an arbitrary
   sentence, and it returns "inconclusive" 20–45% of the time in the Japanese data. fMRI detection
   went from 100% to 33% accuracy when subjects were taught to wiggle a toe. The one federal appeals
   court to rule on fMRI lie detection excluded it on reliability. The "social reasons" half is
   partly right: the claim that a lie detector usurps the jury is real, but in _United States v.
   Scheffer_ (1998) only four justices signed it and five explicitly rejected it.
4. **"Noisy mind reader (CAT scan? kinda exists today but BARELY)."** Not CT, which images
   structure. It is fMRI, MEG and implanted electrodes, and every working decoder needs the
   subject's cooperation. Tang et al. (2023) needed 16 hours of scanner training per person, a
   decoder trained on other people "performed barely above chance", and subjects defeated it by
   silently naming animals (decodable time points fell from 52–57% to 0–3%).
5. **"People wouldn't think bad thoughts... You self censor subconsciously."** The
   thought-suppression literature says people cannot do this on demand. Trying not to think a
   thought produces a rebound, confirmed in a 2020 meta-analysis of 31 studies. The one result
   pointing the other way (Mamat & Anderson 2023) took three days of deliberate training on chosen
   thoughts.
6. **"We are almost certainly happy to be able to make credible committments."** The economics says
   a voluntary, cheap, reliable verifier does not stay voluntary: refusing becomes the signal
   (Grossman 1981, Milgrom 1981; Peppet 2011 applies it to exactly this). The US ran the experiment.
   By 1988 employers were giving over two million polygraph tests a year, 70% of them
   pre-employment, and Congress banned the practice, including acting on a refusal. And "no such
   thing as a trial anymore" runs into the same wall from the other side: only 2.3% of federal
   defendants went to trial in fiscal 2022, and the leading cause of DNA exonerations is sincere
   eyewitnesses who were wrong (69%).

## "We are almost certainly happy to be able to make credible committments... too much iterated back and forth and you reveal secrets, glomarizing and meta-honesty hard"

### The economics

**Verifiable disclosure and unraveling (Grossman 1981; Milgrom 1981).** Setup: a sender knows
something, can stay silent or be vague, but cannot lie. Result: in equilibrium the sender reveals
everything. The best type discloses to separate from the rest, so silence now means "not the best";
the next-best type then discloses, and so on down, until the receiver "correctly infer[s] the very
worst from non-disclosure" (snippet, via
[Jin, Luca & Martin, NBER w21099, 2015](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21099.pdf)). A reliable lie
detector is the technology that makes "can stay silent but cannot lie" true of people. Implication:
once a detector is available and cheap, declining to use it is read as an admission, so "voluntary"
credible commitment becomes mandatory in effect. I did not open either 1981 paper.

**Where unraveling breaks.** Two conditions matter here. (a) If the receiver is unsure whether the
sender has anything to disclose, bad types can pool with the genuinely uninformed (Dye 1985,
snippet, via [Milgrom's 2008 survey](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cla/levrem/843644000000000045.html)).
For people: refusal is only safe if plenty of innocent people also refuse. (b) In lab experiments
receivers are "insufficiently skeptical about non-disclosed information", so senders disclose less
than theory predicts (Jin, Luca & Martin, snippet). Real people under-punish silence, at first.

**Peppet (2011) already made the argument for personal information.** "As those with positive
information about themselves choose to disclose, the economic 'unraveling effect' will occur: in
equilibrium, all will disclose their information, whether positive or negative", and "when
disclosure becomes low-cost and routine, those who hold out are suspect" (snippet,
[Northwestern Law Review](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/nulr/vol105/iss3/4/)). He
says this converts disclosure "from a consensual to a more coerced decision".

**Cheap talk (Crawford & Sobel 1982).** Setup: the sender can say anything at no cost and nothing is
verifiable. Result: all equilibria are partitions; the sender only reveals which interval the truth
lies in, and the equilibrium with the most intervals is best for both (snippet,
[Econometrica](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1982/11/01/Strategic-Information-Transmission)).
That informativeness rises as interests align is the standard reading (unverified against the
paper). Implication: a lie detector moves a conversation from the Crawford–Sobel world (coarse
information between parties with different interests) to the Grossman–Milgrom world (full
information, plus unraveling). The two results are the before and after.

**Lie detection inside cheap-talk models.** Two papers model exactly a noisy detector. Balbuzanov,
"Lies and consequences" (2019): if lies are caught with probability p, fully revealing equilibria
become possible even when p is small (snippet, paraphrased,
[IJGT](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jogath/v48y2019i4d10.1007_s00182-019-00679-z.html)). This is
rung 1 doing real work without being reliable. Dziuda & Salas, "Communication with detectable
deceit" (2018): with repeated rounds, the receiver can be hurt by further communication, and when
detectability is high would prefer to commit to giving no second chances (snippet, paraphrased,
[Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2014/communication-with-detectable-deceit)).
That is a formal version of "too much iterated back and forth".

**Schelling on commitment (1960).** "The power to constrain an adversary may depend on the power to
bind oneself" (snippet, quoted in
[Schelling's own 1993 retrospective](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1993/A1993KJ42800001.pdf)).
His examples bind future action by removing options. A lie detector certifies present intent, which
is weaker: it cannot stop you changing your mind afterwards.

**The right to silence as an equilibrium device (Seidmann & Stein 2000).** "The Right to Silence
Helps the Innocent: A Game-Theoretic Analysis of the Fifth Amendment Privilege", 114 Harv. L. Rev.
430
([record and abstract](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-right-to-silence-helps-the-innocent-a-game-theoretic-analysis/)).
The usual view is that silence "helps only criminals". Their model: without the right, guilty
suspects lie, and factfinders then discount every uncorroborated denial, including true ones. With
the right, the guilty rationally stay silent, so a denial is more believable. They call this the
"anti-pooling rationale". Implication here: their defence of silence depends on lies being
undetectable. A reliable detector separates liars from truth-tellers directly, so the rationale goes
away, and the only remaining protection is a rule against reading silence as guilt. Critique: Bibas
argued the model "does not track observed real-world outcomes"; Stein replied in
[Cardozo Law Review (2009)](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/clr/vol30/iss3/15/) (snippet). Seidmann's
"The Effects of a Right to Silence" (Review of Economic Studies, 2005) is the formal version
(snippet).

**US law already treats the detector as testimony.** _Schmerber v. California_ (1966) allowed a
compelled blood test because it was physical evidence, but the same opinion singles out "lie
detector tests measuring changes in body function during interrogation" as possibly "essentially
testimonial", and says compelling such a test "is to evoke the spirit and history of the Fifth
Amendment" ([opinion, Part II, body text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/384/757)).
So in the US a defendant could refuse, and the live question is the unraveling one: what the refusal
is allowed to mean.

### Binary search

N honest yes/no answers distinguish 2^N possibilities, so twenty questions separate about a million.
The mathematics of the game with a lying answerer is an established field (Rényi–Ulam games; with k
allowed lies the cost is about log n + k·log log n, snippet from a
[Technion talk](https://yuvalfilmus.cs.technion.ac.il/Talks/TwentyQuestionsHUJI.pdf)). I found
nobody in economics or law who states "reliable lie detector plus unlimited questions equals mind
reader" as a result. The closest are Yudkowsky's rule below and the Dziuda–Salas "no second chances"
result.

### Meta-honesty (Yudkowsky 2018): the mechanics

[Post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xdwbX9pFEr7Pomaxv/meta-honesty-firming-up-honesty-around-its-edge-cases),
dated 2018-05-29. Lie detectors and mind reading are not mentioned; the only relative is Dune's
Truthsayers.

- **The rule.** In two adjoining fragments: "Don't lie when a normal highly honest person wouldn't,
  and furthermore, be honest when somebody asks you" / "which hypothetical circumstances would cause
  you to lie or mislead—absolutely honest, if they ask under this code." Object-level honesty is
  high but not absolute; honesty about your own policy is absolute.
- **Why not just glomarize.** To refuse without leaking you must refuse in every case where some
  version of you would have something to hide. He says this "does not work in principle because
  there are too many counterfactual selves who might want to conceal something", and gives the
  example that "if you casually ask me what I was doing last night, I theoretically ought to answer"
  with a refusal; "the number of counterfactuals I have to worry about is too large for me to ever
  answer anything."
- **Why it also fails in practice.** Investigators "will not be familiar with your previous practice
  of Glomarization"; it "doesn't work in practice if the Nazis show up at your door"; "that doesn't
  work for hiding fugitives."
- **The many-questions attack is named and banned by fiat.** "Questions about meta-honesty should be
  careful not to probe object-level information"; "meta-questions are not to be used to extract
  object-level data". His own example has a Gestapo officer say "I am deliberately pressuring you
  into a meta-conversation and trying to use your code of honesty against those counterfactual
  selves", and his answer is that "it is not okay to meta-ask what the Gestapo officer is
  meta-asking here" and he must "Glomarize harder". The defence against binary search is a social
  norm on the questioner. Nothing mechanical.
- **Admitted failure modes.** The literal-truth "wizard's code" "only lets people navigate anything
  like ordinary social reality to the extent that they are very fast on their verbal feet". "I worry
  that the notion of meta-honesty is too complicated and subtle." "I can't consistently Glomarize
  about every point in my life". "I'd outright lie to the Gestapo about Jews in my attic." On
  whether it preserves respect for truth: "I don't actually know if that works either."

### Protocol-style fixes that already exist

- **Pre-agreed, scope-limited question lists.** The 1988 statute writes this into law for the
  polygraphs it still allows. The employee must "review all questions which will be asked during the
  actual test", may not be asked "any questions dealing with religious beliefs or affiliations,
  racial matters, sexual behavior, political beliefs and affiliations, beliefs, or opinions
  regarding unions", may "terminate the test at any time", and the examiner may not ask "any
  questions not reviewed in writing before the test" (Senate Report 100-284, p. 40,
  [PDF](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/110101NCJRS.pdf)).
- **Scope tiers in security clearances.** A counterintelligence-scope polygraph is limited by
  regulation to "espionage, sabotage, terrorism, unauthorized disclosure of classified information"
  (10 CFR 709.2, snippet via
  [USLegal](https://definitions.uslegal.com/c/counterintelligence-scope-polygraph-examination)); the
  expanded-scope exam adds criminal conduct, drugs and falsified forms (snippet). A working example
  of "certify these four things and nothing else".
- **Stipulated polygraphs.** In some US states the parties agree before the test that the result
  comes in whichever way it goes (snippet; see the courts section).
- **The information barrier.** In warhead-dismantlement verification the inspector's detector is
  wrapped in a device that "takes data from a measurement device, processes the data relative to
  predetermined criteria and provides a pass/fail output" (snippet,
  [UK–Norway working paper, 2010](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a79015fed915d04220670ca/npt_revcon_2010_jwp.pdf)).
  This is the "neutral examiner who only reports pass/fail on one pre-registered sentence", built in
  hardware. The acknowledged hard part is trust in the box: making one that the inspector can
  authenticate and that provably leaks nothing "remains the core challenge" (snippet, Sandia).
- **Zero-knowledge warhead verification.** Glaser, Barak & Goldston, "A zero-knowledge protocol for
  nuclear warhead verification" ([Nature, 2014](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1127356)). The host
  preloads neutron detectors so that a genuine warhead leaves each "topped up" to an agreed total; a
  fake gives a different total; the inspector chooses which detector goes with which item. Goal, in
  Goldston's words: "to prove with as high confidence as required that an object is a true nuclear
  warhead while learning nothing about the materials and design" (snippet,
  [Princeton](https://blogs.princeton.edu/research/2014/06/25/a-farewell-to-arms-scientists-developing-a-novel-technique-that-could-facilitate-nuclear-disarmament-nature)).
- **A user-held key.** The Stanford inner-speech implant (Kunz et al., Cell, 2025) only decodes
  after the user thinks a chosen passphrase; the unlock phrase was detected with 98.75% accuracy
  (snippet,
  [Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2025/08/brain-computer-interface.html)).
  The subject, not the examiner, gates the read.

I found no proposal for human lie detection that combines these (one pre-registered sentence,
pass/fail only, no follow-ups).

### Belief versus truth

A detector certifies that the speaker believes the sentence.

- **Sincere and wrong.** Eyewitness misidentification is in 69% of the Innocence Project's DNA
  exonerations, 252 of 367 (snippet,
  [Innocence Project](https://innocenceproject.org/dna-exonerations-in-the-united-states/)). Those
  witnesses would pass.
- **False memory.** Scoboria et al., "A mega-analysis of memory reports from eight peer-reviewed
  false memory implantation studies" ([Memory, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27892833/)):
  across 423 subjects, 30.4% were coded as having formed a false memory of a suggested childhood
  event and another 23% accepted it to some degree (snippet). Loftus & Pickrell's "lost in the mall"
  figure of 25% (1995) is unverified here.
- **Self-deception as a way to pass.** Von Hippel & Trivers, "The evolution and psychology of
  self-deception"
  ([BBS, 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/evolution-and-psychology-of-selfdeception/B87968EC4A6B4DC93A21C217ABC13E13)):
  "self-deception evolved to facilitate interpersonal deception by allowing people to avoid the cues
  to conscious deception that might reveal deceptive intent" (snippet). On this account humans
  already carry the countermeasure.
- **False confessions after a "failed" test.** US police may lie to suspects about evidence
  (_Frazier v. Cupp_, 1969), and lower courts have admitted confessions obtained after falsely
  telling a suspect he failed a polygraph (snippet,
  [Yale Law Journal](https://yalelawjournal.org/article/from-false-evidence-ploy-to-false-guilty-plea-an-unjustified-path-to-securing-convictions)).
  Hawaii is the exception: _State v. Matsumoto_ (2019) held that falsified polygraph results in
  custodial interrogation make what follows "coercive per se" (snippet,
  [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/hawaii/supreme-court/2019/scwc-14-0000933.html)); the court
  reasoned that fake results can lead a suspect to believe he committed the crime. A trusted
  detector makes this ploy stronger.

## "1: noisy lie detector (exists today)"

Covered by the other file. Two facts that matter for the ladder: the National Research Council's
_The Polygraph and Lie Detection_
([2003, ch. 8](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10420/chapter/10)) says specific-incident
polygraphs "can discriminate lying from truth telling at rates well above chance, though well below
perfection", that "the theoretical rationale for the polygraph is quite weak", and that for
screening a test good enough to catch spies would "incorrectly implicate at least hundreds, and
perhaps thousands" of innocent employees per spy. A 2019 review found "the report's landmark
conclusions still stand" and that the standard test's "error rate is unknown" (snippet,
[Iacono & Ben-Shakhar, Law and Human Behavior](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30284848/)).

## "2: noisy mind reader (CAT scan? kinda exists today but _BARELY_)"

CT measures X-ray density, so it shows anatomy and nothing about activity. The decoding results use
fMRI (blood flow, seconds-slow, millimetre resolution), MEG/EEG (fast, blurry) or electrodes in the
cortex (fast, sharp, needs surgery).

**Tang, LeBel, Jain & Huth (2023), fMRI, language.**
[Nature Neuroscience, full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11304553).

- Three subjects, each scanned for "sixteen hours of naturally spoken narrative stories" to train a
  per-person model.
- Output is gist, not transcript. Word error rate 0.92–0.94 against a chance level of 0.96. On a
  meaning-based metric (BERTScore), 72–82% of time points were decoded significantly better than
  chance.
- Imagined speech: the right story was identified 100% of the time out of the candidates; 41–74% of
  time points significantly decoded.
- **Other people's brains don't transfer.** Decoders trained on other subjects "performed barely
  above chance": 1–5% of time points recovered against 65–82% within-subject.
- **Resistance works.** While listening, subjects did mental tasks. Passive listening: 52–57% of
  time points decodable. Silently naming animals: 0–3%. Telling themselves a different story: 1–26%.
  Counting by sevens did not reliably help (4–50%).
- Their sentence: "subject cooperation is required both to train and to apply the decoder."

**Takagi & Nishimoto (2023), fMRI, images.** "High-resolution image reconstruction with latent
diffusion models from human brain activity" (CVPR 2023;
[project page](https://sites.google.com/view/stablediffusion-with-brain),
[preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.11.18.517004v1.full.pdf)). Reconstructs
images a person is looking at by mapping fMRI to Stable Diffusion's latent space. Data: four
subjects from the Natural Scenes Dataset, 7-Tesla scanner, 30–40 sessions each (snippet). This is
seen images, per-subject models; accuracy numbers not retrieved.

**Meta, MEG.** Défossez et al., "Decoding speech perception from non-invasive brain recordings"
(Nature Machine Intelligence, 2023; [text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.12266)): 175
volunteers, 3-second windows. Picking which speech segment a person was hearing from 1,363
candidates: 41.3% top-1 and 70.7% top-10 on the best MEG dataset; EEG about 5% top-1. Heard speech
only. Meta's Brain2Qwerty ([arXiv, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.17480)) decodes sentences as
people type them: 32% character error rate with MEG, 67% with EEG (snippet). MEG needs a
magnetically shielded room.

**Implants.** Willett et al., "A high-performance speech neuroprosthesis"
([Nature, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06377-x)): attempted speech in one ALS
patient decoded at 62 words per minute, 23.8% word error rate on a 125,000-word vocabulary
(snippet). Card et al. (NEJM, 2024), 97.5% accuracy: unverified. Kunz et al. (Cell, 2025): inner
speech, never attempted aloud, decoded at up to 74% accuracy from motor cortex in four implanted
patients; the authors flagged that the device "could end up decoding something the user intended
only to think" and added the passphrase gate (snippet,
[Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2025/08/brain-computer-interface.html)).

Summary of limits: per-subject training of hours (fMRI) or surgery (implants), a subject who goes
along with it, and error rates between a quarter and "gist only". No published result decodes an
unwilling or untrained person.

## "3: Reliable lie detector: possibly exists today, and we have social reasons why we don't use it? What is history of using lie detectors in the courts?"

### Is there a reliable method being held back?

**Concealed information test (CIT, also "guilty knowledge test").** The standard US polygraph
(comparison question test, CQT) compares your reaction to "did you do it?" with your reaction to
deliberately vague control questions. The CIT instead shows a multiple-choice list of crime details
(the weapon was a: knife, rope, hammer...) and looks for a recognition response to the true one. It
"does not aim to detect deception: rather, it aims to detect crime-relevant memory" (snippet,
Matsuda 2019).

- Lab validity: Meijer, Klein Selle, Elber & Ben-Shakhar, "Memory detection with the Concealed
  Information Test: A meta-analysis"
  ([Psychophysiology, 2014](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/memory-detection-with-the-concealed-information-test-a-meta-analy/)):
  effect sizes d\* of 1.55 for skin conductance and 1.89 for the P300 brainwave (snippet). An older
  lab meta-analysis (Elaad) gives 4.1% false positives and 19.4% false negatives
  ([Matsuda 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00024/full)).
- Japan: "About 100 polygraph examiners deal with about 5,000 cases per year"; "The CQT is not
  currently used at all"; the Supreme Court admitted polygraph results in 1968 but only "a few of
  the about 5,000 cases" reach court each year (Matsuda 2019). It is an investigative tool there.
- Japanese accuracy figures, from 152 experimental examinees judged by 36 examiners: hit rate 86.4%,
  correct rejection 94.5%, **20.4% inconclusive**. A stricter threshold gets 100% correct rejection
  at the cost of 44.7% inconclusive. About 25% of people give no usable skin-conductance response at
  all (Matsuda 2019).
- Why it is rare elsewhere: the paper's own explanation is that "many practitioners have not known
  how to apply the CIT in the field", and it concedes "it is sometimes difficult to find enough
  crime details that have not been publicly announced". The test needs secrets only the culprit and
  the police know. The common explanation that US interrogation practice leaks those details to
  suspects is unverified here.
- What it cannot do: certify a sentence. It answers "does this person recognise item C", for items
  the examiner already knows. It is useless for the first bullet's use case.

**P300 "brain fingerprinting" (Farwell).** Same logic as the CIT with EEG as the sensor. Farwell
reports 100% accuracy in all his studies; Meijer, Ben-Shakhar, Verschuere & Donchin (Donchin was
Farwell's mentor) wrote that his review "is misleading and misrepresents the scientific status of
brain fingerprinting technology" (snippet,
[Cognitive Neurodynamics, 2013](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/a-comment-on-farwell-2012-brain-fingerprinting-a-comprehensive-tu/);
[Farwell's reply](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3595431/)). Rosenfeld et al.,
"Simple, effective countermeasures to P300-based tests"
([Psychophysiology, 2004](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/rosenfeld/documents/CMpaperreprint.pdf)):
P300 detection "may be readily defeated with simple countermeasures that can be easily learned"
(snippet); one summary gives detection falling from 65% to 33% (snippet). _Harrington v. State_
(Iowa 2003): the trial judge admitted the brain-fingerprinting evidence but denied a new trial; the
Iowa Supreme Court then reversed the conviction because prosecutors had suppressed exculpatory
evidence, not because of the EEG (secondhand,
[ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/magazine/article/truth_to_tell)).

**fMRI companies (No Lie MRI, Cephos).** Both sold tests from the mid-2000s. Ganis, Rosenfeld,
Meixner, Kievit & Schendan, "Lying in the scanner: covert countermeasures disrupt deception
detection by functional magnetic resonance imaging"
([NeuroImage, 2011](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2316977)): single-subject accuracy was 100%
with no countermeasures and 33% when subjects made a tiny finger or toe movement on some irrelevant
items (snippet). In court: _United States v. Semrau_
([6th Cir. 2012](https://law.stanford.edu/2012/09/07/hot-news-6th-circuit-affirms-in-us-v-semrau-says-no-to-fmri-lie-detection/))
upheld exclusion of a Cephos scan because error rates in real-world settings were unknown and the
field had no consensus, adding that "there may well come a time" when that changes (snippet).
_Wilson v. Corestaff Services_
([N.Y. Sup. Ct. 2010](https://law.stanford.edu/2010/05/05/breaking-news-fmri-lie-detection-evidence-excluded-in-ny-court-2/))
excluded a Cephos scan on the other ground: credibility is for the jury, so the judge "did not need
to decide whether fMRI lie detection is sufficiently reliable" (snippet).

**Countermeasures against the ordinary polygraph.** Honts, Raskin & Kircher, "Mental and physical
countermeasures reduce the accuracy of polygraph tests" (Journal of Applied Psychology, 1994;
[index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/36750725)): with brief
training, mental and physical countermeasures each let about half of guilty subjects beat the CQT,
and examiners could not spot them (snippet). Aldrich Ames passed CIA polygraphs in 1986 and 1991
while spying for Moscow (snippet,
[Deseret News, 1994](https://www.deseret.com/1994/9/19/19131708/warnings-on-ames-ignored-report-says/)).

Three separate questions, three answers. Lab accuracy: good for the CIT, with a large inconclusive
band. Field accuracy: unmeasured almost everywhere, because ground truth is missing. Countermeasure
resistance: poor for every method tested.

### Courts

- **_Frye v. United States_ (D.C. Cir. 1923) was a lie-detector case.** James Frye, convicted of
  second-degree murder, offered Marston's "systolic blood pressure deception test". The court's two
  key sentences: "Somewhere in this twilight zone the evidential force of the principle must be
  recognized, and while courts will go a long way in admitting expert testimony deduced from a
  well-recognized scientific principle or discovery, the thing from which the deduction is made must
  be sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance in the particular field in which it
  belongs." And: "We think the systolic blood pressure deception test has not yet gained such
  standing and scientific recognition among physiological and psychological authorities". The whole
  opinion is under two pages
  ([PDF](https://fpamed.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/09/Frye-v-US-1923.pdf)). The US rule for all
  scientific evidence for seventy years was written to keep out a lie detector.
- **_Daubert_ (1993)** replaced Frye in federal courts with a judge-as-gatekeeper test (testability,
  error rate, peer review, acceptance) (unverified here; standard account).
- **_United States v. Scheffer_ (1998).** An airman wanted to use a polygraph he had passed; a
  military rule banned all polygraph evidence. The Court upheld the ban 8–1. The part everyone
  signed was reliability: "there is simply no consensus that polygraph evidence is reliable"; the
  opinion cites studies from 87% accuracy down to "little better than could be obtained by the toss
  of a coin". The social argument is Part II-B: "A fundamental premise of our criminal trial system
  is that 'the jury is the lie detector'"; "By its very nature, polygraph evidence may diminish the
  jury's role in making credibility determinations"; and the "aura of infallibility attending
  polygraph evidence can lead jurors to abandon their duty". **Only Thomas, Rehnquist, Scalia and
  Souter joined that part** ([opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-1133.ZO.html)).
  Kennedy, with O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer, wrote that the opinion "overreaches" there and agreed
  with the dissenting Stevens that "the argument demeans and mistakes the role and competence of
  jurors"; he called it a "tired argument"
  ([concurrence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-1133.ZC.html)). Five justices rejected
  the usurpation rationale. It survives in lower courts anyway (_Wilson v. Corestaff_ above).
- **States.** "Most States maintain per se rules excluding polygraph evidence"; "New Mexico is
  unique in making polygraph evidence generally admissible without the prior stipulation of the
  parties and without significant restriction" (Scheffer). A group of states admits results when
  both sides stipulate before the test; a 1989 survey counted 18 (snippet, dated; several have since
  reversed).
- **Outside trial, the polygraph is everywhere.** Federal, state and local governments are exempt
  from the employer ban, as are national-security contractors, armoured-car and guard firms, and
  drug manufacturers ([DOL Fact Sheet 36](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/36-eppa)).
  Sex-offender supervision: in Ahlmeyer et al., "The impact of polygraphy on admissions of victims
  and offenses in adult sexual offenders"
  ([Sexual Abuse, 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10872241/)), 60 Colorado offenders showed
  "dramatic increases in the number of admitted victims and offenses" for inmates once polygraphed
  (snippet). Police interrogation uses it as a confession tool; the Senate report quotes an examiner
  calling it "the best confession-getter since the cattle-prod" (p. 42). Use in plea bargaining is
  widely described but unverified here.
- **England and Wales.** Mandatory polygraph conditions for sex offenders on licence under the
  Offender Management Act 2007, national from 2014 (sources give different start dates), extended to
  domestic-abuse offenders by the Domestic Abuse Act 2021
  ([government factsheet](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/domestic-abuse-bill-2020-factsheets/mandatory-polygraph-tests-factsheet))
  and to terrorism offenders (snippet). No UK randomised trial has measured the effect on
  reoffending; evaluations count disclosures (snippet, advocacy source).
- **India.** On 12 June 2008 a Pune sessions court convicted Aditi Sharma of murdering her former
  fiancé, citing a BEOS (Brain Electrical Oscillation Signature) EEG test as showing "experiential
  knowledge" of the crime, the first conviction of its kind; the Bombay High Court later released
  her on bail, finding the other evidence insufficient without ruling on the scan (secondhand,
  [Reason, 2008](https://reason.com/2008/09/15/brain-scans-used-to-convict-wo/),
  [Carnegie Council](https://www.carnegiecouncil.org/publications/archive/policy_innovations/briefings/000172)).
  _Selvi v. State of Karnataka_ (Supreme Court, 5 May 2010) then held that involuntary
  narcoanalysis, polygraph and brain-mapping are "testimonial compulsion" barred by Article 20(3) of
  the constitution; voluntary tests with safeguards remain possible (snippet,
  [judgment](https://main.sci.gov.in/pdf/aorexam/leading_cases/26.pdf)). Same conclusion as
  _Schmerber_'s aside.

## "4: reliable mind reader. I am really not convinced this is bad."

**People who have argued it would be fine, or that the objection is confused.**

- **Posner, "The Right of Privacy"
  ([Georgia Law Review, 1978](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/journal_articles/1804); short
  version,
  ["An Economic Theory of Privacy"](https://cato.org/regulation/may/june-1978/economic-theory-privacy)).**
  Treats privacy as "the withholding or concealment of information" and notes that concealing
  "discreditable facts about oneself" to improve one's reputation is a main motive (snippet). On
  that view much personal privacy is the same act as a seller hiding a product defect.
- **Ryberg, "Neuroscience, Mind Reading and Mental Privacy"
  ([Res Publica, 2017; online 2016](https://forskning.ruc.dk/en/publications/neuroscience-mind-reading-and-mental-privacy/)).**
  "It is difficult to specify what a violation of mental privacy amounts to", given that "we usually
  regard natural mind reading as morally unproblematic" (snippet). We infer each other's thoughts
  from faces and behaviour all day; he asks what is added by a machine.
- **Lippert-Rasmussen, "Brain Privacy, Intimacy, and Authenticity: Why a Complete Lack of the Former
  Might Undermine Neither of the Latter!"
  ([Res Publica, 2017](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11158-016-9344-z)).** The title is the
  thesis; I could not retrieve the abstract. This is the closest philosophy paper to the bullet.
- **Brin, _The Transparent Society_ (1998).** Not about minds, but the structural claim carries
  over: surveillance is coming regardless, so the only stable good outcome is "reciprocal
  transparency" where citizens can watch the watchers (snippet,
  [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Transparent_Society)).

**People who have argued it would be bad.**

- **Nagel, "Concealment and Exposure" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1998;
  [record](https://stafforini.com/works/nagel-2002-concealmente-exposure-other/)).** Civilised life
  depends on "reticence and nonacknowledgment": far more goes on inside us than we are willing to
  express, and the convention of not expressing it "permit[s] peaceful interaction among persons
  with conflicting values" (snippet; I could not open the text, so no direct quotes). The best
  statement of the "that chick is hot" worry.
- **Ienca & Andorno, "Towards new human rights in the age of neuroscience and neurotechnology"
  ([Life Sciences, Society and Policy, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28444626/)).** Proposes
  four rights: cognitive liberty, mental privacy, mental integrity, psychological continuity
  (snippet).
- **Farahany, _The Battle for Your Brain_ (2023).** "Cognitive liberty" as an umbrella over mental
  privacy, freedom of thought and self-determination. She treats freedom of thought as absolute but
  mental privacy as "a relative right" that strong societal interests can override (snippet,
  [IEEE Spectrum interview](https://spectrum.ieee.org/amp/neurotechnology-battle-for-your-brain-2659647798)).
  So even the leading advocate does not claim mind reading is always wrong. Not read.
- **Schneier against Brin (2008).** Mutual transparency ignores differences in power between the
  parties; the less power you have, the more your privacy matters to you (snippet, paraphrased,
  [Techdirt summary](https://archive.techdirt.com/articles/20080307/102347473.shtml);
  [Brin's reply](https://davidbrin.com/nonfiction/tsdefense.html)).

**Law.** Chile's Law 21.383 (25 October 2021) amended Article 19 of the constitution to require the
law to "specially protect brain activity, as well as the information coming from it" (snippet,
[Carey](https://www.carey.cl/en/law-no-21383-constitutional-reform-to-establish-the-scientific-and-technological-development-at-the-service-of-the-people-is-published)).
In August 2023 the Chilean Supreme Court ordered Emotiv to delete a former senator's EEG data
([case analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10929545)); sources disagree on whether
the ruling rested on the new clause or on older privacy rights (snippet,
[The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/ethics/govern-neurodata-by-how-its-used-not-where-its-from/)).
The "Minding Rights" paper (Ligthart et al., 2023) notes there is no consensus on what "mental
privacy" even means (snippet).

## "walk through TSA effortlessly"

**SPOT (TSA behaviour detection), the largest real attempt.** GAO-14-159, "TSA Should Limit Future
Funding for Behavior Detection Activities" ([2013](https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-14-159);
[text](https://www.gao.gov/assets/660/658983.txt)):

- Cost: about $900 million from fiscal 2007 to 2012, about $200 million a year. About 3,000 officers
  at 176 airports.
- Yield, fiscal 2011–2012 at the 49 airports analysed: about 61,000 referrals for extra screening,
  about 8,700 passed to police (14%), 365 arrests (4% of those). The arrests GAO describes were for
  things like "outstanding warrants", "public intoxication", "suspected illegal entry". The report
  identifies no terrorist caught.
- Science: reviewing meta-analyses of over 400 studies, GAO found the human ability to spot
  deception from behaviour "is the same as or slightly better than chance (54 percent)".
- Individual officers' referral rates ranged from 0 to 26 per 160 hours worked.
- TSA folded the programme into ordinary screening in fiscal 2017 (snippet).

**iBorderCtrl (EU pilot, Hungary/Greece/Latvia, ended 2019).** An avatar questions travellers at
home by webcam and scores "micro-gestures". Accuracy was 76% in a test of about 32 people asked to
lie on instruction; the team hoped for 85%. An Intercept reporter who answered truthfully was
flagged for further checks (snippet,
[Engadget, 2018](https://www.engadget.com/2018-10-31-ai-lie-detector-helps-screen-eu-travelers.html)).

**AVATAR (University of Arizona kiosk).** Trialled with CBP at Nogales in 2011–2012 on volunteers
applying for trusted-traveller status, later tested with Canada's border agency. CBP's claim: people
detect deception "about 54% of the time... We have got our machine as high as 90% in the lab". No
independent field accuracy and no adoption that I could find (snippet,
[GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/em/disaster/border-patrol-kiosk-detects-liars.html),
[Future Travel Experience, 2017](https://www.futuretravelexperience.com/2017/01/canadian-border-services-agency-testing-lie-detection-avatar/)).

**Israeli interview screening.** Every passenger is questioned by a trained interviewer; SPOT was
modelled on it. Arab citizens of Israel "see it as outright discrimination", and Israeli
consultancies that tried to export it to the US mostly "downsized or left the field" because it
depends on labour-intensive questioning and group profiling (snippet,
[Forward](https://forward.com/articles/122781)). Cost per passenger: not found.

The base-rate arithmetic from the NRC report applies to all of these: when almost nobody is a
terrorist, a test that flags even 1% of honest people buries the true positives.

## "no such thing as a trial anymore we just know... Only qusetion is about the law, not process or facts"

- **Trials are already rare.** Fiscal 2022, federal: 89.5% of 71,954 defendants pleaded guilty,
  about 8% were dismissed, 2.3% went to trial, 0.4% were acquitted at trial (snippet,
  [Pew, 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/06/14/fewer-than-1-of-defendants-in-federal-criminal-cases-were-acquitted-in-2022/)).
  _Missouri v. Frye_ (2012): "Ninety-seven percent of federal convictions and ninety-four percent of
  state convictions are the result of guilty pleas" (snippet,
  [opinion](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/566/134)). Federal civil cases ending in
  trial: 11.5% in 1962, 1.8% in 2002 (snippet,
  [Galanter, "The Vanishing Trial", 2004](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:empleg:v:1:y:2004:i:3:p:459-570)).
- **What a detector would fix.** Among 2024 exonerations, 72% involved perjury or false accusation
  and 71% official misconduct (snippet,
  [National Registry of Exonerations, 2024 Annual Report](https://exonerationregistry.org/sites/exonerationregistry.org/files/documents/2024_Annual_Report.pdf)).
  Lying witnesses and lying officials are the largest known source of wrongful convictions.
- **What it would not fix.** Mistaken identification (26% of 2024 exonerations; 69% of Innocence
  Project DNA cases): sincere. False confessions (15% of 2024 exonerations): some confessors come to
  believe it (the _Matsumoto_ court's reasoning, above). Crimes with no surviving witness.
  Mental-state elements where the defendant's own memory of his intent is reconstructed after the
  fact.
- **The defendant can refuse** (_Schmerber_, _Selvi_), so the system only becomes "we just know" if
  refusal can be held against him.
- I found no data on what share of contested cases turn on witness credibility as opposed to law.
  The plea figures are the nearest proxy and they cut both ways: pleas happen because the facts are
  usually not in real dispute, and also because trial penalties make contesting them too risky.

## "Employers know if you treated company fairly"

All from Senate Report 100-284 ([1988](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/110101NCJRS.pdf))
unless marked.

- **Scale.** "Today over two million polygraph tests are administered annually... the vast majority
  of tests today are used as a screening procedure in private sector employment." The split: "70% of
  tests administered are preemployment, another 15% of tests are post-employment random, and only
  15% involve polygraph examinations as part of an investigation of a specific incident" (pp. 41,
  46).
- **Error cost.** A witness "calculated that a minimum of 400,000 honest workers are wrongfully
  labeled deceptive, and suffer adverse employment consequences each year." The base-rate point is
  in the report: "even assuming an extraordinary 95% degree of accuracy with such tests, in every
  population of 1,000 examinees, 47 innocent people will be labeled guilty".
- **What the tests were.** Workplace exams were "often no more than 15 minutes long"; F. Lee Bailey,
  a polygraph advocate, told the committee "these exams were not polygraph tests". The committee
  "found no credible study validating the accuracy of the typical examination being given in the
  private sector."
- **Abuse.** "Many employers and polygraph examiners abuse and manipulate the examination process,
  and frequently use inaccurate or unfounded results to justify employment decisions which otherwise
  would be suspect." The statute's list of forbidden topics (religion, race, sex, politics, unions)
  is a list of what was being asked.
- **The business case failed on the evidence.** "Businesses in states which have banned polygraphs
  entirely suffer no apparent disadvantage compared to their counterparts in states where testing is
  utilized."
- **Refusal.** No adverse action may be taken "based on an analysis of polygraph test results or
  refusal to take such a test without additional supporting evidence" (p. 40). Congress blocked the
  unraveling inference by statute.
- **Evasion before the federal law.** Applicants in states that banned testing "have been asked to
  submit to polygraph tests in a neighboring state". A patchwork ban did not hold.
- **What survived.** Tests in a specific-incident investigation with reasonable suspicion, "where
  there is some evidence of validity". Government employers are exempt because, the report says, the
  Constitution already constrains them. Exemptions for security firms and drug companies are in
  [DOL Fact Sheet 36](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/36-eppa). The Act was signed 27
  June 1988 and was expected to eliminate about 85% of tests (snippet).
- **What replaced it.** Written "integrity tests". The committee had heard "few complaints about
  such tests" and left them alone (p. 47). OTA's "The Use of Integrity Tests for Pre-Employment
  Screening" ([1990](https://ota.fas.org/reports/9042.pdf)) estimated 5,000–6,000 firms using them
  (snippet). Do they work? Ones, Viswesvaran & Schmidt (1993): validity .41 for job performance. Van
  Iddekinge et al.
  ([2012](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Criterion-Related-Validity-of-Integrity-Tests/9984380535302771)):
  .15 for job performance and .32 for counterproductive behaviour, and higher (.27) in studies
  written by the test publishers than in independent ones (.12) (snippet). They predict something;
  how much is disputed by a factor of two to three.

## "People know if you cheat so.... they don't cheat? Or if they do people are sadder? unclear."

- **Belief in detection changes what people admit.** The "bogus pipeline" is a fake lie detector.
  Alexander & Fisher, "Truth and consequences: Using the bogus pipeline to examine sex differences
  in self-reported sexuality"
  ([Journal of Sex Research, 2003](https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/media/0768/Alexander%20%26%20Fisher%20%282003%29.pdf)):
  sex differences in reported sexual behaviour "were negligible in the bogus pipeline condition" and
  moderate under anonymity (snippet). The partner counts usually quoted, women 2.6 when answers
  might be seen, 3.4 anonymous, 4.4 on the fake detector, against men 3.7 / 4.2 / 4.0, are
  secondhand; the PDF would not open.
- **Detection raises disclosure of real offences.** Ahlmeyer et al. (2000), above: inmates admitted
  far more victims and offences once polygraphed. The same abstract reports "a substantial decline"
  in admissions by the second exam even though 80% of results still indicated deception (snippet).
  People adapt to the machine. Whether polygraph supervision reduces reoffending: no good evidence
  found.
- **How infidelity comes out matters.** Afifi, Falato & Weiner (Journal of Social and Personal
  Relationships, 2001): ranked by damage to the relationship, hearing from a third party was worst,
  then catching the partner in the act, then asking and being told, with unprompted confession the
  least damaging (secondhand, via citing papers; see the
  [Blow & Hartnett 2005 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15974059/)). A detector result is
  closest to the bad end: discovery, not confession.
- **Known versus secret affairs in therapy.** Atkins et al., "Infidelity and behavioral couple
  therapy: Optimism in the face of betrayal" ([2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15709841/)): 19
  affair couples out of 134 in a trial; those whose affair was revealed started worse and improved
  faster, while couples with an affair kept secret did worse (snippet). Five-year follow-up (Marín,
  Christensen & Atkins, 2014): among couples who stayed married, satisfaction did not differ between
  affair and no-affair couples, but divorce was about twice as common after a revealed affair, 43%
  against 23% (secondhand). Small samples.
- **Deterrence.** No study found on whether a higher chance of detection lowers the rate of
  infidelity. Commercial "infidelity polygraphs" are sold in the US and UK; no outcome data found.

## "wouldn't think bad thoughts because then others would know you even thought them... You self censor subconsciously."

- **Suppression backfires.** Wegner's white-bear studies (1987) and ironic process theory (1994):
  told not to think of a white bear, people think of it more afterwards. Wang, Hagger &
  Chatzisarantis, "Ironic effects of thought suppression: a meta-analysis"
  ([Perspectives on Psychological Science, 2020](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/60795/)), 31
  studies in Wegner's paradigm: "Rebound effects were observed regardless of cognitive load, whereas
  immediate enhancement effects were observed only in the presence of cognitive load" (snippet). The
  rebound replicates; an earlier meta-analysis (Abramowitz et al., 2001) sized it as small to
  moderate (snippet). I did not open Wegner's papers.
- **The counter-result.** Mamat & Anderson, "Improving mental health by training the suppression of
  unwanted thoughts"
  ([Science Advances, 2023](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/suppressing-negative-thoughts-good-for-mental-health)):
  120 people trained over three days to suppress specific feared events; the thoughts became less
  vivid, mental health improved, and only one participant showed a rebound (snippet). Trained,
  targeted suppression of rehearsed thoughts can work. That is different from never having a
  spontaneous reaction.
- **Chilling effects on what people read and say.** Penney, "Chilling Effects: Online Surveillance
  and Wikipedia Use"
  ([Berkeley Technology Law Journal, 2016](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2769645)): views of 48
  terrorism-related Wikipedia articles dropped after the June 2013 Snowden disclosures; coverage
  gives the drop as 20% or 30% depending on the article set, and Penney argues the trend stayed
  lower (snippet). Critique: the study did not benchmark against overall Wikipedia traffic
  (snippet). Stoycheff, "Under Surveillance" (Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly, 2016):
  about 250 people; reminding them of government monitoring made those who thought they held a
  minority view less willing to post it, an effect concentrated among people who consider
  surveillance justified (snippet,
  [Quartz](https://qz.com/650777/research-finds-that-government-surveillance-has-a-chilling-effect-on-online-discourse)).
  Against: Preibusch, "Privacy behaviors after Snowden"
  ([CACM, 2015](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/cacm/Preibusch15.html)): interest in privacy
  tools spiked and "returned to and even fell below original levels" (snippet).

The evidence supports "people change what they do and say when watched" and does not support "people
can stop having the thought".

## "people don't do weird things because then others would know... one hopes we can have social norms against this"

**Visibility produces conformity.**

- Schwartz's 1954 study of an Israeli kibbutz: shared dining, showers and housing arranged "to
  minimize privacy" meant "perfect information, instantaneously communicated", and public opinion
  alone controlled behaviour with no need for formal law (snippet, via
  [a later reappraisal](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3AAD1A374141E6A79B693E9D4BCBC85B/S0023921600024105a.pdf/law-in-the-kibbutz-a-reappraisal.pdf)).
  Caveat in the same source: members self-selected and were screened.
- Bernstein, "The Transparency Paradox"
  ([Administrative Science Quarterly, 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/33780569/bernstein_transparency-paradox.pdf?sequence=1)):
  field experiment at a Chinese phone factory. Watching workers led them to hide their better
  methods; "even a modest increase in group-level privacy sustainably and significantly improves
  line performance" (snippet). Observed people performed the approved procedure. The 10–15% figure
  sometimes quoted is unverified.

**Visibility loosens norms.**

- Among Americans who know many gay or lesbian people, 73% favoured same-sex marriage in 2015; among
  those who know none, 32% (snippet,
  [Pew, 2015](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2015/06/08/section-2-knowing-gays-and-lesbians-religious-conflicts-beliefs-about-homosexuality/)).
  Cross-sectional. Rosenfeld's "Moving a Mountain" (2017) argues coming out caused the shift
  (unverified).
- Kuran, _Private Truths, Public Lies_ (1995): when people hide what they think, public opinion can
  sit far from private opinion and then flip suddenly once enough dissent becomes visible
  ("preference cascade"); his case is 1989 in Eastern Europe (snippet,
  [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur_Kuran)). A mind reader abolishes
  preference falsification by construction, which on Kuran's model means every norm that depends on
  people hiding their dissent falls at once.
- Cannabis: Gallup support for legalisation went from 12% in 1969 to 68% in 2020–21 (snippet,
  [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/356939/support-legal-marijuana-holds-record-high.aspx)).

The cases differ in who is exposed. In the kibbutz and the factory a few deviants are visible to a
watching majority. In the coming-out and Kuran cases everyone's deviance becomes visible together.
The hoped-for norm adaptation is the second kind.

## "No one cares that I smoke pot even though I post pictures about it online?... You get in trouble if someone complains."

**Do police watch social media?**

- Almost all departments use it. The 2016 IACP/Urban Institute survey of 539 agencies found over 90%
  use social media; the survey reports use for gathering evidence in investigations (snippet,
  [NIC summary](https://nicic.gov/2016-law-enforcement-use-social-media-survey)). The usually quoted
  70% "intelligence gathering" figure is unverified.
- Tools: Geofeedia marketed location-based monitoring of public posts to police; after an ACLU of
  California report in October 2016 showed its use on protesters in Oakland and Baltimore, Facebook,
  Instagram and Twitter cut its data access (snippet,
  [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/10/11/FACEBOOK-TWITTER-CUT-OFF-DATA-ACCESS-FOR-GEOFEEDIA-A-SOCIAL-MEDIA-SURVEILLANCE-STARTUP/)).
  The NYPD has since contracted private vendors for the same purpose (snippet,
  [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/brennan-center-files-public-records-requests-information-social-media)).
- NYPD Operation Crew Cut (announced October 2012): monitoring of teenagers' Facebook activity to
  build cases against street "crews". Jelani Henry was arrested for attempted murder "based largely
  on his social media associations and pictures" (snippet, Brennan Center).
- Individual drug cases: Connor Kennedy, Winslow Township NJ, 2015, charged with possession and
  intent to distribute after police used location data in his Instagram photos of his plants
  ([Philadelphia Magazine](https://www.phillymag.com/news/2015/07/28/marijuana-instagram-arrest-jersey/),
  snippet). Indiana: seven charged after a Facebook photo of marijuana captioned "Come get it"
  ([WKBW](https://www.wkbw.com/news/national/come-get-it-7-arrested-in-indiana-after-man-advertises-marijuana-on-facebook),
  snippet). Bexar County TX, 2022: a 17-year-old's Instagram posts of cash, guns and THC cartridges
  led to a stop and a house search
  ([Fox 7](https://www.fox7austin.com/news/teen-posing-on-instagram-with-drugs-money-guns-leads-to-arrests-in-bexar-county),
  snippet).
- What is missing: every documented case is a grower, a seller, or a gang investigation. I found no
  programme that scrapes for personal-use photos, and no count of how often posts lead to arrests.

**How marijuana enforcement has actually run.**

- Volume: over 870,000 marijuana arrests in 2007 (the peak); 227,108 in 2022; 217,150 in 2023, of
  which 200,306 (84%) for possession only (FBI data via
  [NORML, 2024](https://norml.org/blog/2024/09/25/fbi-nearly-one-quarter-of-all-drug-related-arrests-are-for-marijuana-possession/),
  snippet). 6.1 million arrests from 2010 to 2018
  ([ACLU, "A Tale of Two Countries", 2020](https://www.aclu.org/publications/tale-two-countries-racially-targeted-arrests-era-marijuana-reform),
  snippet).
- Who: "despite roughly equal marijuana usage rates, nationally Blacks are 3.73 times more likely
  than whites to be arrested for marijuana possession"
  ([ACLU, "The War on Marijuana in Black and White", 2013](https://www.aclu.org/publications/report-war-marijuana-black-and-white),
  snippet). The 2020 update: "almost four times".
- How, in the clearest case: New York decriminalised small-quantity possession in 1977 unless the
  marijuana was "burning or in plain public view". Police stops produced "public view" by having
  people empty their pockets. About 50,700 arrests in 2011; 86% of those arrested were Black or
  Latino ([WNYC](https://wnyc.org/story/184666-city-hits-highest-marijuana-arrests-more-decade/),
  [ACLU](https://www.aclu.org/blog/criminal-law-reform/nyc-marijuana-arrests-still-too-high),
  snippets). Nobody complained about these people. The arrests came from where police chose to
  stand.
- The part of the draft that is right: federal possession enforcement has nearly stopped. Federal
  sentences for simple marijuana possession fell from 2,172 in fiscal 2014 to 145 in fiscal 2021,
  and by January 2022 nobody was in federal prison solely for it
  ([US Sentencing Commission, 2023](https://www.ussc.gov/research/research-reports/weighing-impact-simple-possession-marijuana),
  snippet). The Cole memo (2013) told federal prosecutors to deprioritise state-legal marijuana;
  Congress has barred DOJ from spending money against state medical programmes since December 2014;
  when Sessions rescinded the Cole memo in January 2018 little changed
  ([Duane Morris](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/sessions_rescinds_cole_memo_legal_cannabis_policy_0118.html),
  snippet).

## "The state _is not bottlenecked on enforcement capability_ when trying to enforce stupid laws. It is bottlenecked on the will of the people."

### Who has already made the argument

- **Stuntz, "The Pathological Politics of Criminal Law"
  ([Michigan Law Review, 2001](https://michiganlawreview.org/journal/the-pathological-politics-of-criminal-law/)).**
  Opens: "Substantive criminal law defines the conduct that the state punishes. Or does it?" Codes
  are so broad that "police and prosecutors frequently decline to arrest or charge", and the law's
  real function "is to empower prosecutors, who are the criminal justice system's real lawmakers."
  Legislators and prosecutors both gain from broader offences, so codes only grow (snippet). His
  conclusion is the reverse of reassuring: breadth plus discretion means the written law does not
  constrain the state.
- **Robert Jackson, "The Federal Prosecutor"
  ([speech, 1 April 1940](https://thejacksonlist.com/2024/04/01/the-federal-prosecutor-1940/)).**
  The danger is "picking the man and then searching the law books"; "With the law books filled with
  a great assortment of crimes", a prosecutor can find a technical violation by almost anyone. The
  original statement of selective enforcement as the harm.
- **Silverglate, _Three Felonies a Day_ (2009).** Federal law is vague enough that ordinary
  professionals commit crimes unknowingly. The number in the title has no derivation in the book;
  Bryan Caplan, a sympathetic reader, guesses it is closer to three a month
  ([EconLib](https://www.econlib.org/three-felonies-a-day/), snippet). Cite the argument, not the
  number.
- **Zittrain, _The Future of the Internet and How to Stop It_
  ([2008](https://cyber.harvard.edu/publications/2008/The_Future_Of_The_Internet_And_How_To_Stop_It)),
  ch. 5, "Perfect Enforcement".** Devices tethered to their makers let law be enforced by design; in
  a Stanford CIS commentator's summary, perfect enforcement "may lock in mistakes, dampen
  creativity, and upset the balance of power between government and citizen" (snippet). Not read.
- **Mulligan, "Perfect Enforcement of Law: When to Limit and When to Use Technology"
  ([Richmond Journal of Law & Technology, 2008](https://scholarship.richmond.edu/jolt/vol14/iss4/4)).**
  Starts from red-light cameras and ID scanners. I retrieved only the opening of the abstract;
  conclusions not verified.
- **Rich, "Should We Make Crime Impossible?"
  ([Harvard Journal of Law & Public Policy, 2013](https://journals.law.harvard.edu/jlpp/wp-content/uploads/sites/90/2013/04/36_2_795_Rich.pdf)).**
  Defines "impossibility structures" as "government mandates that aim to make certain classes of
  criminal conduct effectively impossible" (p. 796). Two of his costs bear directly on the draft.
  First, lawbreaking is how bad laws get changed: "Virginia's anti-miscegenation law was overturned
  through a challenge to the convictions of Mildred and Richard Loving", and "Perfectly preventing
  criminal conduct can foreclose avenues of positive social change" (p. 827). Second, ordinary
  enforcement is public and that is what keeps law tied to opinion: "the charging, trial, and
  punishment processes provide the opportunity for further public comment and agitation", whereas
  with prevention "the individual may not know to complain of her restricted choices... criminal
  laws enforced through impossibility structures may never evolve along with the underlying social
  norms they are meant to embody" (p. 828). In a companion New York Times op-ed he accepts perfect
  prevention for strict-liability offences like drunk driving and rejects it for most crimes
  (snippet).
- **Hartzog, Conti, Nelson & Shay, "Inefficiently Automated Law Enforcement"
  ([Michigan State Law Review, 2015](https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/publications/inefficiently-automated-law-enforcement)).**
  "Inefficiency is also an effective safeguard against perfectly enforcing laws that were created
  with implicit assumptions of leniency and discretion." Their rule: when you automate one stage of
  enforcement, keep or add friction at another (snippet).
- **Marlinspike, "We Should All Have Something To Hide"
  ([2013](https://moxie.org/2013/06/12/we-should-all-have-something-to-hide.html)).** Closest to the
  draft in subject and tone. "Imagine if there were an alternate dystopian reality where law
  enforcement was 100% effective"; "If perfect law enforcement had been a reality in MN, CO, and WA
  since their founding in the 1850s, it seems quite unlikely that these recent changes would have
  ever come to pass." "How could people have decided that marijuana should be legal, if nobody had
  ever used it?" Legalisation "would probably not have been possible without the ability to break
  the law."
- **Kerr, "An Equilibrium-Adjustment Theory of the Fourth Amendment"
  ([Harvard Law Review, 2011](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/an-equilibrium-adjustment-theory-of-the-fourth-amendment/)).**
  A descriptive claim that institutions respond to capability: when technology "expands government
  power", courts tighten Fourth Amendment protection to restore the earlier balance, and the reverse
  (snippet). The nearest scholarly version of "capability is not the binding constraint", with
  courts, not voters, doing the binding.

The existing literature agrees with the draft's observation (laws on the books far exceed laws
enforced) and mostly disagrees with its conclusion. Stuntz, Rich, Hartzog and Marlinspike all treat
the enforcement gap as something that protects people and that better detection would remove.

### Cases where will was the bottleneck

- **Red-light cameras.** They work: cities that switched them off saw fatal red-light-running
  crashes rise 30% relative to cities that kept them
  ([IIHS, 2016 study](https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/turning-off-red-light-cameras-costs-lives-new-research-shows),
  snippet). Programmes still fell from 533 communities in 2012 to 421 in 2018
  ([IIHS, 2018](https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/new-guidelines-for-automated-enforcement-programs-emphasize-safety-amid-rise-in-red-light-running-crash-deaths),
  snippet). At the ballot they lost nearly every time: Houston in 2010, about 24 cities in nine
  states by early 2012, bans passing with 70%+ in several
  ([NPR, 2012](https://www.kunc.org/npr-news/2012-02-22/whats-driving-the-backlash-against-traffic-cameras),
  [TTAC, 2014](https://www.thetruthaboutcars.com/2014/11/bb-reject-red-light-cameras-three-states-election-night/),
  snippets).
- **The seatbelt interlock.** In the 1970s NHTSA required new cars not to start unless belts were
  fastened. "Public outcry over the interlocks, including claims of 'Big Brotherism,' led Congress
  to forbid the Department of Transportation from requiring them" (Rich 2013, p. 800).
- **Tax.** Third-party reporting makes much evasion detectable. Between 2010 and 2019 audits of
  millionaires fell 71%, the overall audit rate fell 58%, and enforcement staff fell by about a
  third, all from budget choices; the net tax gap was $496 billion a year for 2014–2016
  ([CBPP](https://www.cbpp.org/research/federal-tax/the-need-to-rebuild-the-depleted-irs),
  snippets).
- **Dead-letter morals laws.** Adultery was still a crime in about 15–17 states in 2024, with
  prosecutions rare; New York repealed its 1907 statute that year
  ([Justia Verdict, 2024](https://verdict.justia.com/2024/12/05/the-end-of-the-affair-adultery-in-modern-law),
  snippet). Sodomy statutes before _Lawrence v. Texas_ (2003) were similarly unenforced (unverified
  here).
- **Federal cannabis**, above: the capability to prosecute never changed and the prosecutions went
  from 2,172 to 145 a year.
- **The polygraph itself.** Employers had a detection capability, used it two million times a year,
  and Congress removed it.

### Cases where capability was the bottleneck

- **Prohibition.** Usually told as a failure of will, but the federal force was 1,520 agents in 1920
  and 2,836 in 1930 for the whole country; they still made 539,759 arrests from 1921 to 1929, and
  Prohibition cases in federal court rose from 29,114 in 1921 to 65,960 in 1932
  ([Mob Museum](https://prohibition.themobmuseum.org/the-history/enforcing-the-prohibition-laws/),
  snippets). The state enforced a law most people broke as hard as its capacity allowed for thirteen
  years.
- **Xinjiang.** Human Rights Watch, "China's Algorithms of Repression"
  ([2019](https://www.hrw.org/news/2019/05/01/china-how-mass-surveillance-works-xinjiang)): the IJOP
  platform "aggregates data about people and flags to officials those it deems potentially
  threatening", down to phones carrying any of 51 listed apps including WhatsApp and VPNs; up to one
  million people in camps (snippet). The laws and the will predate the system. The scale does not.
- **The Stasi.** 91,015 full-time staff ([Stasi Records Archive](https://bstu.bund.de/en/faqs/),
  snippet) and about 174,000–189,000 informants in 1989, with card files on six million people
  ([American Archivist, 2012](https://american-archivist.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/aarc/75/1/article-p81.pdf),
  snippet), in a country of 16 million. The brief's claim that it was limited mainly by analyst
  labour: I found no source that says so. Unverified.
- **Immigration enforcement through purchased data.** Georgetown, "American Dragnet"
  ([2022](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/privacy-technology-center/publications/american-dragnet-data-driven-deportation-in-the-21st-century/)):
  ICE has driver's-licence data on 3 in 4 US adults, has run face recognition on the licence photos
  of 1 in 3, and can locate 3 in 4 through utility records; where states barred direct access, it
  bought the same data from brokers; $2.8 billion spent on surveillance technology since 2008
  (snippets). No legislature voted for any of that.
- **Genetic genealogy.** Since the Golden State Killer arrest in 2018, 545 cases solved by the end
  of 2022
  ([Criminal Legal News, 2023](https://criminallegalnews.org/news/2023/aug/1/forensic-genetic-genealogy-has-solved-545-cases-and-counting),
  snippet). The will to solve those murders was constant for decades.
- **Contact tracing.** Singapore told the public TraceTogether data was for contact tracing only,
  then confirmed in January 2021 that police could obtain it under the ordinary Criminal Procedure
  Code and had used it in a murder case
  ([NPR, 2021](https://www.kunc.org/npr-news/2021-01-05/singapore-says-covid-19-contact-tracing-data-can-be-requested-by-police),
  snippet).
- **Supervised populations.** Sex offenders on licence in England and Wales are polygraphed as
  routine, and US parole and treatment programmes do the same (above; how many states: not checked).
  Where the target cannot object, the tool is used.
- Licence-plate readers and DNA databases belong on this list; I did not collect numbers for them.

### Selective enforcement

The argument, from Jackson (1940) through Stuntz (2001): a broad code plus discretion means the
state can already charge nearly anyone it has chosen, and the practical limit is the cost of finding
the violation. A detector lowers that cost per person. On this account the number of people punished
may not rise at all; what changes is how cheaply the state can pick someone first and find the
offence second. The New York "public view" arrests are the worked example: a decriminalised act, a
discretionary stop, and 86% of the arrests landing on two groups.
