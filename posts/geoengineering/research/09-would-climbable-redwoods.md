## Headline Findings

- **It exists, twice over.** Western Australia has let the public free-solo three pegged karri trees
  since the 1940s, to platforms around 50 m up, with no harness and no staff. And the exact thing
  Robbie describes (climbing holds on living trees plus auto-belays) is a paid attraction at
  [Snowbird, Utah](https://snowbird.com/summer-activities/tree-climb) and
  [Holiday Valley, New York](https://www.holidayvalley.com/activities/climbing-forest/) (nine trees,
  up to 45 ft, $24).
- **The tree would survive the bolts. Whether it stays sound for 80 years is less clear.** Trees
  wall off wounds instead of healing them, and treehouse builders hang 9,000 to 12,000 lb off a
  single bolt. But all three Australian trees eventually developed rot: the Diamond Tree in its base
  (closed for good in 2019), the Gloucester Tree in its top (shortened in 1987-88 and by another 7 m
  around 2024). Nothing I found pins the rot on the pegs, and nothing clears them.
- **The Australian trees are open again, lower.** Gloucester reopened on 27 June 2026 with a new
  platform at 37 m (it used to be 53 m) after a A$3 million rebuild covering two trees. The
  Bicentennial Tree is capped at 20 m and closed for repegging until the end of November 2026. Still
  no harness.
- **Outdoor auto-belays exist and the manufacturer approves outdoor use.** But the long model tops
  out at a 20 m mounting height, each unit goes back to the factory every 12 months ($269 to $319
  plus shipping), and the accident record is almost entirely people forgetting to clip in. For an
  unstaffed 45 m tree a continuous lifeline is the better fit.
- **The UW rock is not bouldering only.** UW says about 85% of it is bouldering, with "a few
  anchors" for top-roping. It is 32 ft at its highest, built in 1976 for $60,000, free, and
  officially reserved for UW students, faculty and staff.
- **San Francisco already has free roped climbing on city park land:** the Beaver Street Wall in
  Corona Heights Park, a 60 ft slab with chains bolted at the top, next to a playground.
- **California law already names this activity.** Government Code 831.7 lists "tree climbing" and
  "rock climbing" as hazardous recreational activities for which public entities are immune. The
  exceptions are the catch: no immunity for negligent construction or maintenance of a structure
  built for the activity, and none where a specific fee is charged. Bolting holds to a tree moves
  the city from the strong immunity to the weak one.
- **Why don't people do this?** In my order of importance: nobody's job is to propose it and the
  Park Code default is "no climbing trees without permission"; San Francisco's real tree liability
  is limbs falling on people ($14.5 million settlement in 2018), and this gathers people under a
  tree by invitation; a built route needs inspection money forever; and Golden Gate Park's redwoods
  are about 100 years old, probably 30 to 45 m, and nearly all are memorials. The counterargument is
  structural: a 45 m tree is a free 45 m tower, and the tallest free public artificial climbing
  structures in the US are about 10 m.

## 1. Does It Already Exist?

### The Western Australian Climbing Trees

Before spotter planes, Western Australia's forestry department ran a network of
[eight lookout trees and ten towers](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/site/gloucester-tree)
across the karri forest, built between 1937 and 1952. A forester climbed a tall karri (_Eucalyptus
diversicolor_), lopped the crown, built a cabin on top, and drove a spiral ladder of pegs into the
trunk. Three became tourist attractions.

**How they were built.** For the Gloucester Tree, forester Jack Watson first climbed it in boots and
a belt, [taking six hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucester_Tree). Forester George Reynolds
then bored holes with a wood auger and drove in the pegs. The visiting Duke of Gloucester had a go
with the auger, which is where the name comes from. The spacing works out to 53 m / 153 pegs = 0.35
m of rise per peg. Wire mesh was added outside the pegs later; a local who climbed as a child
[told the ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-01-21/gloucester-bicentennial-tree-climbs-closed-tourism-fears/103309592)
there were once "just the pegs". The Bicentennial Tree was never a working lookout. It was pegged in
1988 as a tourist attraction for Australia's bicentenary.

**Rules.** No harness, no staff, no fee beyond park entry. Current signage
[limits Gloucester to 15 climbers at once](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/site/gloucester-tree)
and the Bicentennial platform to three, sets a 100 kg weight limit, bans thongs and backpacks, and
says "Your safety is our concern but your responsibility." One visitor told the ABC her
five-year-old climbed it in flip-flops.

**Usage.** [Only about 20% of visitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucester_Tree) went all the
way up. By 1963, [over 3,000 people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucester_National_Park) had
climbed it. I could not find a modern annual figure (tourism pages claim "over 100,000 a year",
unverified).

**Injury record.** I found no documented fall death and no official statistics. The only statement
is hearsay in a 2013
[Tripadvisor review](https://www.tripadvisor.com.au/ShowUserReviews-g261676-d1554755-r162501057-Gloucester_Tree-Pemberton_Western_Australia.html)
titled "a throwback to pre public liability insurance days": apparently nobody has died on the
climb, and two people had heart attacks after coming down. None of the ABC's closure coverage
mentions an accident.

**How the trees fared.** Mixed, and this is what matters for the redwood idea.

- Diamond Tree: an arborist using ultrasound found the heartwood affected by fungus, and
  [assessments "found rot in the base of the tree"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Tree).
  Closed in 2019 after about 80 years of pegs.
- Gloucester Tree: in 1987-88 the
  [top was found unstable due to fungi and the tree was shortened](https://www.firelookoutsdownunder.com/Western%20Australia/gloucester.html).
  In 2023 arborists found
  [the upper canopy in decline](https://www.dbca.wa.gov.au/management/park-and-infrastructure-projects/south-west-climbing-trees-project),
  and the top was
  [cut down a further 7 m because of rot](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-04-16/gloucester-tree-climb-wa-to-be-rebuilt-lower-height/105183702).
- The 2023 closure itself was about steel, not wood:
  [engineers found structural issues with the steel platforms](https://southernforestsandvalleys.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2023_614_Pemberton_Climbing_Trees_FactSheet_final_V2.pdf)
  on both remaining trees.

A lookout tree is a topped tree, and topping opens a large wound at the top of the stem, so I would
guess the top rot comes from the lopping more than the pegs (my inference). The Diamond Tree's base
rot is harder to explain away.

**The closure and rebuild.** Both trees closed in November 2023 with an "around 12 months" estimate,
and the department
[did not rule out permanent closure](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-01-21/gloucester-bicentennial-tree-climbs-closed-tourism-fears/103309592).
The state committed
[A$3 million](https://www.dbca.wa.gov.au/management/park-and-infrastructure-projects/south-west-climbing-trees-project)
in 2024. The new Gloucester platform is about 13 m², holds 15 people, and "wraps around the trunk"
to [minimise impact on the living tree](https://www.thegreynomads.com.au/karri-2/). Reopening took
2.6 years.

**Liability.** Western Australia's
[Civil Liability Act 2002](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/prod/filestore.nsf/FileURL/mrdoc_46545.pdf/$FILE/Civil%20Liability%20Act%202002%20-%20%5B04-k0-02%5D.pdf)
has a section 5H (no liability for harm from obvious risks of dangerous recreational activities) and
5I (no liability where a risk warning was given), which is what the signage is for. But the trees
were open for 55 years before that Act existed, so for most of their history the government just
carried the risk. The Shire of Manjimup's president said in 2025 that she wonders
["how this is even still insurable in this day and age"](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-04-16/gloucester-tree-climb-wa-to-be-rebuilt-lower-height/105183702).

## 2. Tree Biology

**Wounding.** Alex Shigo's CODIT model
([Shigo and Marx, 1977](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartmentalization_of_decay_in_trees)) says
trees do not heal, they compartmentalize. A wound triggers four "walls": wall 1 plugs the vessels
above and below, walls 2 and 3 resist spread inward and sideways, and wall 4, the strongest, is new
wood laid down after the injury that seals the old wood off from everything grown later. So a bolt
hole becomes a sealed pocket and the tree grows sound wood around it. The damaged volume is never
repaired.

**Why few big bolts beat many small ones.** Wounds close together get walled off as one. Treehouse
builders' rule:
[one large fastener is far better than a dozen small ones](https://www.treetopbuilders.net/fasteners/),
never more than one per square foot, minimum 1 inch diameter. A cluster of lag screws can make the
tree abandon the whole patch.

**The treehouse attachment bolt.** The
[Garnier limb, or TAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garnier_limb), is a 1.25 inch alloy steel bolt
with a 3 inch collar. The collar is the trick: it seats in a counterbore in the sapwood and bears
against the compressive strength of the wood grain, so the bolt acts like a stubby artificial branch
instead of a nail levering in a hole. Rated loads are 9,000 to 12,000 lb. In 2016 tests at
Washington State University, a heavy TAB with a suspension backup took
[over 50,000 lb to fail](https://nelsontreehouse.com/blog/tab-testing/). For scale, OSHA's
fall-arrest anchor requirement is
[5,000 lb per person](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.502).

**Straps versus bolts.** Counterintuitively, wrapping is worse. A strap or cable around a trunk
compresses the phloem and cambium around the whole circumference as the tree thickens, which is
girdling. A bolt wounds one spot and leaves the rest of the ring alone.

**Coast redwood specifics.**

- Bark is
  [up to 12 inches thick](https://www.nationalparkstraveler.org/park/subpage/anatomy-redwood-tree),
  soft and fibrous. You cannot mount a hold on it. Any fastener has to pass through the bark and
  seat in sapwood, so a climbing hold becomes a standoff bracket. On a 100-year-old planted tree the
  bark is much thinner, perhaps 5 to 10 cm (my estimate).
- Bark and heartwood are
  [rich in tannins that insects and fungi avoid](https://www.nationalparkstraveler.org/park/subpage/anatomy-redwood-tree),
  which makes redwood about the best species there is for tolerating bolt holes.
- Roots are the weak point: only
  [10 to 13 ft deep but spreading 60 to 80 ft](https://www.npshistory.com/publications/muwo/brochures/1991.pdf).
  Foot traffic compacts the soil over them. Muir Woods replaced asphalt with boardwalk because
  [compacted soil under paths disrupted water flow](https://www.parksconservancy.org/node/320).
- Hyperion was never open to climbers, only visited. Trampling still killed the ferns around its
  base, and in 2022 the park closed the area with a
  [$5,000 fine and up to six months in jail](https://www.npr.org/2022/08/01/1114846960/hyperion-tree-off-limits-fine).

A popular climbing tree gets hundreds of people a day standing in one spot. I think the root zone is
the realistic way to kill the tree, and decking solves it.

**What canopy scientists do.** Steve Sillett's group at Cal Poly Humboldt gets a line into the crown
by
[shooting an arrow with fishing line over a high branch](https://www.nwf.org/Magazines/National-Wildlife/2008/Climbing-Into-an-Unexplored-Frontier),
hauling up a rope, and ascending the rope, never the trunk. Arborists reserve climbing spurs for
trees being removed because each gaff strike
[punctures the cambium and can carry disease between trees](https://mdc.mo.gov/node/115). In old
growth the larger worry is the canopy: repeated passage
[wears "canopy trails" into bark and strips moss, lichen and fern mats](https://www.mdvaden.com/redwood_climbing.shtml)
that weigh hundreds of kilograms per tree. A 100-year-old park redwood has little of that to lose.

## 3. The Actual Trees

**I could not find a published height for any Golden Gate Park redwood.** What exists:

- [Heroes Grove](https://localnewsmatters.org/2019/05/27/memorial-redwood-grove-in-golden-gate-park-rededicated-on-centennial/):
  first tree planted by John McLaren on 30 May 1919 as a World War I memorial. About 107 years old.
- [Hoover Redwood Grove](https://www.savetheredwoods.org/blog/path/4-peaceful-redwood-groves-in-san-francisco):

- The National AIDS Memorial Grove and the Botanical Garden's Redwood Grove: similar vintage.

My estimate is 30 to 45 m (100 to 150 ft). Forestry yield tables index redwood sites by height at
age 100, and good sites reach around 200 ft (unverified), but the park is windy sand with fog as its
only summer water. That is two to three times a gym wall and roughly the Gloucester Tree's new
platform.

**The park's tree-failure problem.** Golden Gate Park has about
[27,000 trees](https://abc7news.com/post/sf-golden-gate-park-tree-fall-eucalyptus-arborist-trimming/14330788/)
and its original forest is aging out together. Monterey pine typically lives
[80 to 100 years and Monterey cypress 100 to 150](https://arboristnow.com/news/farewell-to-a-giant-remembering-the-cypress-of-hellman-hollow/);
the earliest plantings are about 155. In January 2024 a nearly 100 ft eucalyptus uprooted and
crushed five cars. In October 2025 a
[90 ft cypress at Hellman Hollow](https://sfist.com/2025/10/30/sf-rec-parks-bids-fond-farewell-to-giant-cypress-that-had-to-be-taken-down-in-golden-gate-park)
was removed after its trunk split. In March 2023 a branch from a tree at the park's edge
[killed a woman at a Muni stop on Lincoln Way](https://exlitem.com/jury-verdicts-settlements/tree-branch-kills-woman-at-bus-stop-jury-clears-city-in-sf);
a jury cleared the city. And in 2018 the city paid
[$14.5 million](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/sf-supes-pay-14-5-million-to-woman-paralyzed-by-falling-tree-limb/)
to a woman paralyzed by a 100 lb pine limb in Washington Square, from a tree the city's arborists
maintained was structurally sound.

**Which species.** Redwood. It has a single straight stem and decay-resistant wood, and it is the
one park species that is young relative to its lifespan. Blue gum is taller but sheds bark in strips
and drops limbs. Monterey pine and cypress are at end of life.

**Which grove.** Nearly every redwood stand in the park is a memorial. Heroes Grove and Hoover Grove
honor war dead, and the AIDS Memorial Grove is a national memorial. Bolting a climbing route to one
is a different political proposition from bolting it to an anonymous tree. The Botanical Garden's
Redwood Grove is not a memorial and sits inside an already gated, staffed site.

**Elsewhere.** Muir Woods and the Presidio are federal, so Government Code 831.7 does not apply
there. Oakland's Reinhardt Redwood Regional Park is second growth from 1850s logging, taller and
denser than anything in San Francisco (heights unverified). The Santa Cruz Mountains already host
the commercial versions.

## 4. Outdoor Auto-Belays

**How the dominant design works.** A TRUBLUE has a spring-loaded drum of webbing. When you fall, the
drum spins a rotor of conductive arms past permanent magnets. A conductor moving through a magnetic
field has eddy currents induced in it, and those currents create a field opposing the motion, so
braking force rises with speed. The arms also swing outward as speed rises, pushing more metal into
the field. That is why one device lowers a 10 kg child and a 150 kg adult at about the same rate,
with
[no contacting wear parts and no brake dust](https://trubluetech.com/us/en/adventure/blog/what-makes-trublue-auto-belay-tru).
(The manufacturer's public pages say only "magnetic braking"; the eddy-current detail is from my own
knowledge and is unverified here.) Competing units use friction brakes, which wear.

**Outdoors.** The manufacturer says the unit
["holds up to the elements and can be installed in an outdoor environment"](https://trubluetech.com/us/en/adventure/resources/faq).
The stated outdoor failure mode is debris jamming the webbing nozzle so the line does not retract. I
found no published guidance on salt fog or temperature, which is the relevant question at the ocean
end of the park. Snowbird, Holiday Valley and Maggie Daley Park all run them outdoors seasonally.

**The accident record.** The devices almost never fail. People start climbing without clipping in.

- The Climbing Wall Association:
  ["the majority of auto-belay-related incidents and deaths are not a result of the malfunction of the auto-belay itself"](https://www.cwapro.org/blog/the-future-of-auto-belays).
  It counts at least two deaths in the three years to January 2024 and one $6 million settlement.
- The American Alpine Club's report on a 2021
  [40 ft fatal fall in Colorado](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201216242)
  found no equipment fault, and lists a 2014 Colorado death and a 2021 Sydney death of the same
  kind.
- Norway has the only real data. Forgot-to-clip incidents rose from
  [1 in 2015 to 20 in 2023](https://www.theuiaa.org/reversing-autobelay-accidents-in-norway/). The
  federation published guidelines on 1 March 2024 (rectangular belay gates in place of triangular
  ones, nowhere else to park the carabiner, auto-belay lanes on their own wall sections) and 2024
  ended with 7.
- The gate is the engineering control: a barrier across the start holds that the carabiner clips to,
  so you must unclip it to get on the wall. The CWA's
  [updated guidance](https://www.cwapro.org/blog/updated-auto-belay-guidance-released) requires one.

**The recall.** The one manufacturer failure on record is C3 Manufacturing's Perfect Descent
Model 220. C3 told purchasers to stop using affected units on
[21 October 2019](https://www.theuiaa.org/?p=74164) over retraction springs, and the
[CPSC recall followed on 19 March 2020](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2020/C3-Manufacturing-Recalls-Perfect-Descent-Climbing-Systems-Climbing-Belay-Devices-Due-to-Fall-Hazard),
covering units made February 2017 to April 2019. A broken spring means slack in the line, which
means a real fall. I did not find a separate 2021 recall.

## 5. Free Public Outdoor Climbing Structures

for top-rope. It is free, "climb-at-your-own-risk", open year-round, and reserved for UW students,

The Redmond bridge is the most instructive. A Smith Rock climber proposed bolting gym-style holds
under a city bridge arch. The parks commission approved unanimously; a city official said she was
surprised it passed risk review, and that Oregon law "shields public agencies" as long as nobody
pays. It opened in July 2015. In March 2016 the Oregon Supreme Court held that recreational immunity
did not cover public employees, and the city's insurer
[refused to keep covering the bridge](https://bendbulletin.com/2016/09/20/redmond-halts-climbing-on-maple-avenue-bridge).
It closed in July 2016 and [reopened on 1 July 2017](https://redmondspokesman.com/?p=66451), after
the legislative session the city had been waiting on. The same thing happened statewide in 2023,
when an appeals ruling narrowed the immunity and the insurer told coastal towns to
[consider closing trails](https://opb.org/article/2024/02/13/public-hiking-trails-recreation-outdoors-legal-lawsuit),
until a
[2024 fix](https://opb.org/article/2024/03/05/oregon-legislature-passes-temporary-recreational-immunity-law-liability-lawsuits).
The immunity statute is what keeps these places open.

Burnside in Portland is a skatepark, not a climbing spot: skaters poured concrete under the bridge
[without permission in 1990](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnside_Skatepark) and the city later
accepted it.

## 6. Why Don't People Do This?

**The liability law is better than its reputation.** California
[Government Code 831.7](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_gov%27t_code_section_831.7) says a
public entity is not liable to anyone who participates in a "hazardous recreational activity", and
the statutory list includes "rock climbing", "tree climbing" and "tree rope swinging" by name.
[Section 831.2](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/government-code/gov-sect-831-2/) adds immunity for
injuries caused by a natural condition of unimproved public property.

The exceptions are where this proposal lives. Under 831.7(c)(1) the immunity does not apply to:

- (A) failure to warn of a known dangerous condition that the participant would not reasonably
  assume is part of the activity;
- (B) cases where permission to participate was granted for a specific fee (general park admission
  and parking do not count);
- (C) injury caused by negligent failure to properly construct or maintain any structure or
  equipment used in the activity;
- (D) and (E) reckless promotion and gross negligence.

So a kid who shins up a redwood and falls is covered. A city that bolts holds and an auto-belay to
the same tree has built a structure (exception C), has improved the property (831.2 gone), and if it
charges per climb, has taken a fee (exception B). What remains is ordinary negligence law on whether
the thing was built and maintained properly, the same exposure as a playground. That is manageable
but real, and it makes the free, unstaffed version legally easier than the paid, supervised one.

**The Park Code says no by default, and yes by permit.** San Francisco
[Park Code section 4.01(f)](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/san_francisco/latest/sf_park/0-0-0-130)
makes it unlawful, "without permission of the Recreation and Park Department", to "climb or lie upon
any tree, shrub, monument, wall, fence, shelter, fountain, statue, building, construction or
structure".
[Section 10.01](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/san_francisco/latest/sf_park/0-0-0-596) sets
the penalty: an infraction up to $100 for a first offense and $200 for a second within a year, or a
misdemeanor up to $1,000 or six months, at the District Attorney's choice (amounts unverified
against the page itself). No ordinance change is needed. The department can give permission.

**The skatepark precedent.** In the early 1990s California cities said they could not build
skateparks, and some banned skating outright; Carlsbad did so after a 1993 immunity bill died. The
1997 fix was
[AB 1296](https://www.leginfo.ca.gov/pub/97-98/bill/asm/ab_1251-1300/ab_1296_cfa_19970716_170237_sen_comm.html),
sponsored by the cities' risk pools, which treated skateboarding at a public skate facility as a
hazardous recreational activity. It passed the Assembly 58 to 9 and the Senate 37 to 0 and was
[signed on 29 September 1997](https://www.leginfo.ca.gov/pub/97-98/bill/asm/ab_1251-1300/ab_1296_bill_19970930_chaptered.html),
with conditions (helmets and pads, an age floor) and a sunset that was extended and then
[removed in 2011](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB264/2011). The trial lawyers opposing it could find
only two California skateboard lawsuits, and the one cost figure in the analysis was Moreno Valley's
$31,000. The blocker was perceived exposure and uninsurability, not actual judgments. Once risk
managers had a statute to point to, building became routine; the Tony Hawk Foundation counted about
[3,500 US skateparks by 2018](https://legallysociable.com/tag/skateboards/).

Tree climbing is already on the list. The statute skateboarding needed, climbing has had all along.

**Smaller factors.** Insurance: San Francisco largely self-insures (unverified), which removes the
veto that closed the Redmond bridge. ADA: Marymoor's 1994 design included a route for disabled
climbers, and the path to the base would need to be accessible; I found no technical standard for
the climb itself (unverified).

## 7. Design Sketch and Cost (My Estimate)

**Concept.** Do not put 130 pegs or 200 holds into the tree. Hang a narrow steel or aluminum spine
down the trunk on a few large bolts, standing off the bark, and put everything else on the spine.

- **Tree:** a healthy 40 to 45 m coast redwood, not a memorial, passed by an independent arborist
  using sonic tomography of the lower trunk and an aerial inspection.
- **Attachment:** spine in 3 m sections, two TABs each. 45 m / 3 m = 15 sections, so 30 bolts,
  against about 130 pegs for the Australian method (45 m / 0.35 m). Slotted brackets let the trunk
  thicken and sway.
- **Route:** bolt-on holds on panels fixed to the spine, resettable like a gym, or plain rungs for a
  via ferrata version.
- **Protection:** small platforms at 15 m, 30 m and the top. Either two XL auto-belays per pitch
  (six units, gates on each start), or a continuous cable with a locking sleeve for the unstaffed
  version, or just lead bolts and top anchors for bring-your-own-rope.
- **Top:** a deck below the live crown. Do not top the tree.
- **Base:** raised boardwalk over the root zone, roughly 80 m², and a fence so nobody stands on bare
  soil.
- **Inspection:** arborist twice a year and after major windstorms; hardware check annually;
  auto-belays to the factory annually.

Cross-checks: Western Australia spent A$3 million on two trees, roughly US$1 million each.
Marymoor's rock cost $350,000 in 1994, about $770,000 today (CPI factor about 2.2 from the
[BLS calculator](https://www.bls.gov/data/inflation_calculator.htm), my approximation). A whole
commercial treetop park runs
[$1.6 million](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2019/apr/16/construction-underway-on-16m-adventure-park-near-o/).
They agree on about $1 million.

**Annual cost.** Unstaffed: arborist $8,000, structural inspection $6,000, auto-belay service 6 x
$269 to $723 = $1,600 to $4,300, holds and webbing $6,000, so about $25,000 a year. Staffed like
Maggie Daley: two attendants x 8 h x 250 days x $40 per hour loaded = $160,000 a year on top.

**The artificial alternative.** A concrete rock like UW's ($60,000 in 1976, about $340,000 today at
a CPI factor of 5.7) or Marymoor's gets you 10 m with no tree risk and almost no maintenance. It is
the obviously sensible thing and San Francisco does not have one. But nothing artificial gets you 45
m for $1 million. The tree is the cheap way to get the height because it already is the tower, and
it has been wind-tested for a century.

## 8. Verdict (My Judgment)

**Could it be done?** Yes. Every component is already in public use somewhere.

**The actual blocker,** in order:

1. Nobody has asked, and the default answer in the Park Code is no. Every example above started with
   one named person: Clark Schurman, the UW climbers, Ian Caldwell in Redmond.
2. The city's fear is not a climber falling, which the law handles. It is the tree or a limb failing
   with people gathered under it, after the city made the tree a destination and drilled holes in
   it. That is the Washington Square case with worse facts.
3. A recurring maintenance line with no revenue. Western Australia shows how a deferred steel
   problem becomes a multi-year closure and a seven-figure rebuild.
4. The best trees are memorials.

Liability for the climbing itself is the perceived blocker and mostly not the real one.

**Who would have to say yes.** The Recreation and Park Department can grant permission under
4.01(f). Anything permanent would go to the Recreation and Park Commission, with the department's
forestry staff and the City Attorney's risk managers as the practical vetoes. The Urban Forestry
Council is advisory and handles landmark-tree nominations, with
[final say at the Board of Supervisors](https://www.ronhenggeler.com/Newsletters/2017/3.29/Newsletter.html);
its support would help but is not required. The park's historic status may add a preservation review
(unverified).

**Smallest pilot that might get approved.**

1. **No hardware.** A permitted outfitter runs rope-only canopy climbs in one grove for a few
   weekends, with its own insurance. This is what Tree Climbing Planet sells for $250 in the Santa
   Cruz Mountains. One permit, nothing left behind.
2. **One pitch.** Snowbird-style holds and a single auto-belay on the lowest 12 m of one redwood,
   fenced, staffed on weekends, decking over the roots, four to six bolts. Probably under $100,000.
3. **A concrete rock.** Skip the tree and build San Francisco its Marymoor. Easier to approve, and
   it creates the constituency that would later ask for the tree.

The 45 m version would follow from a few uneventful years of the second.

## Best Anecdotes

- Forester Jack Watson spent six hours climbing a 58 m karri in boots and a belt to check the view.
  The visiting Duke of Gloucester then picked up the auger and bored some of the peg holes himself,
  and the tree got his name.
- For 75 years the Western Australian government let anyone climb 53 m up a spiral of steel pegs
  with no harness. A mother recalls her five-year-old doing it in flip-flops. The sign still reads
  "Your safety is our concern but your responsibility."
- A tourism operator built a harnessed high-ropes course next to the Gloucester Tree because of how
  many people came to climb the unharnessed one. The tree then closed for two and a half years.
- The UW rock exists because students kept climbing university buildings.
- Redmond, Oregon let climbers bolt gym holds under a city bridge. A court case about a jogger
  stepping in a hole in a Portland park then closed the bridge for a year, because the city's
  insurer walked.
- Opponents of California's 1997 skatepark immunity bill went looking for the lawsuits cities said
  they feared and found two.
- San Francisco's tallest listed tree, a 218 ft blue gum in Stern Grove, vanished after a 2021 water
  main flood. A seven-year-old with a stick and a tape measure is trying to find its successor.
