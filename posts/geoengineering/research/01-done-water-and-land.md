## Headline Findings

- The Netherlands is
  [26% below sea level and 55% flood-prone](https://www.pbl.nl/en/correction-wording-flood-risks-for-the-netherlands-in-ipcc-report)
  (PBL), and a large part of that is self-inflicted: draining peat makes it oxidize and sink, at
  [about 8 mm/yr on average](https://undark.org/2020/01/15/windmills-netherlands-sinking/) today.
  The country spends
  [just over €11 billion a year, about 1% of GDP](https://www.dutchnews.nl/2026/09/netherlands-must-double-water-spending-delta-commissioner-warns/),
  on water, and in September 2026 its Delta Commissioner said that has to double.
- The Mississippi's alternative route to the Gulf via the Atchafalaya is
  [150 miles shorter and more than twice as steep](https://www.wunderground.com/cat6/Americas-Achilles-Heel-Mississippi-Rivers-Old-River-Control-Structure).
  One complex of concrete gates holds the river in place. In April 1973 a scour hole about 50 feet
  deep undermined 7 of the 11 gates on the main structure, which has been limited to 60% of its
  former flow ever since.
- The price of pinning the Mississippi: coastal Louisiana lost
  [2,006 square miles of land from 1932 to 2016, about 25% of its 1932 land area](https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/usgs-louisianas-rate-coastal-wetland-loss-continues-slow)
  (USGS, 2017). The flagship fix, the ~$3 billion Mid-Barataria Sediment Diversion, was
  [cancelled in July 2025](https://www.audubon.org/news/louisiana-pulls-plug-nations-largest-ecosystem-restoration-project)
  with roughly
  [$600 million already spent](https://www.ehn.org/louisiana-cancels-3-billion-coastal-restoration-project-as-seas-rise).
- The Erie Canal cost [$7.1 million](https://eriecanal.org/texts/NYS1961/chap1.html), cut Buffalo to
  New York freight from
  [$100 a ton to under $10, and repaid its debt from tolls in nine years](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Erie-Canal/From-commercial-artery-to-national-symbol).
  Nothing else on this list has a payback period that short.
- Los Angeles has spent
  [more than $2.5 billion](https://ladwp.com/who-we-are/water-system/los-angeles-aqueduct/owens-lake)
  suppressing dust on the lakebed it dried out. The aqueduct that dried it was funded by about
  [$24 million of bonds](https://www.history.com/articles/water-wars-los-angeles-aquaduct), roughly
  $0.8 billion today, so the cleanup has cost about three times the project.
- The cheapest large restoration here: a project whose centerpiece was a 13 km dike, funded by a
  [$64.5 million World Bank credit](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/888771468089960580/pdf/462360BRI0Box31e0no0FS231Kazakhstan.pdf)
  (2008 report) raised the North Aral fish catch from 50 tons to 2,000 tons in three years. The
  catch: the North Aral now holds
  [about 23 km³](https://timesca.com/astana-accelerates-northern-aral-sea-recovery-plan/), 2% of the
  [1,100 km³](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aral_Sea) the whole sea held in 1960.
- The Salton Sea was created by an irrigation accident and is now being killed by irrigation
  efficiency. Farm runoff was its only inflow, and the 2003 farm-to-city water transfer cut it.
- Chicago opened its river-reversing canal on
  [January 2, 1900, ahead of Missouri's application for an injunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Sanitary_and_Ship_Canal).
  126 years later the same canal needs a
  [$1.15 billion fish barrier](https://csgmidwest.org/2025/01/09/construction-to-begin-on-long-sought-project-to-keep-invasive-carp-out-of-the-great-lakes),
  whose construction was
  [halted for federal review in August 2026](https://www.michiganpublic.org/environment-climate-change/2026-08-20/invasive-carp-get-closer-to-lake-michigan-as-project-to-halt-their-spread-is-ordered-to-stop).
- Mexico City, built on a drained lake, sinks
  [up to 50 cm a year](https://eos.org/research-spotlights/the-looming-crisis-of-sinking-ground-in-mexico-city)
  (Chaussard et al., 2021). Its 1900 gravity drain lost its slope and now needs pumps.

## Premise Check

- **"Dutch levies."** The word is dikes (or levees). "Drained their swamps" is true but backwards as
  a success story: draining the peat swamps from the Middle Ages on is what sank the western
  Netherlands below sea level. The famous sea reclamation is small: the 20th-century polders total
  [1,650 km²](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuiderzee_Works), about 5% of the land area. The
  often-quoted "17% reclaimed" I could not source.
- **"Kept the Mississippi in Louisiana."** Both routes are in Louisiana. What the Corps did was keep
  the river flowing past Baton Rouge and New Orleans instead of past Morgan City.
- **"Canals in USA (Erie, Idaho)."** Different kinds of thing. Erie is a transport canal. Idaho's
  are irrigation canals, which is desert conversion, not freight.
- **Lake Chad as an engineering failure.** Mostly wrong. The collapse from
  [25,000 km² in 1963 to 1,350 km² by 2001](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2001/02/010228080245.htm)
  was mainly Sahel drought. Coe and Foley (2001) put irrigation at about 5% of the 1966-75 decline
  and about half of the 1983-94 one. And
  [the lake has been stable for two decades](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-62417-w) (Pham-Duc
  et al., 2020).
- **Aral Sea as an accident.** It was planned. Soviet experts
  [expected the sea to shrink and some called it "nature's error"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aral_Sea).
- **Chicago's reversal as a clean public-health win.** The 1885 epidemic that supposedly triggered
  it [did not happen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Sanitary_and_Ship_Canal), and a
  [2025 preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.10.14.25337993.full.pdf) using
  ward-level deaths finds the immediate typhoid effect was smaller than the legend.

## Netherlands

**How a polder works.** Ring a low area with a dike, cut ditches through it, and pump the ditch
water up into a _boezem_, a network of canals held above the land that stores water until it can be
sluiced or pumped to the sea. Windmills did the lifting from the 1400s, then steam, now electric and
diesel stations. There are
[about 3,000 polders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation_in_the_Netherlands). The
governance is as old as the hardware: water boards date to the 13th century, numbered
[about 3,500 in 1850 and have merged down to around two dozen](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_board_(Netherlands)>),
and they tax the people they protect.

**Zuiderzee Works.** The [Afsluitdijk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuiderzee_Works), 32 km long,
built 1927-1932 for about €700 million in 2004 money, cut the Zuiderzee off from the North Sea and
turned it into the freshwater IJsselmeer. Four polders followed: Wieringermeer (200 km², 1930),
Noordoostpolder (480 km², 1942), Eastern Flevoland (540 km², 1957), Southern Flevoland (430 km²,
1968). A fifth, the Markerwaard, was shelved in 1986.

**Delta Works.** The [1953 North Sea flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_flood_of_1953)
killed 1,836 people in the Netherlands and flooded 9% of its farmland. The response was to dam most
of the southwestern estuaries. The
[1958 estimate was 3.3 billion guilders, about 20% of GDP at the time; the bill at completion in 1997 was 8.2 billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Works).
Design standard for Holland: a flood once in 10,000 years. The Eastern Scheldt was going to be a
solid dam until fishermen and environmentalists forced a gated barrier that stays open except in
storms.

**The ongoing cost.** Drained peat meets air and oxidizes, the ground drops, so the water table must
be lowered again, which exposes more peat. Rotterdam sinks
[1 to 1.5 cm a year](https://undark.org/2020/01/15/windmills-netherlands-sinking/). Old buildings
stand on wooden piles that rot once the water table drops below their tops. PBL (2016) put the
damage through 2050 at about
[€22 billion](https://klimaatadaptatienederland.nl/stresstest/bijsluiter/bepalen-dreigingen/droogte/bodemdaling),
of which
[at least €16 billion is foundation repair](https://www.pbl.nl/en/latest/news/subsidence-of-peatlands-leads-to-high-costs).
A 2008 state commission told the country to plan for 1.3 m of sea-level rise by 2100 and budget over
€100 billion.

## Mississippi and Atchafalaya

**Why the river wants to move.** A delta river builds its bed up with its own sediment until a
shorter, steeper path opens, then jumps. The Mississippi has done this every thousand years or so.
The Atchafalaya is the next path. Its share of the combined flow rose from
[under 10% in 1850 to about 30% by 1950](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_River_Control_Structure),
and in 1953 the Corps concluded the main river would switch by 1990 if left alone.

**The fix.** In the Flood Control Act of 1954
[Congress told the Corps to hold the Atchafalaya's share at 30%](https://www.atchafalaya.org/Rivers-Flood-Control),
freezing the split at roughly its 1950 value. The Low Sill and Overbank structures were finished
in 1963. Jeff Masters (2019) puts their cost at about
[$560 million in 2019 dollars](https://www.wunderground.com/cat6/Americas-Achilles-Heel-Mississippi-Rivers-Old-River-Control-Structure).

**1973.** With the Low Sill passing about 500,000 cubic feet per second, a 67-foot wing wall
collapsed on April 14. The scour hole behind it undermined 7 of 11 gates. The Corps opened the
Morganza Floodway two days later to take pressure off. The structure survived but was permanently
limited to 60% of its former flow, so the Corps built the Auxiliary Structure (1986, about $480
million in 2019 dollars).

**If it fails.** Masters (2019) collects the estimates:
[about $295 million a day in lost shipping; the last 200-plus miles of the old channel become a saltwater estuary, reaching Baton Rouge; about 1.5 million people lose their main freshwater supply; the ports that handle over 60% of US grain exports are cut off](https://wunderground.com/cat6/If-Old-River-Control-Structure-Fails-Catastrophe-Global-Impact).
Morgan City, on the Atchafalaya, would probably be abandoned. The first study of this was
[Kazmann and Johnson (1980)](https://biotech.law.lsu.edu/la/geology/LWRRI_B12_1980-ocr-small.pdf).

**Levees after 1927.** The 1927 flood covered
[27,000 square miles](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20161019092738/http://www.mvd.usace.army.mil/Portals/52/docs/04_MRT_WEB.pdf).
The federal Mississippi River and Tributaries project followed. By the Corps' own accounting it has
cost $14.8 billion since 1928 and prevented $666 billion in flood damage.

**The bill.** The levees that stop floods also stop mud. Sediment that used to spread over the delta
is now shot off the continental shelf, while the delta keeps compacting. Add
[oil and gas canals that let salt water into freshwater marsh](https://mississippiriverdelta.org/our-coastal-crisis/land-loss/)
and you get the 2,006 square miles lost. Louisiana's master plan is
[$50 billion over 50 years](https://wunderground.com/cat6/If-Old-River-Control-Structure-Fails-Catastrophe-Global-Impact).
Its centerpiece was killed in 2025 over cost, and because diverting fresh water and mud into
Barataria Bay would wreck the oyster and shrimp grounds that grew up in the salty, starved version
of the bay.

## Chicago

**Raising the city (1855-1860s).** Chicago sat a few feet above Lake Michigan with no slope to
drain. After the
[1854 cholera outbreak killed about 6% of the population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raising_of_Chicago),
engineer Ellis Chesbrough's 1856 plan laid sewers on top of the ground and raised the city over
them. Streets were filled and buildings jacked up. In 1860 a team including George Pullman lifted a
whole Lake Street block, about 35,000 tons, 4 feet 8 inches in five days with 600 men and 6,000
jackscrews.

**Reversing the river (1900).** The sewers emptied into the river, the river into the lake, and the
lake was the water supply. Late-19th-century typhoid deaths averaged about
[65 per 100,000 a year, peaking at 174 in 1891](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_public_health_in_Chicago).
The fix was a 28-mile canal cut through the low divide to the Des Plaines River, deep enough that
lake water runs downhill to the Mississippi.
[About 43 million cubic yards](https://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/300018.html) were
excavated, the largest earth-moving job in North America to that date, for
[$35.4 million through 1901](https://kwx.liehendr.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/Page%3AEB1911_-_Volume_06.djvu/132),
about $1.4 billion today.

**Consequences.** Chicago's sewage went to St. Louis. Missouri sued, and in
[Missouri v. Illinois (1906)](https://supreme.justia.com/us/200/496/) Holmes dismissed the case
because Missouri could not prove the typhoid came from Chicago. Then the Great Lakes states sued
over the water being taken:
[Wisconsin v. Illinois](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/388/388.US.426.1.2.3.11.html)
capped the diversion, set at 3,200 cubic feet per second in the 1967 decree. The third consequence
nobody planned: the canal joined two watersheds, so invasive carp from the Mississippi can swim to
the Great Lakes. Michigan asked the Supreme Court to close the canal in 2009 and lost.

## US Canals and Irrigation

**Erie Canal (1817-1825).** 363 miles, built by New York State alone after the federal government
declined. New York City's population
[quadrupled between 1820 and 1850](https://www.history.com/articles/erie-canal) and it passed
Philadelphia as the country's financial center.

**Idaho.** The Snake River Plain gets desert rainfall, but the river carries Rocky Mountain snowmelt
through it. The Carey Act (1894) gave arid states federal land if private companies would build
canals to it. It flopped almost everywhere except Idaho, which holds
[about 60% of all Carey Act land irrigated in the US](https://history.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/0260_Irrigation-in-Idaho-1.pdf).
The model project is Twin Falls: [Milner Dam, 1905](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milner_Dam), with
[270,000 acres contracted](https://americanheritage.com/node/54295). The federal Minidoka Project
(authorized 1904) now waters [over a million acres](https://www.usbr.gov/projects/index.php?id=361).
Statewide, Idaho irrigates about 3.4 million acres (unverified).

**California.** The Los Angeles Aqueduct (1913, 233 miles, gravity-fed) took the Owens River. The
federal Central Valley Project irrigates
[about 3 million acres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Valley_Project). The State Water
Project irrigates
[750,000 acres and lifts water 1,926 feet over the Tehachapis at one pumping plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_State_Water_Project),
the highest single lift in the world. Tulare Lake, once the largest freshwater lake west of the
Mississippi at
[up to 800 square miles](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/californias-long-dry-tulare-lake-has-returned-180982507/),
was
[farmland by 1920](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/return-of-tulare-lake-151174)
once its rivers were dammed and diverted. It came back in 1969, 1983, 1997 and 2023, when it covered
about 114,000 acres of cotton, tomatoes and pistachios.

**Aral.** The sea covered 68,000 km². Fishing employed about 40,000 people and supplied a sixth of
the Soviet catch, and it was over by 1987. The exposed bed is salt and pesticide residue, and the
dust affects at least 4.4 million people. Child mortality in the region was 75 per 1,000 in 2009.
The Kok-Aral dike (August 2005) simply stops the Syr Darya's water from spilling south to evaporate.
The North Aral refilled in months, salinity halved to under 10 g/L by 2007, and the catch reached
[5,595 tons in 2014](https://www.stephenmbland.com/kazakhstan-measuring-the-northern-aral). The
shore came back from 100 km to 25 km from the old port of Aralsk.

**Salton.** As of March 2026 the surface is
[243 feet below sea level with about 24,490 acres of exposed lakebed, growing by 2,400 acres a year](https://water.waterboards.ca.gov/board_info/agendas/2026/may/05202026-6staffrpt.pdf).
Salinity is about twice the ocean's. Over 20% of children in the area have asthma.

**Owens.** It was once the largest single source of particulate pollution in the US. LADWP now
manages 48.6 square miles of gravel, brine and shallow flooding and reports
[99.4% less dust](https://ladwpnews.com/ladwp-achieves-99-4-dust-control-reduction-at-owens-lake/).

**Dust Bowl.** By the 1940s many Plains counties had
[lost over 75% of their topsoil, and farmland values in high-erosion counties fell 30% relative to others and never recovered](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/11303325)
(Hornbeck, 2012). The "2.5 million fled" story is weaker than it sounds:
[out-migration rates matched the 1920s; the population fell because people stopped moving in](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22108)
(Long and Siu, 2016).

**Marshes.** After 2003 the dikes were breached and the marshes recovered to
[58% of original extent by 2006](https://e360.yale.edu/features/iraq-marshes-drought-climate-change).
They are drying again because Turkey and Iran dammed the headwaters.

## What Separates Successes from Failures

1. **Terminal lakes have no slack.** Aral, Owens, Salton, Tulare, Texcoco and Chad are all closed
   basins, where the lake is whatever inflow is left over after evaporation. Divert a cubic
   kilometer upstream and the lake loses exactly that, then the bed becomes airborne. Rivers that
   reach the ocean hide the same diversion in a much bigger sink. Every lakebed-dust disaster on
   this list is a closed basin.
2. **Engineers budgeted the water and forgot the mud.** Louisiana, the Nile delta, the Yangtze below
   Three Gorges and the Colorado delta are sediment failures, not water failures. A levee or dam
   that performs perfectly on flow can still starve a delta.
3. **Drainage is a ratchet.** Dry the ground and it sinks (Dutch peat, Mexico City clay, New
   Orleans). Then you need more pumping, which sinks it further. These projects cannot be finished,
   only subscribed to.
4. **The successes charge the beneficiaries.** Dutch water boards tax the people behind the dike.
   Erie charged tolls. Seattle assessed the landowners whose hills it removed. The failures sent the
   cost to someone with no vote: Karakalpaks, Owens Valley ranchers, Mexican delta fishermen, St.
   Louis, the Marsh Arabs.
5. **Most failures were predicted.** Soviet planners priced the Aral at zero on purpose. Owens was
   obvious. The surprises are biological (carp) or slow (medieval peat). The problem was whose loss
   counted, not forecasting.
6. **A success is a state that keeps paying.** The Netherlands pays 1% of GDP forever. Old River
   nearly failed in 1973 and needed a second structure. Libya's water system worked until the
   government fell, then
   [armed groups cut Tripoli's supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Man-Made_River).
7. **Reversal cost depends on whether the water still exists.** Kok-Aral and the Iraqi dikes were
   cheap because a river was still arriving and only needed to be let in. The Everglades and
   Louisiana cost tens of billions because cities and farms now sit where the water has to go.

## Best Anecdotes

- **Lifting a city block with people in it.** Chicago, 1860: 600 men turning 6,000 jackscrews in
  unison raised half a block of Lake Street 4 feet 8 inches in five days. Business reportedly
  carried on inside, and hotels were raised with guests in them (both unverified).
- **The preemptive canal opening.** Chicago's trustees knew Missouri was about to seek an
  injunction, so they opened the canal on January 2, 1900, before it could be filed. Full flow
  followed on January 17.
- **April 1973 at Old River.** A wing wall fell into the river and soundings found a 50-foot hole
  under the gates. The Corps opened Morganza with two days' warning instead of the promised five.
- **The Colorado changes its mind.** In 1905 engineer Charles Rockwood cut a temporary intake
  without headgates. Floods widened it and the entire river ran into the Salton Sink for two years.
  The Southern Pacific Railroad finally closed it in February 1907.
- **A gravel train every 45 minutes.** To fill Back Bay, 35-car trains ran from Needham day and
  night for years.
- **Hosing down a hill.** Seattle pumped
  [20 million gallons a day from Lake Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regrading_in_Seattle)
  through water cannons to wash Denny Hill into Elliott Bay.
- **The ghost lake.** Tulare Lake returned in 2023 on top of some of the most valuable farmland in
  the US, a century after it was "drained".
- **The sea that refilled early.** Planners expected the North Aral to take ten years to rise behind
  the Kok-Aral dike. It took months.
