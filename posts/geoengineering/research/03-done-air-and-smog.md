# Air and Smog: What We Did to the Air, and How Much We Undid

Research notes, compiled 2026-11-17. EPA figures are from the 2025 data release (published June
2026).

## Headline Findings

- **Q1, how much better than peak US badness:** it depends on the pollutant. Since 1980 the median
  US monitor is 1.4x cleaner for ozone, 2.8x for NO2, 8x for CO, 24x for SO2 and 38x for airborne
  lead ([EPA national trends, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/air-quality-national-summary)).
  Against the true coal-smoke peak (1910s-1940s), big-city particulate mass is down roughly 10x or
  more.
- **Q2, how much worse than 1500:** outdoor air away from fires is about 2-7x dirtier in fine
  particles (US median 7.2 µg/m³ against a natural background of 1-3). The air a typical person
  breathed is 10-50x cleaner, because in 1500 almost everyone lived with an open hearth.
- Los Angeles recorded 0.68 ppm ozone in 1955 and had 121 Stage 1 smog alerts in 1977. It has had
  zero Stage 1 days since 1999, but still exceeded the current federal 8-hour ozone standard on 157
  days in 2020
  ([South Coast AQMD](https://www.aqmd.gov/home/air-quality/historical-air-quality-data/historic-ozone-air-quality-trends)).
- Fourteen large US cities averaged 510 µg/m³ of total suspended particulates in the winters of
  1931-33, about the same as 58 Chinese cities in 1980-93 (538)
  ([Clay, Lewis and Severnini, 2016, Table A.1](https://docs.iza.org/dp9884.pdf)).
- EPA's own accounting: the 1970-1990 Clean Air Act cost $523 billion and returned about $22
  trillion; the 1990 amendments return about 30:1.
- Greenland ice shows Arctic lead pollution rose 250-300x from the early Middle Ages to the 1970s,
  has fallen more than 80% since, and is still about 60x the early-medieval level
  ([McConnell et al., 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v116y2019p14910-14915.html)).
- Beijing went from 89.5 to 27.0 µg/m³ PM2.5 between 2013 and 2025, a 70% cut in 12 years. The US
  took 25 years to cut 45%.
- The cleanup has a climate cost: aerosols mask roughly 0.4-0.5°C of warming, and removing them
  unmasks it.
- Wildfire smoke has erased about a quarter of US PM2.5 progress since 2000, and EPA's PM10 metric
  is up 23% since 2010.

## 1. Peak Badness in the US

There is no single peak. Coal smoke peaked between about 1910 and the late 1940s, Los Angeles ozone
in the mid-1950s to 1960s, and lead, CO and SO2 around 1970. National monitoring series start in
1980 (2000 for PM2.5), so "since 1980" understates the improvement.

### National numbers, 1980 to 2025

([CO](https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/carbon-monoxide-trends),

[SO2](https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/sulfur-dioxide-trends),
[NO2](https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/nitrogen-dioxide-trends),

[PM2.5](https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/particulate-matter-pm25-trends)).

| SO2 (1-hour, 99th pct)  | 142 ppb           | 5.8 ppb     | -96%   | 24x             |

From 1970 to 2025 combined emissions of the six criteria pollutants fell 78% while GDP rose 349%
([EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/air-quality-national-summary)).

For PM2.5 before 2000, a satellite-and-model reconstruction puts population-weighted North American
PM2.5 at 22 µg/m³ in 1981, 12 in 1998 and 7.9 in 2016
([Meng et al., 2019](https://sites.wustl.edu/acag/datasets/historical-pm2-5-across-north-america)),
so about 3x cleaner since 1981. The University of Chicago's index gives a 64.9% drop in particulate
exposure since 1970
([AQLI, 2023](https://aqli.epic.uchicago.edu/post/united-states-clean-air-act-1970)).

### The coal-smoke era

Before PM2.5 the metric was total suspended particulates (TSP), which counts coarse dust too, so it
is not directly comparable to PM2.5. The compiled record
([Clay, Lewis and Severnini, 2016, Table A.1](https://docs.iza.org/dp9884.pdf)):

| Where               | When            | TSP (µg/m³) |
| ------------------- | --------------- | ----------- |
| Chicago             | 1912-13         | 760         |
| 14 large US cities  | winters 1931-33 | 510         |
| 8 of those 14       | 1954            | 214         |
| US urban stations   | 1953-57         | 163         |
| US urban stations   | 1960            | 118         |
| US national average | 1990            | 60          |
| 58 Chinese cities   | 1980-93         | 538         |

The rows mix winter with annual and big cities with national averages, so read them as orders of
magnitude. Even so, most of the particulate cleanup happened before the 1970 Clean Air Act, driven
by local smoke ordinances and the switch from coal to gas and oil for heating. Chaining 1931-33 to
1990 (8.5x) and then EPA's PM10 trend gives roughly 10x cleaner than the 1930s; that is my
arithmetic, not a published figure.

### City by city

- **Los Angeles.** Peak 1-hour ozone was 0.68 ppm downtown in 1955
  ([South Coast AQMD, 1997](https://www.aqmd.gov/home/research/publications/50-years-of-progress)).
  Basin maxima were 0.49 ppm in 1980, 0.137 in 2019 and 0.185 in 2020, so 3.7-5x lower than the
  record. Stage 1 episodes (1-hour ozone at or above 0.20 ppm): 121 in 1977, 102 in 1980, 41 in
  1992, 11 in 1998, zero every year from 1999 through 2020
  ([South Coast AQMD](https://www.aqmd.gov/home/air-quality/historical-air-quality-data/historic-ozone-air-quality-trends)).
  The last Stage 3 episode (above 0.50 ppm) was in 1974. The basin still exceeded today's 0.070 ppm
  8-hour standard on 126 days in 2019 and 157 in 2020, against 211 in 1980. Peaks collapsed; the
  chronic problem did not.
- **Pittsburgh.** No reliable 1940s concentration exists. NASA says particulates in some eastern
  cities "likely exceeded 1,000 micrograms per cubic meter"
  ([NASA Earth Observatory, 2013](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hell-with-the-lid-taken-off-84271/)).
  The 1941 smoke ordinance was enforced from 1946, and hours of heavy smoke fell from 298 in 1946 to
  10 in 1955 ([Mackinac Center](https://www.mackinac.org/2821), secondary source).
- **St. Louis.** On "Black Tuesday", 28 November 1939, streetlights stayed on all day; 97% of homes
  burned coal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1939_St._Louis_smog)). A new ordinance
  forced cleaner fuel, and by mid-January 1941 the clearer sky was "the talk of the town"
  ([Wyss, 2025](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/coal-smoke-choked-st-louis-165408755.html)).
- **Donora, Pennsylvania, 26-31 October 1948.** An inversion trapped zinc-works and steel-mill
  emissions in a river valley: 20 dead and about 5,900 sick, 43% of the town
  ([Jacobs, Burgess and Abbott, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5922205)).
- **New York.** Estimated deaths: 220-240 in November 1953, 300-405 in 1963, 168 over
  Thanksgiving 1966. The city's average SO2 over 1957-1964 was 0.18 ppm (180 ppb)
  ([Wikipedia, citing McCarroll 1967 and Greenburg 1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_New_York_City_smog)).
  That long-run average is 30x today's national median for the 99th-percentile hour.

**Summary sentence:** US urban air is roughly 1.4x (ozone) to 40x (lead) cleaner than in 1980, and
about 10x cleaner in particulates than at the coal-smoke peak, with LA's worst ozone hours 4-5x
lower than in the 1950s.

## 2. What Did It

- **Local smoke control, 1940-1960.** St. Louis (1940), Pittsburgh (1946), then the market: gas
  pipelines and diesel locomotives replaced coal in homes and railways.
- **Clean Air Act of 1970.** Created national ambient standards and federal authority over
  tailpipes. The 1990 amendments added acid rain trading (section 5) and toxics rules.
- **Catalytic converters.** Required from the 1975 model year. A platinum-group catalyst oxidizes CO
  and unburnt hydrocarbons to CO2 and water; the later three-way version also reduces NOx to N2. New
  vehicles are about 99% cleaner per mile than 1970 models for hydrocarbons, CO, NOx and particles
  ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/transportation-air-pollution-and-climate-change/accomplishments-and-successes-reducing-air)).
- **Lead phase-out.** Lead poisons the catalyst, so converters forced unleaded fuel. In 1973
  gasoline averaged 2-3 g of lead per gallon, about 200,000 tons a year; the target was 0.1 g/gal by
  1986, with a full on-road ban on 1 January 1996
  ([EPA, 1996](https://www.epa.gov/archive/epa/aboutepa/epa-takes-final-step-phaseout-leaded-gasoline.html)).
  Median blood lead in children aged 1-5 fell from 15 µg/dL in 1976-80 to 0.6 in 2017-20
  ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/americaschildrenenvironment/biomonitoring-lead)).
- **The shift from coal.** Coal supplied 16% of US electricity in 2024
  ([EIA, 2025](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=65787)), down from about half in the
  mid-2000s (unverified).

The 2011 study's central benefit-cost ratio is over 30:1, with a low estimate of 3:1 and a high of

Independent life-expectancy estimates: across 51 metro areas, each 10 µg/m³ drop in PM2.5 between
about 1980 and 2000 went with 0.61 years of added life expectancy, up to 15% of the total gain in
that period
([Pope, Ezzati and Dockery, 2009](https://cleanairnortheast.epa.gov/pdf/res-links/Pope-PM-life-expectancy-NewEngJMed2009.pdf)).
A follow-up for 2000-2007 found 0.35 years per 10 µg/m³
([Correia et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3521092)). AQLI puts the national
gain since 1970 at 1.4 years
([2023](https://aqli.epic.uchicago.edu/post/united-states-clean-air-act-1970)).

## 3. The 1500 Baseline

**Natural background.** EPA's zero-out modelling (remove all US human emissions) leaves annual PM2.5
of 0.5-3 µg/m³, and remote monitors sit at 1-3 µg/m³
([EPA Policy Assessment, 2022, section 2.4](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2022-05/Final%20Policy%20Assessment%20for%20the%20Reconsideration%20of%20the%20PM%20NAAQS_May2022_0.pdf)).
What is left is sea salt, soil dust, organic aerosol from vegetation, and smoke. Today's US median
of 7.2 µg/m³ is therefore 2.4-14x background, 2-7x against the 1-3 range. Globally the natural floor
is higher: a model with all human emissions removed still leaves more than half the world's
population above the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³, because of dust and fire
([Pai et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9202349)).

**Fire.** Outdoor air in 1500 was not pristine. An estimated 4.4 million acres of California burned
every year before 1800, and "skies were likely smoky much of the summer and fall"
([Stephens, Martin and Clinton, 2007](https://www.frames.gov/catalog/46785)). Charcoal records show
the 20th-century West has a "fire deficit"
([Marlon et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295264)). One wrinkle: the same
record shows burning was at a low during the Little Ice Age, about 1400-1700, so 1500 itself was a
relatively smoke-poor year to pick.

**Ice cores.** Greenland black carbon rose sevenfold after 1850 and peaked in 1906-1910, from North
American coal
([McConnell et al., 2007](https://www.ess.uci.edu/~esaltzma/pub_pdfs/McConnelletalScience07.pdf)).
Lead is the oldest signal: Roman silver smelting is visible in Greenland ice, rising and falling
with the empire's economy
([McConnell et al., 2018](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/lead-pollution-recorded-in-greenland-ice-indicates-european-emiss/)).
Arctic lead then rose 250-300x from the early Middle Ages to the 1970s, fell more than 80%, and
remains about 60x the early-medieval level
([McConnell et al., 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v116y2019p14910-14915.html)). Lead is
the one pollutant for which 1500 was already measurably dirty and today is still far worse.

**Ozone.** Paris measurements from 1876-1910 imply rural surface ozone has more than doubled in a
century ([Volz and Kley, 1988](https://www.nature.com/articles/332240a0.pdf)).

**Cities.** London banned sea-coal burning by proclamation in 1306, and John Evelyn's _Fumifugium_
(1661) complained to Charles II of a "Hellish and dismall cloud of sea coal"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumifugium)). The reconstructed London series has
suspended particulates at 260 µg/m³ in 1700, a peak of 623 in 1891, and 16 in 2016
([Our World in Data, from Fouquet, 2011](https://ourworldindata.org/london-air-pollution)). In 1500
London had perhaps 50,000 people (unverified) burning mostly wood; no estimate exists for that date.

**Indoors.** This dominates. Chimneys were still a novelty in ordinary English houses in the 1570s:
old men remarked on "the multitude of chimneys lately erected", where before "each one made his fire
against a reredos in the hall"
([Harrison, 1577](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1577harrison-england.asp)). Measured
proxies:

| Setting                                                       | PM2.5 (µg/m³)          | Source                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Reconstructed Viking-age house, near the fire                 | 3,400                  | [Christensen and Ryhl-Svendsen, 2015](https://read.qxmd.com/read/25065944/household-air-pollution-from-wood-burning-in-two-reconstructed-houses-from-the-danish-viking-age) |
| Same, estimated personal exposure                             | 410                    | same                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Reconstructed 17th-19th century Danish farmhouse, living room | 21-160                 | [Ryhl-Svendsen et al., 2010](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/fine-particles-and-carbon-monoxide-from-wood-burning-in-17th-19th/)                                       |

| Kitchens cooking on dung today                                | 224                    | same                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Kitchens cooking on gas today                                 | 45                     | same                                                                                                                                                                        |
| US outdoor median, 2025                                       | 7.2                    | EPA                                                                                                                                                                         |

## 5. Two Atmospheric Repairs

**Ozone layer.** Chlorine and bromine from CFCs and halons destroy stratospheric ozone
catalytically, so each atom removes many ozone molecules, and polar stratospheric clouds concentrate
the reaction over Antarctica each spring. The 1987 Montreal Protocol has phased out about 99% of
controlled ozone-depleting substances. Total column ozone is projected to return to 1980 values
around 2040 for 60°N-60°S, 2045 for the Arctic and 2066 for the Antarctic
([WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/executivesummary/)).
The 2025 hole peaked at 8.83 million square miles, the fifth smallest since 1992, and
ozone-depleting substances in the Antarctic stratosphere are down about a third from their 2000 peak
([NASA, 2025](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/nasa-noaa-rank-2025-ozone-hole-as-5th-smallest-since-1992)).
The protocol's 2016 Kigali Amendment on HFCs should avoid 0.3-0.5°C of warming by 2100
([WMO, 2023](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/ozone-layer-recovery-track-helping-avoid-global-warming-05degc)).

**Acid rain.** SO2 and NOx oxidize to sulfuric and nitric acid and rain out hundreds of kilometres
downwind. The 1990 amendments capped power-plant SO2 and let plants trade allowances; they met the
cap with flue-gas scrubbers (a limestone slurry turns SO2 into gypsum) and low-sulfur Wyoming coal.
Power-plant SO2 fell 95% from 1995 to 2023
([EPA, 2025 snapshot](https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/power-sector/progress-report-emissions-reductions/index.html)),
and wet sulfate deposition fell more than 70% between 1989-91 and 2020-22
([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/acidrain/acid-rain-program-results)). Costs ran about $3 billion a year
against benefits estimated at $122 billion a year in 2010, almost all from avoided PM2.5 deaths, not
from lakes ([Chestnut and Mills, 2005](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/89305)).

## 6. The Twist: Cleaner Air Is Warmer Air

Sulfate particles reflect sunlight and seed brighter clouds. IPCC AR6 says well-mixed greenhouse
gases caused 1.0-2.0°C of warming by 2010-2019 while "other human drivers (principally aerosols)
contributed a cooling of 0.0°C to 0.8°C"
([IPCC, 2021, SPM A.1.3](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)).
The usual central estimate is about 0.4-0.5°C of masked warming, with sulfate forcing of -0.9 W/m²
(range -1.6 to -0.3). Global SO2 emissions are about 40% below their mid-2000s level, and China's
fell more than 70% from 38 Mt in 2006 to about 10 Mt. The 2020 shipping rule (fuel sulfur cap cut
from 3.5% to 0.5%) cut shipping SO2 about 65%; most studies put the forcing at 0.11-0.14 W/m² and
the warming at about 0.04-0.05°C, while Hansen et al. (2025) argue for 0.5 W/m². All SO2 declines
together may explain about 0.14°C, a quarter of the warming since 2007
([Carbon Brief, 2025](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-how-human-caused-aerosols-are-masking-global-warming/)).
Deliberate stratospheric aerosol injection is the same mechanism run on purpose (another agent's
lane).

## 7. What Is Left on the Table

- **Remaining deaths.** Estimates of US deaths attributable to PM2.5 range from 58,600 a year for
  2010 to 88,400 for 2015
  ([Zhang et al., 2018](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/18/15003/2018/)), down from 123,700
  in 1990. The spread comes from the assumed dose-response curve at low concentrations.
- **Cost of the next cut.** EPA's 2024 rule lowering the annual PM2.5 standard from 12 to 9 µg/m³
  was costed at $590 million a year in 2032 against $22-46 billion in benefits and up to 4,500
  deaths avoided
  ([EPA, 2024](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-02/pm-naaqs-overview.pdf)). EPA asked
  the D.C. Circuit to vacate the rule in November 2025; the court upheld it on 26 June 2026
  ([Harvard EELP tracker](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/epa-finalized-stricter-national-ambient-air-quality-standards-naaqs-for-particulate-matter-pm/)).
- **Wildfire smoke.** Since 2016 smoke has slowed or reversed PM2.5 progress in nearly
  three-quarters of contiguous states, erasing about 25% of the gains since 2000
  ([Burke et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06522-6)). Smoke PM2.5 caused an
  estimated 41,380 excess US deaths a year in 2011-2020, projected to reach about 71,000 by 2050
  ([Qiu et al., 2025](https://climatelab.stanford.edu/publications/peer-reviewed-papers/wildfire-smoke-exposure-and-mortality-burden-us-under-climate)).
  EPA's own data shows it: the PM10 24-hour metric is up 23% since 2010. Section 3 implies part of
  this is the West returning toward its pre-suppression fire regime.
- **Gas stoves and indoor air.** A study of more than 100 homes found gas and propane stoves add
  about 4 ppb to annual NO2 exposure, with up to 19,000 deaths a year (95% CI 8,500-34,000) and
  about 50,000 current pediatric asthma cases attributed to it
  ([Kashtan et al., 2024, via Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/allergy-asthma/20240503/nitrogen-dioxide-from-gas-stoves-associated-with-thousands-of-asthma-cases-in-the-us)).
  Outdoor standards do not cover the air where people spend about 90% of their time (unverified).

## Best Anecdotes

- St. Louis, 28 November 1939: streetlights on all day, headlights at noon. The _Post-Dispatch_ won
  the 1941 Pulitzer for its anti-smoke campaign.
- Experts estimated in 1966 that a Donora-scale smog in New York could kill 11,000 people and sicken
  four million.
- William Harrison's old men in the 1570s listed the three great changes of their lifetimes:
  chimneys, better bedding, and pewter replacing wooden plates. Chimneys came first.
- Late-1940s Los Angeles had more than 300,000 backyard incinerators and over a million citrus
  smudge pots
  ([South Coast AQMD, 1997](https://www.aqmd.gov/home/research/publications/50-years-of-progress)).
- The Roman economy is readable in Greenland ice: lead fallout drops after the Antonine plague.
- An 1866 _Atlantic_ writer on Pittsburgh: "like looking over into hell with the lid taken off."
- In March 2025 the US State Department ended its embassy air-monitoring programme
  ([IQAir, 2026](https://www.iqair.com/us/newsroom/waqr-2025-pr)).
