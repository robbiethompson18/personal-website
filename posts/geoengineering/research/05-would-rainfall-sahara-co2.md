# 05: Greening the Sahara, More Global Rain, and High CO2 With Low Warming

Research notes, compiled 2026-11-17. Estimates marked "my arithmetic" are built here from sourced
inputs so they can be checked.

## Headline Findings

- **Q1: yes, the Sahara can be greened, but only by paying for the water forever or by changing the
  energy balance over North Africa at continental scale.** There is no evidence of a tipping point
  that a planted patch could trigger. The cheap, working version is the Sahara's southern margin
  (the Sahel), where farmer-managed regeneration costs on the order of $10 per hectare.
- **Q2: you cannot meaningfully raise global rainfall except by warming the planet, and more total
  rain would not be good on net.** Global rain is capped by how fast the atmosphere can radiate away
  latent heat, not by how much water is around. It rises
  [1-3% per °C](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-8/) while water vapor rises about
  7% per °C, so the extra arrives as heavier downpours. The useful goal is moving rain, and the
  tools for that shift a few percent regionally.
- **Q3: for crop calories, "high CO2, low warming" does look good in models, better than unabated
  warming or emissions cuts. For the world it is a bad steady state**: cooling does nothing about
  ocean acidification or nutrient dilution in food, the sunshade cuts rainfall slightly, and the
  commitment grows every year CO2 keeps rising.
- The Sahara was green until about 5,000 years ago, with a
  [330,000 km²](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_humid_period) Lake Chad. Orbital forcing will
  not bring it back for roughly 10,000 years, and possibly not on the next cycle either.
- Irrigating 10% of the Sahara with desalinated seawater costs roughly **$0.5-0.9 trillion per year
  (0.4-0.8% of world GDP), indefinitely** (my arithmetic). It needs 12-25 times the world's
  desalination capacity. In the one study that modeled an irrigated Sahara,
  [only 26%](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00382-017-3890-8) of the evaporated water fell back on it
  (Kemena et al. 2018), so the loop never closes.
- Covering the whole Sahara in wind and solar farms adds
  [+0.35 mm/day of rain in the Sahara and +1.34 mm/day in the Sahel](https://sciencythoughts.blogspot.com/2018/09/assessing-potential-impact-of-large.html)
  in Li et al. (2018, Science). The follow-up (Lu et al. 2021) finds the darker surface
  [warms the globe 0.16-0.39°C](https://thenextweb.com/news/solar-panels-in-the-sahara-could-boost-renewable-energy-but-raise-temperatures-worldwide-syndication)
  and dries the Amazon.
- CO2 fertilization is real: about
  [+18% yield for C3 crops at +200 ppm](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.15375), near zero for maize and
  sorghum except in drought. The same CO2 cuts wheat zinc by
  [9.3%, iron by 5.1% and protein by 6.3%](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13179) (Myers et al. 2014).

## Part 1: Greening the Sahara

### It Was Green 6,000 Years Ago

The African Humid Period ran from roughly
[14,500 to 5,000 years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_humid_period). The cause was
orbital precession: Northern Hemisphere summer sunlight was
[at least 4% stronger than today, about 8% at the peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_humid_period).
A hotter summer continent pulls the West African monsoon north. Leaf-wax isotopes give a median
rainfall of
[640 mm/year, against 35-100 mm today](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5242556), with the
green zone reaching 31°N (Tierney, Pausata and deMenocal 2017). Lake Chad grew to
[about 330,000 km²](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_humid_period), near the size of the
Caspian Sea. Fossils and rock art record hippos, crocodiles and giraffes.

Orbit alone is not enough. Models forced only by the orbital change produce too little rain, which
is the main evidence that feedbacks did much of the work. Vegetation darkens the surface, and a
wetter Sahara emits less dust: Atlantic sediment cores show dust flux
[about 5 times lower than in the last 2,000 years](https://www.ldeo.columbia.edu/~winckler/Publications_files/Mcgee_AfricanDust_EPSL2013.pdf)
(McGee et al. 2013). In one model, removing the dust pushes the monsoon
[about 500 km](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016E&PSL.434..298P) further north than vegetation
alone (Pausata et al. 2016).

**How abrupt was the end?** Contested, and it matters, because abruptness is the evidence for a
tipping point. deMenocal et al. (2000) found in a dust record off Mauritania that the start and end
each took
[decades to centuries, both when summer insolation crossed the same threshold, 4.2% above present](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0277-3791%2899%2900081-5).
Kröpelin et al. (2008), reading pollen in Lake Yoa in Chad, found
[a gradual drying between 5,600 and 2,700 years ago](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1154913) and
concluded the vegetation feedback is weak. Shanahan et al. (2015) reconciled them: the end was
[locally abrupt but swept southward over millennia](https://www.doi.org/10.1038/NGEO2329). Each
place fell off a cliff, but the continent did not flip at once.

**When does it come back?** Precession cycles every 21,000-23,000 years, so the next
summer-insolation peak is roughly 10,000 years out. Earth's orbit is now near-circular, which
shrinks the effect: orbital swings over the next 100,000 years
[will be smaller than in past humid periods](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/18/1897/2022/cp-18-1897-2022.pdf).
A [2026 EGU abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-11631.html) argues the
Sahara will probably not regreen on the next cycle at all. Greenhouse warming pushes the other way.

### The Charney Mechanism, and Why There Is No Easy Tipping Point

Charney's (1975) argument: sand reflects 35-45% of sunlight, vegetation 15-20%. A bright surface
absorbs less, so the air column above it loses more heat to space than it gains. To balance, the air
must sink and warm by compression, and sinking air cannot rain.
[Remove vegetation, albedo rises, air sinks more, rain falls less](https://doi.org/10.1002/qj.49710142802).
Run it backward and darkening the surface makes rain.

The sign has held up. The strength has not. Charney proposed it to explain the 1970s-80s Sahel
drought as overgrazing feeding on itself. Later models reproduced that drought from
[observed sea-surface temperatures alone](https://www.ldeo.columbia.edu/~biasutti/papers/Giannini+al_GPC08.pdf)
(Giannini et al. 2008), and the rains returned when the oceans changed. A system locked in by its
own albedo would not have recovered. The case for two stable states rests on simplified models such
as Claussen et al. (1999), where vegetation feedback
[strongly amplifies the orbital change](https://epic.awi.de/id/eprint/13567/) into an abrupt
collapse. More detailed models mostly give a smoother response.

On how big a dark patch would tip it: I found no study showing a partial patch triggering runaway
greening. Li et al. call their effect [scale dependent](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar5629).
The best reading is an amplifier with gain below one. It multiplies whatever forcing you supply and
stops when you stop.

### Wind and Solar Farms as Rainmakers

Li et al. (2018, Science) covered the Sahara (about 9 million km²) with turbines and panels in a
climate model with dynamic vegetation. Turbines add surface roughness, which slows wind and forces
air to converge and rise. Panels are darker than sand.

| Scenario | Electricity                                                                                        | Sahara rain                                                                                               | Sahel rain                                                                            | Local warming                                                                                    |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Wind     | [3 TW](https://www.sci.news/othersciences/climatology/solar-wind-farms-sahara-climate-06392.html)  | [+0.25 mm/day](https://www.sci.news/othersciences/climatology/solar-wind-farms-sahara-climate-06392.html) | [+1.12 mm/day](https://phys.org/news/2018-09-large-solar-farms-sahara-vegetation.pdf) | [+2.16 K](https://sciencythoughts.blogspot.com/2018/09/assessing-potential-impact-of-large.html) |
| Solar    | [79 TW](https://www.sci.news/othersciences/climatology/solar-wind-farms-sahara-climate-06392.html) | [+0.13 mm/day](https://sciencythoughts.blogspot.com/2018/09/assessing-potential-impact-of-large.html)     | +0.57 mm/day                                                                          | about +1 K                                                                                       |
| Both     | 82 TW                                                                                              | [+0.35 mm/day](https://sciencythoughts.blogspot.com/2018/09/assessing-potential-impact-of-large.html)     | +1.34 mm/day                                                                          | +2.65 K                                                                                          |

In annual terms, +0.35 mm/day is +128 mm/year on a baseline under 100 mm; the abstract calls it
["more than twofold"](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar5629). That turns hyper-arid desert into
sparse steppe, well short of the Humid Period's 640 mm. The Sahel gets +490 mm/year, which is
transformative. Vegetation feedback supplies [about 80%](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar5629) of
the wind-farm effect. World energy demand was
[about 18 TW](https://www.sci.news/othersciences/climatology/solar-wind-farms-sahara-climate-06392.html),
so this generates over four times what civilization uses.

Lu et al. (2021, Geophysical Research Letters) reran it in a full Earth-system model at 20% and 50%
solar coverage. Panels turn about 15% of sunlight into electricity and the rest into heat, so the
desert warms
[1.5°C at 20% and 2.5°C at 50%](https://thenextweb.com/news/solar-panels-in-the-sahara-could-boost-renewable-energy-but-raise-temperatures-worldwide-syndication)
and the heat spreads:
[+0.16°C and +0.39°C global mean](https://thenextweb.com/news/solar-panels-in-the-sahara-could-boost-renewable-energy-but-raise-temperatures-worldwide-syndication).
The tropical rain belt shifts north, so the Sahara's gain is the Amazon's loss, with
[drought and forest degradation there and added Arctic sea-ice loss](https://doi.org/10.1029/2020GL090789).

### Irrigating It: The Arithmetic

Ornstein, Aleinov and Rind (2009, Climatic Change) proposed drip-irrigated eucalyptus across the
Sahara and the Australian Outback, fed by coastal desalination:
[about 4,900 km³ of water a year for the Sahara at 500 mm/year](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-009-9626-y),
[about $1.9 trillion a year](https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/3374-sowing-seeds-in-the-sand/),
[around 8 billion tons of carbon captured a year](https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/3374-sowing-seeds-in-the-sand/),
and
[up to 8°C of local cooling](https://e360.yale.edu/digest/heat-resistant-forests-could-reverse-warming-in-the-sahara-study-says)
from evaporation. That is about $65 per ton of CO2, but it implies roughly $0.39 per m³ all-in,
which is the price of desalinated water at the plant gate with nothing for moving it.

My version, for 10% of the Sahara (920,000 km², 92 million hectares, about the area of Nigeria):

| Input               | Value                                                      | Source                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Water demand        | 500-1,000 mm/year, so 5,000-10,000 m³ per hectare per year | Ornstein's 500 mm; FAO crop needs of [450-650 mm for wheat, 800-1,600 mm for alfalfa](https://www.fao.org/4/s2022e/s2022e02.htm)                                                                                                                                                |
| Desalination        | $0.40 per m³                                               | [$0.365 at Hassyan, Dubai](https://dewa.gov.ae/en/about-us/strategic-initiatives/hassyan); [$0.58 at Sorek, Israel](https://www.technologyreview.com/2015/11/23/247438/bankrolling-the-10-breakthrough-technologies-megascale-desalination/amp/)                                |
| Desalination energy | about 3.5 kWh per m³                                       | my assumption for a whole plant; the membrane stage runs [about 2.5 kWh/m³ against a thermodynamic floor near 1.05](https://core.ac.uk/download/78053489.pdf)                                                                                                                   |
| Pumping energy      | 2.0 kWh per m³                                             | physics: 400 m of lift plus 200 m of friction head at 80% efficiency. 1,000 kg × 9.81 × 600 m / 0.8 = 7.4 MJ                                                                                                                                                                    |
| Solar electricity   | $37 per MWh                                                | [Wood Mackenzie's 2025 MENA average](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2025/10/24/wood-mackenzie-records-solar-lcoe-of-37-mwh-in-mena-region/); record bids are near [$10](https://reneweconomy.com.au/saudi-solar-plant-locks-in-new-record-low-price-for-power-1-04c-kwh/amp/?nb=1) |
| Pipeline capital    | $0.30-0.60 per m³                                          | Libya's Great Man-Made River: [$25 billion for 6.5 million m³/day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Man-Made_River), or $10.50 per m³/year of capacity; at 5% over 50 years, $0.58 per m³                                                                                    |

Delivered water: $0.40 + (2.0 kWh × $0.037 = $0.07) + $0.30-0.60 = **about $0.80-1.10 per m³**. Call
it $1. Moving the water costs more than making it.

- **Per hectare:** $5,000-10,000 per year ($2,000-4,000 for desalination alone). A hectare of
  irrigated wheat grosses perhaps $1,500, so farming never pays for it.
- **Total:** 460-920 km³ of water and **$0.46-0.92 trillion per year**, 0.4-0.8% of
  [$117 trillion world GDP](https://www.statisticstimes.com/m/economy/world-gdp.php).
- **Scale:** 920 km³/year is 2.5 billion m³/day, about 25 times world desalination capacity
  ([roughly 90-110 million m³/day](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2025/dec/mena-global-water-sector-8-transformations.html)).
  Energy is 5.5 kWh × 920 km³ = about 5,000 TWh/year, a sixth of
  [world electricity (about 30,900 TWh)](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/global-electricity-review-2025/),
  needing about 2.5 TW of solar. The world has
  [2.2 TW installed](https://www.pv-tech.org/iea-world-reaches-2-2tw-cumulative-installed-solar-capacity).
- **As carbon removal:** at Ornstein's implied uptake of about 17 tons of CO2 per hectare per year,
  $5,000 per hectare is about $290 per ton.

Does the irrigation bootstrap its own rain? Kemena et al. (2018, Climate Dynamics) modeled a fully
irrigated, forested Sahara:
[6 K cooler, +267 mm/year of rain, but only 26% of the evaporated water re-precipitates over the Sahara](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00382-017-3890-8).
Most blows south to the Sahel. The forest needs more than it gets back, and the rain exists only
while the pumps run. The same work found the afforestation
[intensified North Atlantic tropical cyclones more than a worst-case warming scenario](https://oceanrep.geomar.de/id/eprint/41494/1/Kemena_PhDThesis_2018.pdf).

### Fossil Water

The Nubian Sandstone Aquifer under Egypt, Libya, Sudan and Chad is the largest fossil aquifer known:
over 2 million km², holding
[about 150,000 km³](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubian_Sandstone_Aquifer_System) by the commonly
quoted figure, of which about
[14,500 km³ is counted as recoverable](https://books.gw-project.org/large-aquifer-systems-around-the-world/?p=53).
The water fell as rain in earlier humid periods,
[38,000 or more years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubian_Sandstone_Aquifer_System). It is a
mine, not a river.

Libya's Great Man-Made River taps it:
[more than 1,300 wells, most over 500 m deep, 2,820 km of pipe, 6.5 million m³/day, over $25 billion, built from 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Man-Made_River).
At 2.4 km³ a year the bulk arithmetic says thousands of years, but published lifetimes run from
[60-100 years to about 1,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Man-Made_River), because what
binds is local drawdown: the Tazerbo wellfield had dropped
[up to 28 m by 2019](https://doaj.org/article/94339e18fb004ce09128ab331af4fe6a). Scale is the real
limit. My 10% scenario would drain the recoverable volume in about 16 years.

Egypt's Toshka project (1997) aimed at about 540,000 feddans (a feddan is 0.42 hectares) and
[had irrigated perhaps 16,500 by 2010 (unverified)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Valley_Project).
Its New Delta,
[inaugurated in May 2026](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/mena/2026/05/17/egypts-president-opens-new-delta-project-to-grow-wheat-in-desert-with-artificial-river/),
targets
[2.2 million feddans for about $15 billion](https://millingmea.com/egypt-inaugurates-us15b-new-delta-project-to-turn-desert-into-farmland/),
watered by recycled farm drainage from a
[7.5 million m³/day treatment plant](https://smartwatermagazine.com/news/metito/metito-construct-worlds-largest-wastewater-treatment-plant-egypt).
That is 9,200 km², 0.1% of the Sahara, on reallocated Nile water.

### Flooding Schemes

**Roudaire's Sahara Sea (1874-1882).** French officer François Roudaire, backed by Suez builder
Ferdinand de Lesseps, proposed a
[120-mile canal from the Gulf of Gabès to flood the salt basins of Tunisia and Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_%C3%89lie_Roudaire),
expecting a moister climate. His own surveys found
[key basins were above sea level](https://bigthink.com/strange-maps/617-the-sahara-sea-a-french-mirage-in-north-africa/).

**Qattara Depression (Egypt).** A basin of nearly 20,000 km² reaching
[133 m below sea level](https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/567838.aspx), 55-100 km from the
Mediterranean. Let the sea in through turbines and let evaporation hold the lake near -60 m, so the
flow never stops. Designs ran to
[670 MW at first and about 5,800 MW with pumped storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project).
Friedrich Bassler's 1970s study proposed digging the canal with
[213 nuclear devices of 1.5 megatons each](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project).
In May 2026 the Egyptian cabinet
[formally scrapped the project](https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/567838.aspx), citing cost and
groundwater salinization.

**Would an inland sea make rain?** I found no climate-model study of Qattara or the Tunisian basins.
Two things say not much. The paleo analog: in a model of Lake Mega-Chad, 17 times larger than a
flooded Qattara,
[rain is drastically reduced over the lake because cool water suppresses convection, with some enhancement at the edges and too little net effect to change vegetation](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/9/1417/2013/)
(Contoux et al. 2013). Some later models find
[modest local increases](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/18/1035/2022/cp-18-1035-2022.pdf). And
my arithmetic: 20,000 km² evaporating 2 m/year (assumed) is 40 km³ of vapor, which over the Sahara's
9.2 million km² is 4 mm/year even if every drop fell back. The Sahara does not lack vapor passing
overhead. It lacks rising air.

### What Has Been Tried at Scale

**Great Green Wall (African Union, 2007).** Originally a tree belt across the continent, later
redefined as restoring 100 million hectares by 2030. By 2020,
[about 4 million hectares had been restored inside the official zones, or 17.8 million counting the wider region](https://www.unccd.int/resources/unccd-library/africas-great-green-wall-officially-4-and-unofficially-18-complete).
Hitting the target needs
[8.2 million hectares and $4.3 billion a year](https://earth.org/great-green-wall-in-africa-4-complete/).
Donors pledged $19 billion in 2021;
[about $2.5 billion had been disbursed by 2023](https://www.unccd.int/news-stories/stories/progress-accelerated-targeted-action-needed-realize-africas-great-green-wall).
Planted trees die: in Niger before 1980,
[60 million were planted and officially 20% survived](https://www.unccd.int/sites/default/files/library/489_Article.pdf).

**What worked** was not planting. In farmer-managed natural regeneration, farmers protect and prune
shoots from living root systems already in their fields. In southern Niger this added
[at least 200 million trees across 5-7 million hectares](https://www.unccd.int/sites/default/files/library/489_Article.pdf)
at
[roughly $10-15 per hectare](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08ac440f0b652dd0008c4/Niger-InsideStory_cbc2_web.pdf)
(proponents' figures). Zai pits (small manured planting holes) and half-moon bunds catch runoff on
crusted soil; half-moon plots in Niger yielded
[about 535 kg/ha of cereal on abandoned land](https://www.unccd.int/sites/default/files/library/103_looseleaf_1-2-Hassane.pdf).
These work because the Sahel has 200-600 mm of rain to harvest. They do not transfer to the Sahara
proper. Modeling of a completed Wall finds
[more rain and shorter droughts but more extreme-heat days](https://www.openforest.org.ua/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/contrasting-consequences-of-the-great-green-wall-.pdf)
(Ingrosso and Pausata 2024).

**China.** The Three-North Shelterbelt (1978-2050) is the largest precedent. Officially, forest
cover in the program area rose
[from 5.05% to 13.84%](https://english.www.gov.cn/news/202411/28/content_WS6748240fc6d0868f4e8ed7ed.html).
Critics, chiefly Cao Shixiong, report that
[only about 15% of trees planted in China's arid north since 1949 survived](https://e360.yale.edu/features/chinas_reforestation_programs_big_success_or_just_an_illusion)
and that thirsty poplars drew down groundwater that native shrubs needed. In the Kubuqi Desert
[about a third has been greened](https://pulitzercenter.org/reporting/chinas-crazy-plan-keep-sand-swallowing-world).
On the Loess Plateau, revegetation is
[approaching the limit the water supply can sustain](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate3092)
(Feng et al. 2016). The lesson: greening works with 200-450 mm of rain and shrubs or grass, and
fails where trees are planted past the water budget.

### The Sahel Is Already Greening

Satellites show the Sahel greening since the early 1980s. First, the rains recovered from a drought
that was partly man-made: sulfate pollution from Europe and North America
[cooled the North Atlantic and pulled the rain belt south](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6445402),
and clean-air laws reversed it (Giannini and Kaplan 2018). Dong and Sutton (2015) credit
[mainly greenhouse gases](https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate2664) instead. Second, woody cover rose:
in Senegal, [woody leaf biomass doubled in 27 years](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.12807) (Brandt et
al. 2015). Third, CO2 fertilization and farmer regeneration. A deep-learning count found
[1.84 billion individual trees across 1.3 million km² of the western Sahara and Sahel](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2824-5)
(Brandt et al. 2020), where maps had shown almost none.

### What a Green Sahara Costs Everyone Else

- **Amazon phosphorus.** Of
  [182 million tons of dust leaving Africa each year, 27.7 million land on the Amazon carrying about 22,000 tons of phosphorus, roughly what the basin loses to rain and flooding](https://phys.org/news/2015-02-massive-amounts-saharan-fertilize-amazon.pdf)
  (Yu et al. 2015). The figure checks out, with two caveats. African biomass-burning smoke supplies
  [up to half](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1906091116) of the phosphorus arriving from Africa
  (Barkley et al. 2019). And 22,000 tons is small: by my arithmetic about 110,000 tons of ordinary
  phosphate fertilizer, tens of millions of dollars a year.
- **Hurricanes.** Dusty years are quiet hurricane years in the satellite record
  ([Evan et al. 2006](https://phys.org/news/2006-10-dampen-hurricane-fury.pdf)), though
  [both may respond to the same winds](https://www.aoml.noaa.gov/hurricane_blog/paper-on-the-relationship-between-african-dust-and-hurricane-activity-published-in-atmospheric-chemistry-and-physics).
  Pausata et al. (2017, PNAS) simulated a green Sahara with up to 80% less dust and got
  [more tropical cyclones in both hemispheres, especially the Caribbean and US East Coast](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5474772).
  Sediment records [do not clearly confirm it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_humid_period).

### Verdict on Question 1

| Path                                                      | What you get                                                             | Cost                                                                               | Catch                                          |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Restore the Sahel margin (regeneration, water harvesting) | Savanna and farmland on up to 100 million ha that already get 200-600 mm | $10-500 per hectare once, so $1-50 billion                                         | Not the Sahara. Depends on the rains holding   |
| Irrigate with desalinated seawater                        | Any vegetation, anywhere                                                 | $5,000-10,000 per hectare per year; $0.5-0.9 trillion a year per 10% of the Sahara | Forever. No tipping point. Stronger hurricanes |
| Darken and roughen the surface at continental scale       | Steppe in the Sahara, a wet Sahel                                        | Over $100 trillion (about 300 TW of panels at $0.50 per watt, my rough figure)     | Global warming of 0.2-0.4°C, Amazon drying     |

The cheapest path to a green Sahara proper is to wait 10,000 years. The cheapest path to a lot more
green in North Africa is the Sahel, where the cost per hectare is two to three orders of magnitude
lower because the rain is free.

## Part 2: Increasing Global Rainfall

### The Energy-Budget Constraint

Rain is not limited by water supply. The ocean is an unlimited source and the air holds only about
nine days of rain at a time. Global rain is limited by energy.

Evaporating a kilogram of water takes about 2.5 MJ. That heat is released when the vapor condenses,
kilometers up. The troposphere has to dispose of it, and its only exit is radiation. So over the
globe and over a year, **rain rate equals the atmosphere's net radiative cooling rate (less a
smaller sensible-heat term) divided by the latent heat of water.** Check: global precipitation is
about 1 meter a year. 1,000 kg/m² × 2.5 MJ/kg ÷ 31.6 million seconds = 79 W/m², which matches the
latent-heat term in the standard global energy budget.

Raising global rain 10% therefore requires the atmosphere to shed another 8 W/m², twice the entire
forcing from doubling CO2. Extra vapor does nothing by itself. This is why warming adds vapor at
about 7% per °C but rain at only
[1-3% per °C](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-8/), a result from
[Allen and Ingram (2002)](https://iri.columbia.edu/~alesall/ouagaCILSS/articles/allen+ingram_nature2002.pdf)
and [Held and Soden (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI3990.1). A warmer atmosphere radiates more,
permitting [2-3% more rain per °C](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-8/). CO2
partly cancels that by absorbing infrared within the atmosphere, reducing the cooling the rain has
to balance.

Three consequences:

1. **The only levers on global rain are temperature and the radiative budget.** Seeding, pumping and
   planting do not change the total.
2. **Vapor outruns rain, so rain gets lumpier.** With 7% more water in the air and 2% more rain, the
   atmosphere overturns more slowly. Heavy events scale near
   [7% per °C](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-8/) and dry spells lengthen.
   Warming buys rain in its least useful form.
3. **Solar geoengineering reduces global rain.** Sunlight heats the surface, where it drives
   evaporation, so rain is more sensitive to sunlight than to CO2:
   [2.4% per K against 1.5% per K](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2409412) (Bala, Duffy
   and Taylor 2008). Dim the sun enough to cancel CO2 warming and CO2's direct suppression remains.
   Across 12 models, cancelling quadrupled CO2 cut global precipitation
   [about 4.5%, most over monsoon land](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/the-hydrological-impact-of-geoengineering-in-the-geoengineering-m/)
   (Tilmes et al. 2013). Realistic deployments are a fraction of that, but the sign is robust. The
   year after Pinatubo saw a [record drop in river discharge](https://doi.org/10.1029/2007GL030524)
   (Trenberth and Dai 2007).

### Where It Falls, and Whether More Is Good

"Wet gets wetter, dry gets drier" holds over oceans. Over land, Greve et al. (2014, Nature
Geoscience) found it on only
[10.8% of land area, the opposite on 9.5%, and no detectable trend on about three-quarters](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/09/140914150803.htm).
A higher global total says almost nothing about any particular farm.

More rain helps only where water is the binding constraint. Nemani et al. (2003, Science) mapped
what limits plant growth:
[water on about 40% of vegetated land, temperature on 33%, sunlight on 27%](https://scholarworks.umt.edu/ntsg_pubs/126)
(split from my memory of the paper). On the other 60%, more rain means more cloud, flooding, erosion
and nutrient leaching. And since warming raises evaporative demand along with rain, getting rain by
warming does not reliably leave soils wetter. The right goal is redistribution.

**Cloud seeding after 75 years.** The physics was finally demonstrated in SNOWIE: aircraft seeded
clouds producing no natural snow, and radar followed lines of new snow to the ground
([French et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1716995115);
[Friedrich et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917204117)). Scaling to a season is the
problem. Wyoming's randomized experiment (2008-13) found
[no statistically significant effect (p = 0.28); modeling suggested +5% in seeded storms, and only about 30% of precipitation comes from seedable clouds](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/evaluation-of-the-wyoming-weather-modification-pilot-project-wwmp/).
The state's
[5-15%](https://coyotegulch.blog/2014/12/13/report-wyoming-cloud-seeding-program-said-to-increase-snowpack/)
applies to ideal storms. Idaho Power, which seeds to fill its hydro reservoirs, claims
[about 10% more snowpack and 1.24 million acre-feet a year](https://idwr.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/iwrb/CloudSeeding/CS-FAQ-sheet.-Mar-2023.pdf)
from its own models. Seeding is cheap enough to pay at a 2% true effect, so programs persist without
proof.

The UAE flies
[nearly 300 missions a year at about $8,000 per flight hour](https://english.alarabiya.net/News/gulf/2024/01/18/UAE-to-carry-out-hundreds-of-cloud-seeding-missions-in-2024-to-tackle-water-scarcity)
and claims 10-30% from before-and-after comparisons. China runs the largest program: a 2020 plan to
cover
[5.5 million km², 56% of the country](https://www.businessinsider.in/science/news/china-is-massively-expanding-its-weather-modification-program-saying-it-will-be-able-to-cover-half-the-country-in-artificial-rain-and-snow-by-2025/articleshow/79568008.cms),
with [about 35,000 staff](https://www.freethink.com/energy/cloud-seeding). Its Tianhe ("Sky River")
scheme would put
[tens of thousands of silver iodide burners across 1.6 million km² of the Tibetan Plateau, aiming for up to 10 billion m³ a year; about 500 were deployed experimentally](https://gizmodo.com/chinas-ambitious-new-rain-making-system-would-be-as-big-1825536740).
No published evidence shows it working at scale.

**Does seeding steal rain from downwind?** Clouds rain out only a small fraction of the vapor
passing through, so a few percent more barely dents the supply. A review of five projects found
downwind effects were, if anything,
[positive (5-15%) out to a couple hundred kilometers](https://data.ucar.edu/dataset/8d9009e1-1fe4-4ece-8212-89082a486c8b)
(DeFelice et al. 2014), and Wyoming modeling found
[under 0.5% change outside target areas](https://www.wylr.net/2015/01/03/weather-modification-study-shows-no-extra-area-impacts-from-cloud-seeding/).
Several of those authors work in the industry.

**The biotic pump.** Makarieva and Gorshkov claim forests do more than recycle moisture:
condensation over forest lowers air pressure and drags ocean air inland, so forests make their own
winds. Atmospheric scientists mostly reject the physics
([Meesters et al. 2009](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/13/1299/2009/)); the key paper appeared
in 2013 after years of review,
[with an editor's note saying so](https://news.mongabay.com/2013/01/controversial-research-outlines-physics-behind-how-forests-may-bring-rain/).
Recycling is established. The pump is not.

**Smaller ideas.** Condensing a liter of water from air releases 0.68 kWh of latent heat, so even a
good heat pump needs 0.15-0.25 kWh per liter against 0.0035 for seawater desalination (my
arithmetic), about 50 times worse. Towing icebergs: a 2018 Cape Town proposal priced one at
[$100 million to tow plus $50-60 million to harvest, with about 30% melting en route](https://phys.org/news/2018-07-tow-an-iceberg-ease-cape-town-drought.html),
and the city judged desalination cheaper.

### Verdict on Question 2

There is no way to "just increase global rainfall" short of warming the planet, which yields about
2% per °C, delivered as heavier storms, on a planet where most land is not water-limited. Rain can
be nudged regionally by single-digit percentages for almost nothing (seeding) or shifted by tens of
percent through continental land-use change, which takes the water from somewhere. If the goal is
water in a dry place, desalination at $0.40 per m³ is the reliable tool.

## Part 3: High CO2 With Low Warming

### How CO2 Fertilization Works

Photosynthesis runs on the enzyme Rubisco, which evolved when the air held far more CO2 and almost
no oxygen, and cannot fully tell the two apart. In C3 plants (wheat, rice, soy, potatoes, nearly all
trees) Rubisco grabs O2 roughly a quarter of the time at today's CO2, and the plant spends energy
undoing the mistake (photorespiration). More CO2 tilts the competition, so C3 photosynthesis rises.
C4 plants (maize, sorghum, sugarcane, millet) run a biochemical pump that already concentrates CO2
around Rubisco, so extra CO2 does little for them directly.

Second effect, for both types: leaves take in CO2 through pores that also leak water. With more CO2
outside, the plant can open them less for the same carbon, so it uses less water per unit of growth.
That is why C4 crops gain under drought and why dry regions green most.

### What Has Been Observed

- **Satellites.** Leaf area rose over
  [25-50% of vegetated land from 1982 to 2009 and fell over less than 4%; models attribute 70% of the trend to CO2](https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate3004)
  (Zhu et al. 2016, Nature Climate Change). The 70% is a model attribution, not a measurement.
- **Saturation.** Wang et al. (2020, Science) reported the fertilization effect
  [declined across most of the globe from 1982 to 2015, tracking nutrient and soil-water limits](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb7772).
  Three published comments attacked the satellite processing
  ([Frankenberg et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abg2947)); the authors' reply reports
  [a smaller but still significant decline](https://ddd.uab.cat/record/299972). Separately, greening
  [stalled or reversed after the late 1990s as the air's vapor-pressure deficit rose](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax1396)
  (Yuan et al. 2019): warmer air pulls water from leaves harder, pores close, growth drops. That is
  a warming effect, so cooling would remove it.
- **Open-field experiments.** Thirty years of free-air CO2 enrichment:
  [+200 ppm gives about +18% yield across 18 C3 crops without stress; about +10% under nitrogen shortage or with 2°C of warming; no gain in maize or sorghum except in drought](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.15375)
  (Ainsworth and Long 2021).
- **Forests.** In a mature eucalypt forest, +150 ppm raised carbon uptake 12% and
  [none of it was stored; it was respired back, half through soil](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2128-9)
  (Jiang et al. 2020, Nature). Across 110 tree species,
  [faster growth means earlier death](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-17966-z) (Brienen et al.
  2020).

### The Catch in the Food

Plants grown at high CO2 are bigger but more dilute. At about 550 ppm, wheat has
[9.3% less zinc, 5.1% less iron and 6.3% less protein; rice 7.8% less protein](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13179)
(Myers et al. 2014, Nature). Across 130 species, 25 minerals fall
[8% on average](https://elife.elifesciences.org/articles/03233) (Loladze 2014). Rice at
end-of-century CO2 loses
[about 10% of protein, 8% of iron, 5% of zinc, and B vitamins](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5966189)
(Zhu et al. 2018, Science Advances). Smith and Myers (2018) estimate 550 ppm would make
[an additional 175 million people zinc-deficient and 122 million protein-deficient](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-018-0253-3),
assuming unchanged diets. This is a pure CO2 effect, so cooling does not touch it. It is fixable
with fortification, breeding and richer diets, in the places least able to pay.

### What Cooling Does Not Undo

**Ocean acidification.** About a quarter of emitted CO2 dissolves in seawater and forms carbonic
acid. Surface pH has fallen
[about 0.1 since preindustrial](https://archive.ipcc.ch/publications_and_data/ar4/wg1/en/ch10s10-4-2.html)
(26% more hydrogen ions), and models project
[-0.16 by 2100 under strong mitigation and -0.44 under high emissions](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/3439/2020/)
relative to the late 1800s (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020). A sunshade does not change this chemistry. The
evidence of harm is uneven:

| Claim                                   | Evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Strength                                  |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Shellfish larvae die in corrosive water | Oregon's Whiskey Creek hatchery: [larval production tracked the carbonate saturation of intake water](https://tos.org/oceanography/assets/docs/28-2_barton.pdf) (Barton et al. 2012, reviewed in Barton et al. 2015). Hatcheries now buffer their water                         | Strong, with commercial losses            |
| Pteropod shells dissolve                | California Current: [53% of onshore individuals with severe dissolution, estimated double the preindustrial rate](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2014.0123) (Bednaršek et al. 2014)                                                                                               | Good observation; the doubling is modeled |
| Reefs calcify less                      | Whole-reef experiments on the Great Barrier Reef: restoring preindustrial chemistry [raised calcification 7%](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17155) (Albright et al. 2016); adding CO2 [cut it by about a third](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25968) (Albright et al. 2018)     | Strong                                    |
| Fish behave recklessly                  | Three years and 900+ fish found [no consistent effect](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1903-y) (Clark et al. 2020, Nature). Null results in this field are harder to publish, as the editor of the ICES Journal of Marine
Science argued when he called for more skepticism
([Browman 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/icesjms/fsw010)). The calcifier results have held up; the
fish-behavior results have not.

**Indoor cognition.** Outdoor air at 800-1,000 ppm puts every room several hundred ppm higher.
Satish et al. (2012) found
[decision-making scores fell on 6 of 9 measures at 1,000 ppm versus 600](https://doi.org/10.1289/ehp.1104789),
and Allen et al. (2016) found
[15% lower scores at about 945 ppm and 50% lower at 1,400](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26502459/).
But 36 submariners showed
[no effect even at 15,000 ppm](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10180048/1/1-s2.0-S0360132323009940-main.pdf).
Contested; I would not rest an argument on it.

### Paleo Context

Early Eocene CO2 was [around 1,400 ppm](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/89477/) (Anagnostou et
al. 2016), with forests at the poles. Life is fine at high CO2 given time. The issue is rate: the
fastest natural analog, the PETM 56 million years ago, released carbon at
[less than a tenth of today's rate](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2016/march/human-carbon-release-rate.html)
(Zeebe et al. 2016). Slow releases let ocean sediments buffer the acid.

The low end is more striking. At the last glacial maximum CO2 was 180-190 ppm, and juniper wood from
the La Brea tar pits shows trees operating
[near the minimum CO2 at which C3 photosynthesis breaks even](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC544040)
(Ward et al. 2005, PNAS). Sage (1995) proposed that agriculture arose independently on several
continents right after the ice age because CO2
[rose from under 200 to 270 ppm and removed a productivity barrier](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2486.1995.tb00009.x).

### Winners and Losers

Extra CO2 favors fast growers that put carbon into length instead of structure: vines and weeds. In
the Duke forest experiment, poison ivy grew
[faster than nearly any other woody species, and the allergenic form of its oil rose 153%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1474014)
(Mohan et al. 2006, PNAS). Where nitrogen or phosphorus is short, which includes most unmanaged
land, the response fades.

### Is "Sulfur Shield Plus Let CO2 Ride" a Good World?

**The strongest case for** comes from mainstream modeling, not from the contrarians. Fan et al.
(2021, Nature Food, with David Keith) ran crops inside an Earth-system model: by late century,
global yields were
[about 10% higher under solar geoengineering than business as usual, and 5% lower under emissions cuts](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/solar-geoengineering-can-alleviate-climate-change-pressures-crop-yields),
because cutting emissions removes the fertilization. Pongratz et al. (2012, Nature Climate Change)
found the same direction: geoengineering in a high-CO2 climate
[generally raises yields](https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate1373). Add the greening record, the
glacial-starvation evidence, and the fact that cooling removes the vapor-pressure stress now eroding
the greening.

Who has argued it: Freeman Dyson, in
["Heretical Thoughts About Science and Society" (2007)](https://www.edge.org/conversation/freeman_dyson-heretical-thoughts-about-science-and-society)
and a foreword to Indur Goklany's 2015 report "Carbon Dioxide: The Good News"; and William Happer's
CO2 Coalition. Their core fact, that fertilization is real, large and underweighted in public
discussion, is right. Dyson's own proposal, that the excess is a
[land-management problem because topsoil could absorb it](https://www.edge.org/conversation/freeman_dyson-heretical-thoughts-about-science-and-society),
was a back-of-envelope with no mechanism. Goklany's report and the CO2 Coalition lean on chamber and
greenhouse results over lower field ones
([Grantham Institute 2015](https://granthaminstitute.com/2015/10/19/carbon-dioxide-the-good-and-the-bad-the-right-and-the-wrong/))
and omit nutrient dilution, nitrogen limits and the ocean. None of them propose the sunshade half;
they argue warming is small. So the combined position has decent evidence and no serious champion.

**The strongest case against:**

1. **The ocean gets no relief.** A pH drop of up to 0.44, with strong evidence of harm to shellfish
   and reefs.
2. **The food is worse per bite.** Hundreds of millions pushed toward zinc and protein deficiency.
3. **The sunshade taxes light.** Offsetting doubled CO2 takes
   [about 1.8% less sunlight](https://doi.org/10.1029/1999GL006086) (Govindasamy and Caldeira 2000).
   Proctor et al. (2018, Nature) used the Pinatubo and El Chichón eruptions as natural experiments:
   for maize under a sulfate shield, a cooling benefit of
   [+6.3% was nearly cancelled by -5.3% from scattered light, with no significant net effect on soy, rice and wheat](https://news.berkeley.edu/2018/08/08/blocking-sunlight-to-cool-earth-wont-reduce-crop-damage-from-global-warming).
   So dimming cancels the cooling benefit, not the fertilization, which is +18% for C3 crops.
   Against that, scattered light penetrates canopies better: the hazier skies of 1960-1999 raised
   the land carbon sink by [about a quarter](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature07949) (Mercado et al.
   2009). The models and the volcano data disagree.
4. **Rain falls**, a few percent globally, concentrated in monsoon regions.
5. **The gains saturate and the commitment does not.** Fertilization flattens as nutrients bind. CO2
   stays for centuries and the aerosols last a year or two, so every year of letting CO2 ride raises
   the required shield and the size of the jump if it ever stops.

**My judgment (labeled as mine).** The premise is roughly right for land plants and crop calories,
which deserves saying because it is usually denied. As a world to aim for, it fails on the ocean,
which gets all of the cost and none of the benefit, and on being a commitment that only grows. The
defensible version is a temporary shield to shave the temperature peak while CO2 is brought down.
Ranked for plants and people together: low CO2 with low warming, then high CO2 with low warming,
then high CO2 with high warming. The middle option clearly beats the last, but the ocean chemistry
makes it a waiting room and not a destination.

## Best Anecdotes

- **213 hydrogen bombs for a canal.** Bassler's Qattara plan meant evacuating at least 25,000
  people. The CIA had pitched flooding Qattara to Eisenhower in 1957 as a way to
  ["materially alter the climate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project). Egypt
  kept the idea alive for a century and killed it in May 2026.
- **The sea that was uphill.** Roudaire's own surveys killed his Sahara Sea. He died of fever in
  1885, and Jules Verne's last novel, "Invasion of the Sea" (1905), is about the project.
- **The underground forest.** Niger's regreening began when it was noticed that the "bushes" farmers
  cleared every year were shoots from living stumps of felled trees. Stop cutting them and the
  forest regrows from below for $10 a hectare, after decades of planted seedlings dying.
- **A dead lake feeds the Amazon.** A major source of the phosphorus-bearing dust is the Bodélé
  Depression in Chad, the dry floor of Lake Mega-Chad. The rainforest is partly fertilized by the
  remains of a lake that died when the Sahara dried.
- **NATO bombed the pipe factory.** In July 2011 an air strike hit the Brega plant that makes pipe
  for the [Great Man-Made River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Man-Made_River), the system
  supplying most of Libya's water.
