## Headline Findings

- **San Francisco, curb parking only:** 275,500 on-street spaces
  ([SFMTA census, published 2017](https://www.sfmta.com/press-releases/sfmta-completes-citywide-census-street-parking-spaces))
  cover 870 to 1,140 acres. That is 2.9% to 3.8% of the city's 30,016 acres of land, almost exactly
  one Golden Gate Park (1,017 acres). Numerator: parking-lane area. Denominator: all land in the
  city.
- **San Francisco, every space:** about 634,000 spaces on-street, public off-street and private
  ([MTI/SPUR census, 2022](https://www.sfexaminer.com/archives/the-bay-area-has-twice-as-many-parking-spots-as-people-and-there-s-a/article_2ef9ce1e-1b53-5b51-a0e7-935ac32beb97.html)),
  1.7 per household. Their floor area is 2,800 to 3,800 acres, equal to 9% to 13% of the city's
  land. That is floor area, not footprint: most off-street spaces are stacked in garages or sit
  under housing.
- **New York City:** the city says "nearly three million free parking spaces" at the curb, over 96%
  of the on-street total
  ([NYC DOT, 2026](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/central-business-district-tolling-program-parking-study.pdf)).
  At 160 to 180 sq ft each that is 5.7% to 6.4% of city land, or 13 to 15 Central Parks. The only
  block-by-block inventory
  ([Scharnhorst, 2018](https://www.mba.org/docs/default-source/research---riha-reports/18806-research-riha-parking-report.pdf))
  found about 1.28 million on-street spaces: 2.5% to 2.8% of land, about 6 Central Parks. Three
  million would need 90% of every curb in the city to be legal parking, so treat it as a ceiling.
- **Detroit:** 31% of downtown's developable land is off-street parking (surface lots plus
  above-ground decks), against 3% in San Francisco and 0.4% in New York
  ([Parking Reform Network data](https://github.com/ParkingReformNetwork/parking-lot-map/blob/main/packages/primary/data/city-stats.json)).
  The denominator is a 0.8 square mile downtown, not the 139 square mile city. No citywide count
  exists. Detroit also has more than 18 square miles of vacant land, 13% of the city
  ([Detroit Future City, 2025](https://detroitfuturecity.com/2025/10/30/detroit-future-citys-new-report-calls-for-transformative-approach-to-land-conservation-in-detroit/)),
  so parking is not what stops Detroit from being green.
- **The street is the bigger number.** Streets are 25% of San Francisco's land; parking lanes are
  12% to 15% of that. In New York more than 75% of street space goes to cars, which make 38.5% of
  trips; 57% of households own none.
- **Removal is slow even where it is policy.** Paris planned to remove 60,000 of about 135,000
  on-street spaces and has removed "more than 10,000" since 2020. Amsterdam removes about 1,400 a
  year.
- **Retail:** merchants overestimate how many customers drive by a factor of two to three (Berlin:
  21.6% guessed, 6.6% actual). Controlled studies show neutral to mildly positive sales after
  parking removal, not a boom.
- **Politics:** San Francisco's District 4 supervisor was recalled over the Great Highway closure in
  September 2025 with 62.7% of the vote. Fourteen months later the measure to bring cars back lost
  66 to 34, and the precincts next to the road had flipped to favor the park.
- **Failure mode:** of about 200 pedestrian malls built in US downtowns from 1959 to the 1980s,
  roughly 89% were later reopened to cars.
- **Scenario:** converting half of San Francisco's curb parking frees 440 to 570 acres. That buys
  50,000 street trees (canopy from 13.7% to about 15.6%), a doubled protected bike network (which
  needs only 11% of spaces) and 140 miles of wider sidewalk.

## Definitions: Four Kinds of Parking, Three Denominators

No US city counts all four kinds.

| Kind                                   | Typical area per space                       | Who counts it                           |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| On-street (curb)                       | 8 ft by 17 to 22 ft, 140 to 180 sq ft        | City transportation agencies, sometimes |
| Off-street surface lot                 | 300 to 350 sq ft with aisles                 | Aerial imagery                          |
| Structured garage                      | 300 to 350 sq ft of floor per space, stacked | Tax assessors, patchily                 |
| Private residential garage or driveway | 200 to 330 sq ft                             | Nobody; modeled from zoning codes       |

"Percent of the city" can mean all land in the municipality, the street right-of-way only, or a
hand-drawn downtown. Detroit's famous 30%-plus and San Francisco's 3% use the third. The curb
figures in this note use the first.

## San Francisco

**Counts.** SFMTA finished a manual count of every block in 2014 and published it in 2017:
[275,500 on-street spaces, about 10% of them metered, plus 166,500 publicly available spaces in garages and lots, for "over 442,000"](https://www.sfmta.com/press-releases/sfmta-completes-citywide-census-street-parking-spaces).
This is still the latest census. The sampled 2010 census gave
[441,541 and noted that the on-street spaces cover "roughly the equivalent area of Golden Gate Park"](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2010/03/30/san-francisco-first-city-in-the-nation-to-count-its-parking-spaces).
Private parking was never counted.

Two later studies fill the gap. The county transportation authority estimated
[172,000 off-street nonresidential spaces citywide, 87,400 of them in the northeast quadrant](https://www.sfcta.org/sites/default/files/2019-03/Parking_Supply_summary_report_11.29.16.pdf)
(SFCTA, 2016). The [MTI/SPUR census](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/60934) (Chester, Helmrich
and Li, 2022) modeled every Bay Area parcel from zoning codes and reported
[634,000 spaces in San Francisco, split evenly between on-street and off-street](https://www.sfexaminer.com/archives/the-bay-area-has-twice-as-many-parking-spots-as-people-and-there-s-a/article_2ef9ce1e-1b53-5b51-a0e7-935ac32beb97.html).
It put nonresidential off-street at 153,000, which leaves about 164,000 private residential spaces
(my subtraction: 317,000 minus 153,000). Treat that as a floor: the model assumed no parking in
pre-1930 buildings, many of which had garages cut into them later.

A check from the demand side: San Francisco's 371,841 households hold
[372,035 vehicles, and 33% of households have none](https://censusreporter.org/data/table/?table=B25044&geo_ids=16000US0667000,16000US3651000,16000US2622000)
(ACS 2024). That is 1.7 parking spaces per household vehicle.

**Area.** SFMTA says the on-street spaces laid end to end run nearly 900 miles.

- Lane geometry: 900 miles x 5,280 ft x 8 ft wide = 38.0 million sq ft = 873 acres = 1.36 sq mi.
- Per-space rule of thumb: 275,500 x 160 to 180 sq ft = 1,012 to 1,138 acres.
- City land: 46.9 sq mi x 640 = 30,016 acres. Curb parking is 2.9% to 3.8% of it.
- Golden Gate Park is [1,017 acres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Gate_Park). Curb parking is
  0.86 to 1.12 of one.
- Off-street nonresidential: 172,000 x 300 to 350 sq ft = 1,185 to 1,382 acres of floor.
- Private residential: 164,000 x 200 to 330 sq ft = 753 to 1,242 acres of floor.
- Total: 2,810 to 3,760 acres, 4.4 to 5.9 sq mi, 9.4% to 12.5% of city land if laid flat, 2.8 to 3.7
  Golden Gate Parks.

**Right-of-way.**
[Streets are 25% of San Francisco's land](https://sfplanning.s3.amazonaws.com/archives/BetterStreets/docs/Draft_BSP_for_Adoption_1_Introduction.pdf)
(Better Streets Plan, 2010), about 7,500 acres. Parking lanes are 12% to 15% of that. A 2014 study
put them at
[15% of paved roadway area, 902 miles long against 143 miles of bike lanes, on land worth $8 billion to $35 billion](https://sf.streetsblog.org/2014/11/03/study-quantifies-how-unbalanced-sfs-car-centric-streets-are).
I found no official split of the right-of-way into travel lanes, parking and sidewalk. On one
generic block (60 ft wall to wall, two 10 ft sidewalks, two 8 ft parking lanes, two 12 ft travel
lanes) parking is 27% of the width; intersections, driveways, red curbs and arterials pull the
citywide share down.

## New York City

**The 3 million problem.** NYC DOT's 2026 parking study says
["over 96% of on-street parking in our city remains ostensibly free"](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/central-business-district-tolling-program-parking-study.pdf)
and refers to "nearly three million free parking spaces." It is an estimate, not a count. An older
rough estimate by Rachel Weinberger, extrapolated from two neighborhoods, was
[3.4 to 4.4 million, with 81,875 metered](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2011/03/22/new-york-has-81875-metered-parking-spaces-and-millions-of-free-ones)
(2011).

The check: New York has [6,300 miles of streets](https://nyc25x25.org/25x25report.html). Two curbs
each gives 66.5 million feet. At 20 ft per car that is 3.33 million spaces if every foot of every
curb, including highways, hydrants, driveways, bus stops and crosswalks, were legal parking. Three
million needs 90% of it. Scharnhorst's inventory, built by joining the city's parking-sign database
to street geometry, found
[1,965,377 spaces of all kinds, 65.3% of them on-street](https://www.mba.org/docs/default-source/research---riha-reports/18806-research-riha-parking-report.pdf)
(2018). That is 1.28 million at the curb, 39% of curb length. It also lines up with demand: the
city's households own 2.01 million vehicles. My read: the true number is between 1.3 and 3 million
and closer to the low end.

**Area.** [Central Park is 843 acres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park). City land is
300.5 sq mi, or 192,300 acres.

| On-street count            | Area at 160 to 180 sq ft                    | Share of city land | Central Parks |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------- |
| 3.0 million (NYC DOT)      | 11,000 to 12,400 acres (17.2 to 19.4 sq mi) | 5.7% to 6.4%       | 13.1 to 14.7  |
| 1.28 million (Scharnhorst) | 4,700 to 5,300 acres (7.4 to 8.3 sq mi)     | 2.5% to 2.8%       | 5.6 to 6.3    |

Off-street, Scharnhorst's remaining 34.7% is about 682,000 spaces, or 5,200 acres of floor at 330 sq
ft each.

**Street space against trips.** Transportation Alternatives'
[25x25 report](https://nyc25x25.org/25x25report.html) (2021) splits the city's street space as more
than 75% for cars (driving and parking), 24% sidewalk, 0.93% bike lanes and 0.02% bus lanes. Cars
make
[38.5% of trips, walking 37.6%, transit 20.7%](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/2024-cms-report.pdf)
(NYC DOT, 2024), and 56.7% of households have no vehicle (ACS 2024). The report asked for 25% of car
space by 2025, sized as 13 Central Parks. It was not met.

## Detroit

**The downtown number.** The Parking Reform Network's map traces surface lots and above-ground
parking structures from OpenStreetMap and satellite imagery. It excludes street, underground and
podium parking. The denominator is a hand-drawn "central city" with a quarter knocked off for
streets. I recomputed it from their published geometry:

| City          | Central-city boundary  | Parking lots and decks | Share of 75% of boundary | Published |
| ------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------ | --------- |
| Detroit       | 514 acres (0.80 sq mi) | 120 acres              | 31.2%                    | 31%       |
| San Francisco | 251 acres (0.39 sq mi) | 5.1 acres              | 2.7%                     | 3%        |
| New York      | 554 acres (0.86 sq mi) | 2.0 acres              | 0.5%                     | 0.4%      |

So San Francisco's 3% describes the Financial District and nearby SoMa, 0.8% of the city, and New
York's 0.4% describes a slice of Midtown. Across the 108 cities in the
[dataset](https://github.com/ParkingReformNetwork/parking-lot-map/blob/main/packages/primary/data/city-stats.json),
New York is lowest and San Francisco second lowest, then Washington (4%), Boston (6%) and Chicago
(7%). The highest are San Bernardino (49%), San Juan (41%), Arlington, Texas (39%) and Lexington
(38%). The median is 27%. A separate 2013 count by Rob Linn put parking at
[39.2% of downtown Detroit's land](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2013-08-21/how-too-much-parking-strangled-the-motor-city)
(unverified, page blocked).

**History.** Detroit's lots are not the product of one clearance. Commercial lot operators were
buying and flattening buildings at downtown's edge
[from 1917, with dozens of lots by the 1930s](https://core.ac.uk/download/386137931.pdf)
(unverified, snippet only). The Michigan Theatre (1926) was gutted in 1977 and turned into a
160-space parking deck with the plaster ceiling left in place (unverified, snippet only). The
process is still running: in 2019 the Saturday Night Building was demolished
[to make room for 12 parking spots](https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/2019/11/19/demolition-begins-on-detroits-saturday-night-building-to-make-room-for-12-parking-spots).
Advocates say
[downtown has more parking now than ever](https://archive.strongtowns.org/journal/2020/11/26/detroiters-push-for-parking-reform-in-the-heart-of-motown)
(2020).

**Vacant land.** Detroit Future City counts
[more than 18 square miles of vacant land and over 100,000 vacant lots, nearly 60% publicly owned](https://detroitfuturecity.com/2025/10/30/detroit-future-citys-new-report-calls-for-transformative-approach-to-land-conservation-in-detroit/)
(2025). That is 13% of the city and 96 times the downtown parking acreage.

**Land value tax.** Surface lots are cheap to hold because property tax falls mostly on buildings,
and a lot has none. Mayor Duggan's 2023 plan would have
[cut 14 mills from everything and added 118.3 mills on land only](https://detroitmi.gov/sites/detroitmi.localhost/files/2023-10/Land%20Value%20Tax%20Proposal_City%20Council_10-10-23.pdf),
taking the rate on land from 85.6 to about 190 mills. The city projected
[the median vacant lot's bill up 185% and scrapyards' up 100%](https://detroitmi.gov/node/67861),
with 97% of homeowners paying less. The enabling bills stalled in the Michigan House in October 2023
and have not come back.

## National Context

**How many spaces.**
[Chester, Horvath and Madanat](https://escholarship.org/content/qt16r4t7p7/qt16r4t7p7.pdf) (2010,
summarized 2011) built four scenarios: 722 million, 810 million, 844 million and 2 billion. The
spread comes from assumptions about what nobody measures. The low scenarios count commercial spaces
from floor area plus one home space per vehicle. The 844 million case assumes 3.4 spaces per
vehicle. The 2 billion case assumes 8 per vehicle, a planners' rule of thumb the authors call
unverified. Most of the gap is unmarked curb on residential streets, which is a space only if you
choose to count it. The middle scenarios put paved parking at 0.64% to 0.90% of US land, 22,600 to
31,800 square miles (my multiplication). That is the size of West Virginia (24,230 sq mi).

**Scharnhorst (2018)** replaced guesses with parcel-level inventories of five cities:

| City         | Spaces    | Per household | Replacement cost | Cost per household |
| ------------ | --------- | ------------- | ---------------- | ------------------ |
| New York     | 1,965,377 | 0.6           | $20.6 billion    | $6,570             |
| Philadelphia | 2,172,896 | 3.7           | $17.5 billion    | $29,974            |
| Seattle      | 1,596,289 | 5.2           | $35.8 billion    | $117,677           |
| Des Moines   | 1,613,659 | 19.4          | $6.4 billion     | $77,165            |
| Jackson, WY  | 100,119   | 27.1          | $711 million     | $192,138           |

Seattle's parking is worth more than New York's because Seattle built garages on expensive land.

**Shoup.** The High Cost of Free Parking (2005) makes three arguments that have held up.

1. Underpriced curbs cause cruising. Across
   [sixteen studies from 1927 to 2001, cruising averaged 30% of traffic and 8.1 minutes per search](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6sn7s1x2/qt6sn7s1x2.pdf)
   (Shoup, 2007). Only six of the sixteen measured a traffic share, from 8% to 74%, in places chosen
   because they were congested, so 30% is not a citywide average. In Westwood Village, 470
   underpriced meters generated 950,000 excess vehicle-miles a year. The fix is to price each block
   so about 85% of spaces are full.
2. Minimum parking requirements are a hidden tax. Cities copied ratios from each other, the cost of
   required spaces got bundled into rents and prices, and the subsidy came to
   [$127 billion to $374 billion in 2002, 1.2% to 3.6% of GDP](https://cato-unbound.org/2011/04/26/donald-shoup/are-minimum-parking-requirements-social-engineering).
   The requirements also spread buildings apart, which makes walking and transit worse, which raises
   parking demand.
3. Meter revenue should stay on the block. Old Pasadena's 690 meters returned
   [$1.2 million a year, $1,712 per meter](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3vq8794s/qt3vq8794s.pdf),
   to sidewalk and alley repairs, which is why merchants there asked for meters (Kolozsvari and
   Shoup, 2003).

**What a space costs now.** WGI's 2026 outlook puts the
[median above-ground structured space at $33,300, with San Francisco highest at $43,000](https://www.parking-mobility.org/news/wgi-releases-2026-parking-structure-cost-outlook/),
before land, soft costs (15% to 25% more) and anything underground. Rebuilding San Francisco's
166,500 public off-street spaces at $43,000 each would cost $7.2 billion.

Paris's air got cleaner mostly for other reasons: Airparif attributes a quarter of the NO2 decline
to reduced traffic and half to cleaner vehicles. Paris also governs by tiny electorates. The 2024
vote to triple parking fees for SUVs (to €18 an hour in the center) passed with
[54.55% on 5.68% turnout](https://www.paris.fr/pages/plus-ou-moins-de-suv-les-parisiens-et-parisiennes-sont-invites-a-voter-le-4-fevrier-25381),
and the 2025 vote to pedestrianize and plant 500 more streets passed with 65.96% on 4.06% turnout.
The mayor elected in 2026
[says he will continue the policy](https://fr.motor1.com/news/790813/projet-automobile-paris-emmanuel-gregoire-maire/).

Barcelona has the best health model and the worst legal record.
[Mueller et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2019.105132) (2020) estimated that building all
503 planned superblocks would avoid 667 premature deaths a year (95% interval 235 to 1,098): about
291 from cleaner air, 163 from less noise, 117 from less heat. That is a model, not a measurement.
Meanwhile a court annulled the flagship Consell de Cent green street on procedural grounds,
[upheld in 2025](https://www.poderjudicial.es/cgpj/en/Judiciary/Novelties/El-TSJ-de-Cataluna-confirma-la-sentencia-contra-el-eje-verde-de-la-calle-Consell-de-Cent-de-Barcelona).
The street has not been rebuilt for cars.

Japan never gave the curb away. The 1962 garage law makes it
[illegal to use a road as a storage place: no parking in one spot for 12 hours, or 8 hours at night](https://hourei.net/law/337AC0000000145).
To register a car you need a police-issued certificate proving you own or lease
[an off-street space within 2 km of home](https://www.police.pref.kanagawa.jp/tetsuzuki/english4/engf4001.html).
The cost of storing a car lands on its owner at the moment of purchase, and
[a leased space counts](https://www.parkingreformatlas.org/parking-reform-cases-1/japan%27s-proof-of-parking-rule-%28shako-shomeisho%29),
so a private market of small pay lots fills odd parcels. Because the curb never became storage,
residential streets can stay narrow and are shared by walkers and slow traffic. Tokyo has
[0.405 cars per household against 1.009 nationally](https://www.airia.or.jp/publish/file/kenbetsu2025.pdf)
(2025).

## San Francisco's and New York's Own Experiments

- **Parklets.** The first
  [opened on Divisadero in March 2010](https://sf.streetsblog.org/2010/03/18/newsom-christens-new-mojo-cafe-parklet-pledges-more-to-come/).
  There were about 50 to 70 before the pandemic and
  [more than 2,000 approved permits at the peak; 371 are permitted and compliant now](https://sfpublicworks.org/sites/default/files/Commissions/May%2014%202026/Item%206_PWC%20Shared%20Spaces%20Program%202026-5-14%20v56.pdf)
  (May 2026).
- **JFK Promenade.** Car-free since April 2020, made permanent by Prop J in 2022 with 63%.
  [12.6 million visits in its first four years over 1.5 miles](https://sfrecpark.org/CivicAlerts.asp?AID=1792)
  (2024).
- **Sunset Dunes.** Prop K (November 2024) passed 54.7% citywide while
  [the west side voted 64% against](https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/sf-great-highway-sunset-dunes-prop-g/).
  The park
  [opened April 12, 2025: 2 miles, 50 acres](https://sfrecpark.org/CivicAlerts.asp?AID=2183), and
  drew
  [1.7 million visits in its first year](https://sf.streetsblog.org/2026/04/09/sunset-dunes-one-year-out-they-built-it-and-people-came).
  Supervisor Joel Engardio was
  [recalled on September 16, 2025 with 62.7%](https://missionlocal.org/2025/09/sf-district-4-joel-engardio-election-results/).
  Prop G, to return cars on weekdays, went to the ballot on November 3, 2026 and
  [failed, 34.3% to 65.7%](https://www.kqed.org/elections/results/sanfrancisco/proposition-g) (count
  as of November 10, not yet certified). The eight precincts touching the road
  [went from 40% pro-park in 2024 to 58%](https://sfstandard.com/2026/11/05/great-highway-s-neighbors-flipped-saved-park-once-hated/).
  It remains a park.
- **Slow Streets.** 18 permanent corridors.
  [Collisions fell 48% against 14% citywide, but 12 of 16 streets still miss the speed target](https://sfmta.com/project-updates/2023-slow-streets-evaluation-report)
  (2023).
- **Freeway removals.** Around the demolished Embarcadero Freeway,
  [housing grew 54% in a decade against 31% nearby, and jobs 23% against 5.5%](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/33q2012g)
  (Cervero, Kang and Shively, 2009). The Central Freeway carried 93,100 vehicles a day; Octavia
  Boulevard carries 44,900, and nearby home prices jumped $116,000 the year it opened. The 22 freed
  parcels, about 7 acres, were planned for
  [750 to 900 homes](https://urbanland.uli.org/planning-design/freeway-boulevard?p=25).
- **Market Street.** Private cars banned January 29, 2020.
  [Muni ran about 6% faster and cycling rose 25%](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/car-free-market-street-faster-muni-traffic-impact-inrix-study/).
  In August 2025 the mayor
  [let Waymo and black-car services back on](https://www.kqed.org/news/12065918/); the formal
  evaluation is pending.
- **Parking minimums.** Abolished citywide in December 2018 by a
  [7 to 4 vote](https://sf.streetsblog.org/2018/12/17/san-francisco-eliminates-parking-minimums).
  San Francisco was the largest US city to do so at the time, not the first.
- **Daylighting.** California's AB 413 bans parking within 20 feet of a crosswalk approach. SFMTA
  estimates it removes
  [about 13,775 spaces, 5% of the curb supply](https://sfstandard.com/2024/11/10/san-francisco-loses-thousands-parking-spaces/);
  [65% of intersections were marked by May 2026](https://www.sfmta.com/Daylighting). That is 45
  miles of curb, imposed by the state.

**New York.** The
[14th Street busway](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2019/12/18/mayor-finally-takes-his-busway-victory-lap-highlighting-need-for-more-car-free-transit-routes)
(2019) cut bus travel times 22% to 47% and raised ridership 24%, with cross streets 0 to 2 minutes
slower. On the 34th Avenue open street in Queens
[pedestrian crashes fell 60%](https://qns.com/2025/12/selfhelp-audit-34th-avenue-open-street/).
Outdoor dining went from 12,000 restaurants using about 8,500 parking spaces to
[2,314 licenses by August 2026](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/investigation-of-processing-times-in-dots-dining-out-nyc-program/)
after the city added fees and a winter teardown rule. Containerized trash would take
[150,000 spaces, about 5% of the total](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2023/05/03/sanitation-trash-containerization-can-be-done-with-a-historic-reuse-of-parking-pilot-to-start-uptown-this-fall),
with
[citywide rollout set for 2031](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/04/mayor-mamdani-takes-major-step-toward-citywide-trash-containeriz).
Congestion pricing ($9, from January 5, 2025) produced in its first year
[73,000 fewer vehicles a day (down 11%), crashes down 7%, PM2.5 down 22%, subway entries up 9% and $550 million in net revenue](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/less-traffic-better-transit-its-first-anniversary-governor-hochul-celebrates-transformational).
The feared park-and-ride surge at the zone's edge did not appear.

## A Concrete Scenario: Half of San Francisco's Curb Parking

Half is 137,750 spaces, 450 miles of curb lane: 436 acres by lane geometry, up to 569 acres at 180
sq ft a space. That is about half a Golden Gate Park, or nine to eleven Sunset Dunes, spread in
8-foot ribbons through every neighborhood. One way to spend it:

| Use                                | Spaces | Arithmetic                                  | What it buys                                                                                                                 |
| ---------------------------------- | ------ | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Daylighting (already law)          | 13,775 | 5% of supply                                | 45 miles of clear corners                                                                                                    |
| Protected bike lanes               | 30,600 | 100 miles of curb lane / 17.25 ft per space | Lanes on both sides of 50 miles of street, doubling the roughly 50 miles of separated bikeway now (unverified, snippet only) |
| Street trees in planters           | 50,000 | One tree per space                          | 50,000 trees on top of [125,000 today](https://sfpublicworks.org/trees)                                                      |
| Wider sidewalks, parklets, loading | 43,375 | Remainder; x 17.25 ft = 142 miles           | 137 acres of sidewalk, 8 ft wider along 142 miles of block face                                                              |

**Canopy.** San Francisco's canopy is [13.7%](https://sfplanning.org/node/779) (2014), among the
lowest of large US cities. A medium street tree with a 25 ft crown shades 491 sq ft. 50,000 x 491 =
24.5 million sq ft = 564 acres = 1.9% of city land, taking canopy to about 15.6% at maturity, 20 to
30 years out. The trees occupy 158 acres of parking lane and shade 3.6 times that, because crowns
overhang the street and sidewalk. At about $2,200 to plant and establish each (unverified, snippet
only) the bill is $110 million.

**Stormwater.** San Francisco has a combined sewer, so in heavy rain street runoff and sewage
overflow together: about ten discharges a year,
[more than 1.8 billion gallons annually](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/united-states-and-california-take-enforcement-action-against-san-francisco-violations)
by EPA's count (2024). One acre of pavement sheds 22.89 inches x 27,154 gallons = 621,600 gallons a
year. If the 158 acres of tree planters are built as rain gardens that each drain ten times their
own area (my assumption, unverified for San Francisco soils) and capture 80%, they manage 158 x 10 x
621,600 x 0.8 = 790 million gallons a year. The utility's goal is
[1 billion gallons a year by 2050, with 280 million managed today](https://www.sfpuc.gov/about-us/news/sf-green-sponge-stormwater-here-ordinance-made-it-possible).
One measured data point: the
[Holloway green street captured 764,000 gallons, 78% of its runoff](https://www.sfpuc.gov/node/43),
in one wet season.

**Housing** comes from off-street lots, not curbs. An acre of surface lot holds 132 cars (43,560 /
330). The Central Freeway parcels were planned at 107 to 129 homes an acre (750 to 900 on 7 acres).
So midrise housing replaces surface parking at about one home per space. If 10% of San Francisco's
172,000 nonresidential off-street spaces are in surface lots (my assumption; no citywide figure
exists), that is 130 acres and 14,000 to 17,000 homes. Downtown Detroit's 120 acres would hold
13,000 to 15,500 at the same density.

## Measured Effects

**Retail.** The consistent finding is a perception gap.

| Place                                                                                                                                               | Merchants' guess of customers arriving by car | Measured                |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Berlin, two shopping streets ([von Schneidemesser and Betzien, 2021](https://findingspress.org/article/24497))                                      | 21.6%                                         | 6.6%                    |
| Toronto, Bloor Street ([TCAT, 2017](https://ero.ontario.ca/public/public_uploads/2024-11/Bloor-Economic-Impact-Study-Full-Report-2019-09-03_4.pdf)) | Most said 25% or more                         | Under 10%               |
| San Francisco, Polk Street ([SFMTA, 2013](https://www.sfmta.com/media/1902/download))                                                               | n/a                                           | 15% by car, 58% on foot |

The sales evidence is weaker than advocates say. On Bloor, after about 160 spaces became bike lanes,
the share of merchants serving 100-plus customers on a Saturday rose from 46% to 62%, but the
control street rose more, and the authors conclude "positive, or at least neutral." Drivers
reporting trouble parking went from 8% to 33%. A review of 15 studies
([Volker and Handy, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2021.1912849)) found effects on retail
and food service "generally positive or non-significant," with possible harm to car-oriented
businesses (unverified, snippet only). Card-transaction data from Spanish cities shows
pedestrianized streets gaining sales,
[mostly at cafés and restaurants](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/402932) (Yoshimura et al.,
2022).

**Heat.** In a Davis parking lot,
[unshaded asphalt ran above 60°C and tree-shaded asphalt just under 40°C](https://fs.usda.gov/psw/topics/urban_forestry/products/11/psw_cufr68_EffectsTreeCoverOnEmissions.pdf)
(Scott, Simpson and McPherson, 1999). For air temperature, EPA's current summary of 308 studies says
urban forests average [3.0°F cooler](https://www.epa.gov/heatislands/benefits-trees-and-vegetation)
than non-green urban areas (2026). The often-quoted "20 to 45°F cooler" surface figure is from 1990s
studies and is no longer on EPA's live page.

**Injuries, noise, property.** Ghent's crashes fell 25%; Oslo and Pontevedra reached zero pedestrian
deaths. Ljubljana measured about 6 dB less noise, a quarter of the sound energy. The Embarcadero and
Octavia figures are the best property-value evidence, and both involve removing a freeway, not a
parking lane.

## The Other Side

**People with disabilities.** San Francisco has
[about 840 blue zones against 26,500 metered spaces, 3.2%, short of its own 4% goal](https://sfmta.com/accessibility-strategy-needs-assessment-2024/streets-capital-projects/15-accessible-parking-and-loading)
(2024). A car at the door is the mobility aid for many people. A serious plan adds accessible spaces
as it removes general ones.

**Trades and deliveries.** Delivery volumes are rising, which argues for converting car storage to
loading, not everything to planters.

**Transit-poor neighborhoods.** The west side voted 64% against closing the Great Highway and
recalled its supervisor. Removing parking where the bus comes every 20 minutes is a tax on people
with no substitute.

**Merchants, sometimes.** Valencia Street's center bike lane (August 2023) removed parking and
loading, and merchants blamed it for a bad year. The city controller found Valencia's 2023 sales tax
at 75% of 2019, among the weakest of 29 corridors, but
[no statistical basis for linking that to the bikeway](https://sf.gov/file/valencia-economic-context-statistical-detail),
adding that this "does not mean that no business was adversely affected" (2024). The lane's
[first-year collisions were 28 against a prior average of 26](https://www.sfmta.com/media/41378/download),
so it also failed on its own terms. It was rebuilt at the curb in 2025.

**Pedestrian malls.** About 200 US downtowns closed their main street to cars between 1959 and the
1980s. Roughly
[89% were later reopened; 80% of the survivors are in places under 100,000 people](https://viewpointvancouver.ca/2014/02/12/the-failed-experiment-of-the-american-pedestrian-mall/),
typically with a university, a beach or tourists (Judge, 2013, via a secondary source). Fresno's
Fulton Mall lasted 1964 to 2017 and cost [$22.4 million](https://www.fresno.gov/?p=1329) to undo.
The malls removed cars from downtowns whose customers had already left for the suburbs. European
pedestrian zones sit on residential density.

**Pricing and equity.** SFpark (2011 to 2013) moved meter prices block by block toward a target
occupancy.
[Search time fell 43%, cruising miles fell 30%, citations fell 23%, and the average meter rate went down 4%](https://www.sfmta.com/node/12570)
(SFMTA, 2014); an independent evaluation estimated
[cruising cut by half](https://millardball.its.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/22/2022/06/Millard-Ball_Weinberger_Hampshire_2014_Assessing_the_impacts_SFPark.pdf)
(Millard-Ball, Weinberger and Hampshire, 2014). But about 90% of San Francisco's curb spaces have no
meter, and the politics of charging residents for what has always been free are worse than the
economics. Shoup's answer, returning revenue to the block, is the only version with a record of
merchants asking for it.

**Autonomous vehicles.** Waymo runs
[more than 1,200 vehicles in the Bay Area](https://sf.gazetteer.co/waymo-has-been-slapped-with-more-than-8300-parking-tickets-since-january-2025),
stored in [about 30 depots, mostly in the industrial southeast](https://missionlocal.org/?p=870286),
not at the curb (2026). In principle a shared fleet cuts parking demand: simulations find up to 90%
for the households that switch
([Zhang et al., 2015](https://catalog.comses.net/publications/10982)), and a Denver study found
[26% of ride-hail trips replaced one that would have needed parking](https://jtlu.org/index.php/jtlu/article/view/1392/1227)
(Henao and Marshall, 2019). The catch is
[Millard-Ball](https://millardball.its.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/22/2022/06/Millard-Ball_2019_The_autonomous_vehicle_parking_problem_parking_preprint.pdf)
(2019): for a driverless car, circling costs about $0.48 an hour, less than any meter, so unpriced
streets get cruising in place of parking, and vehicle travel in dense cores could more than double.
AVs make the curb more valuable for pickup and make road pricing necessary. I found no data showing
they have reduced car ownership in San Francisco.

## Verdict (My Judgment)

This section is opinion.

**San Francisco: 15% to 20% of curb parking in fifteen years is realistic; 35% to 40% is feasible;
50% is not, without something else changing.** Five percent is already going to daylighting. Another
10% to 15% (30,000 to 40,000 spaces) covers a real protected bike network, bus lanes, loading zones
and trees on the corridors that flood and bake. That pace, 1% a year, is what Paris manages with
more political support. Beyond about 35% the constraint is storage: 372,000 household vehicles,
275,500 curb spaces and a private garage stock that is partly full of boxes. Getting to half needs
car ownership to fall by a quarter, or residential permits priced near garage rents. Off-street
surface lots should all go; the land is worth far more as housing.

**New York: half or more of the curb in Manhattan and the inner boroughs, 20% to 25% citywide.** New
York has the weakest case for free curb storage of any US city: 57% of households own no car, and
the city already needs 5% of spaces for trash containers. Congestion pricing showed that the feared
effects mostly do not happen. The obstacle is that the curb is free, so every space has a defender
and no price says which ones matter.

**Detroit: the wrong question.** Downtown's 120 acres of lots are a land-market failure, and a land
value tax is the right tool; most would be built on within a generation if holding them stopped
being cheap. But Detroit does not need parking to become green. It has 18 square miles of vacant
land already. Its lesson for the other two is that empty space is not an amenity by itself.

**What it would be like.** Not a change you would notice from the air: 3% to 4% of the land, in
ribbons. On the ground it is a street with a continuous tree canopy, a protected bike lane and
corners you can see around. The measured package is roughly: NO2 down 20% to 25% on treated streets,
pedestrian deaths approaching zero where speeds also fall, restaurants up, hardware stores annoyed,
and several years of furious local politics that reverse once people have used the result.

## Best Anecdotes

- San Francisco's curb parking covers one Golden Gate Park. End to end it is 900 miles, longer than
  California's 840-mile coast.
- Des Moines has 1.6 million parking spaces for 83,141 households, 19 each. Jackson, Wyoming has 27
  per household.
- Detroit's Saturday Night Building came down in 2019 for 12 parking spots. The Michigan Theatre is
  a parking deck with its 1926 ceiling still overhead, on
  [the site of the shed where Henry Ford built his first car](https://theclio.com/entry/24963)
  (unverified, snippet only).
- Joel Engardio lost his seat over a park in September 2025. In November 2026 the blocks facing that
  park voted 58% to keep it.
- A Japanese car buyer must show the police a parking space before the car can be registered. Tokyo
  households own 0.4 cars each.
- New York
  [legalized overnight street parking only in 1952](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/central-business-district-tolling-program-parking-study.pdf).

## Could Not Verify

- **Private residential parking in San Francisco.** No count exists. The 164,000 figure is my
  subtraction from a modeled total and is probably low.
- **New York's on-street total.** DOT's "nearly three million" and Scharnhorst's 1.28 million cannot
  both be right; I could not find DOT's method.
- **Detroit:** any citywide parking count, the share of land in streets, and a dated history of
  downtown demolitions.
- **An official breakdown of San Francisco's right-of-way** into travel lanes, parking and sidewalk.
  The 15% figure is from a 2014 advocacy study reported second-hand.
- **Citywide acreage of surface lots in San Francisco.** The 10% share in the housing scenario is an
  assumption, as is the rain-garden sizing ratio.
- **Whether Amsterdam hit its 2025 target.** The last official tally I found runs through 2022.
- **Barcelona superblock noise in decibels and business effects**, and a primary dataset for
  Pontevedra.
- **Pedestrian malls.** Judge's 89% reached me through a secondary quotation, and counts of how many
  malls were built range from 120 to over 200.
- **Seen only in search snippets:** Hoboken's zero traffic deaths since 2017 after daylighting, New
  York's Open Streets mileage (83 miles at peak, about 20 by 2022) and package volumes (1.8 million
  a day to 2.5 million), TfL's "40% more spending" claim, the Sustrans Bristol survey (merchants
  guessed 41% by car, actual 22%), an average of $73,000 per underground space (UCLA, 2026), the
  Prop J and Prop K citywide vote shares, and the March 2026 court ruling for congestion pricing.
- **Leads that were wrong or stale:** Zurich's cap was scrapped in 2019. Paris has removed about
  10,000 spaces, not tens of thousands. Embarcadero housing growth was 54%, not 51%. The Central
  Freeway parcels were planned for 750 to 900 homes, not 1,000. US parking area compares to West
  Virginia, not Connecticut. The "one shared AV replaces 11 cars" figure is Fagnant and Kockelman,
  not Zhang.
