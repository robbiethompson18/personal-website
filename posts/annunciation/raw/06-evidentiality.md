# Pirahã, grammatical evidentiality, and whether it changes thought

## 1. What he was remembering

**Pirahã (Brazil, ~400 speakers) is real, and the thing he half-remembers is Daniel Everett's
"immediacy of experience principle" (IEP). It is a claim about the culture, not a grammar rule that
forces you to mark sources.** Everett, an ex-missionary turned linguist, argued in _Current
Anthropology_ (2005) that "Pirahã culture constrains communication to nonabstract subjects which
fall within the immediate experience of interlocutors". He defined an experience as immediate "if it
has been seen or recounted as seen by a person alive at the time of telling". In his 2009 reply he
restated it more loosely: declarative utterances contain only assertions "experienced (i.e. seen,
overheard, deduced, etc. – as per the range of Pirahã evidentials …) by the speaker or as witnessed
by someone alive during the lifetime of the speaker". So "you can only say you saw it, or that some
specific living person saw it" is a fair gloss. The one caveat is that hearsay and deduction are
allowed, as long as the chain ends at a living witness. The Jesus story follows from this. Per
Colapinto's 2007 _New Yorker_ profile, the Pirahã "had always reacted to stories about Christ by
asking, 'Have you met this man?'" When Everett admitted he hadn't, and that nobody he knew had, the
stories stopped counting as information. Everett later became an atheist.

**He is also blending in a second, separate phenomenon: obligatory grammatical evidentiality.**
Pirahã does have evidential suffixes (-híai hearsay, -xáagahá observed, -sibiga deduced; Everett
1986). But the languages where grammar truly forbids a bare past-tense statement are the Vaupés
languages, Tuyuca and Tariana, and the Panoan language Matsés. Those systems are well established.
The IEP itself is heavily contested (see section 4), and Everett's own 2009 restatement says it "was
never intended to be a hard and fast deductive explanation".

## 2. Most interesting findings

1. **Tuyuca: five ways to say "he played soccer", and you must pick one** (Barnes 1984, _IJAL_
   50:255–271, as reproduced in Kroeger's textbook, LibreTexts, 2022:
   https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Linguistics/Analyzing_Meaning_-_An_Introduction_to_Semantics_and_Pragmatics_(Kroeger)/17%3A_Evidentiality/17.02%3A_Some_common_types_of_evidential_systems).
   The forms are _díiga apé-wi_ (I saw it), _-ti_ (I heard it but didn't see it), _-yi_ (I saw
   evidence, e.g. his shoe print on the field), _-yigi_ (someone told me) and _-hĩyi_ (it's a
   reasonable assumption). WALS notes that the Tuyuca affixes also carry tense, person, number and
   gender. The evidential is fused into the verb ending, so you can't leave it out.

2. **Aymara: using the eyewitness form when you didn't witness it makes you "a liar" and rude**
   (M.J. Hardman, "Data source and person in the Jaqi languages", Spanish original 1988–89:
   https://mason.gmu.edu/~ataylor/dvd_project/Readings/Data%20source%20and%20person%20in%20Jaqi.pdf).
   Aymara has five forms of "she went":
   - _Sariwa_: personal witness. "If the form is used in other contexts the speaker is judged a liar
     and as one who shows disrespect towards the listener."
   - _Sariw siw_: "through language". This covers anything you read in a book, and Hardman stresses
     that it is "not a question of truth or falsity, but rather one of data source".
   - _Saratinwa_: non-personal knowledge, also used for surprise. It is the normal form for history,
     legends and myths.
   - _Sarpachawa_: deduced.
   - _Sarchixalla_: "of course she left, not my fault".

   Andean Spanish absorbed the system: the preterite (_fue_) marks personal knowledge and the
   pluperfect (_había ido_) marks non-personal knowledge. Hardman describes a bilingual student who
   couldn't produce a source-less Spanish sentence about a baby, because "with each effort one of
   [_seguro_, _de repente_, _a lo mejor_] would slip out." The popular version, "Aymara distrust
   written history", distorts this. What Hardman documents is that book knowledge gets a
   hearsay-type marker.

3. **Tariana: white people are "liars" because "they never tell you how they know things"**
   (Aikhenvald, "'Me', 'us' and 'others'", in _Expressing the Self_, OUP 2018:
   http://etnolinguistica.wdfiles.com/local--files/biblio:aikhenvald-2018-self/Aikhenvald_2018_First_person_Arawak_OCR.pdf).
   Tariana and Tucano speakers say this about Portuguese speakers. Tariana also has words for
   evidential misuse: the verb _-maneta_ means 'lie, get something wrong', and _medite_, 'a useless
   person', describes someone whose evidential choice is off. When news arrived by radio, a speaker
   reported "a plane has arrived" with the reported evidential. Aikhenvald describes Tariana
   evidential choice as having evolved over 30 years to handle radio, TV, phone and internet.

4. **Dreams get the "I didn't see it" evidential, unless you're a shaman** (same Aikhenvald 2018
   chapter). Ordinary Tariana dream reports use the non-visual evidential. One speaker explained: "I
   didn't really see it, in the dream, don't you know." In 2000 a powerful shaman said in the visual
   evidential that he had seen in a dream that Aikhenvald's work was good. Asked why, her consultant
   answered: "He is a real shaman, he sees everything." In Shipibo-Konibo, ordinary dreams take the
   reportative _=ronki_, while a shaman's ayahuasca vision takes the direct evidential (Valenzuela
   2003, cited there). So the grammar encodes who counts as having epistemic access. Elgin's conlang
   Láadan (1984) has a dedicated dream evidential, _we_ ("perceived in a dream"), and _waálh_:
   "assumed false because the source is distrusted and believed to have evil intent"
   (https://laadanlanguage.com/laadan-reference/evidence-morphemes/).

5. **Quechua: you can pass on a claim without vouching for it.** Faller's 2002 Stanford
   dissertation, as reproduced in Kroeger
   (https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Linguistics/Analyzing_Meaning_-_An_Introduction_to_Semantics_and_Pragmatics_(Kroeger)/17%3A_Evidentiality/17.05%3A_Two_types_of_evidentials),
   gives _Para-sha-n=si, ichaqa mana crei-ni-chu_: "It is raining (they say), but I don't believe
   it." That is fine in Cuzco Quechua. The St'át'imcets reportative cannot be used for a payment the
   speaker knows never happened. The distinction is "illocutionary" evidentials (these modify the
   speech act: "I'm presenting, not asserting") versus "propositional" ones (part of the claim
   itself). This maps onto the rationalist distinction between "I'm told X" and "I believe X".
   Quechua grammaticalizes the first without committing you to the second.

6. **Matsés: evidentiality lives only in the past tense, and it records when you got the evidence**
   (Fleck 2007, _Language_ 83:589–614, "Evidentiality and double tense in Matses"). There are
   separate experiential, inferential and conjectural suffix sets, split by recent versus distant
   past. The "double tense" places both the event and the moment you acquired the evidence. Guy
   Deutscher popularized this in the _NYT Magazine_ (2010:
   http://www.nytimes.com/2010/08/29/magazine/29language-t.html; I checked the wording via the
   reprint at
   https://hotair.com/headlines/2010/08/28/confirmed-your-native-language-affects-how-you-think-n222070).
   Ask a Matsés man how many wives he has, and unless they are in view he answers in the past tense:
   "There were two last time I checked." The reprint also says that with the wrong evidentiality, a
   statement "is considered a lie". I could not trace that line to Fleck himself (UNVERIFIED). On
   the EA Forum (2021) someone proposed Matsés as a model for certainty marking. A commenter quoting
   Fleck's 2003 thesis corrected it: evidentiality is obligatory only for past events, and
   _certainty_ marking is optional
   (https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/MYCbguxHAZkNGtG2B/matses-are-languages-providing-epistemic-certainty-of).

7. **Japanese grammar treats other minds as inferred.** You say _kanashii_ ('(I'm) sad'), but _ano
   hito wa kanashii_ ('he is sad') is not normally acceptable. You need _kanashisō da_ ('he appears
   sad') or a verb in _-garu_ ('shows signs of'). The source is Maynard's _Expressive Japanese_ via
   Language Hat (2011: https://languagehat.com/inaccessible-emotions/). Linguists call this the
   "first-person psych-predicate restriction" and analyze _-garu_ and _no_ as evidentials that lift
   it (Tenny 2006). A 2025 ACL short paper found that most 7–10B open LLMs fail this restriction
   (https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19599v1).

8. **Turkish -mIş covers hearsay, inference and surprise, and liars over-use the eyewitness form.**
   _Kemal gel-di_ means 'Kemal came (I saw)'. _Kemal gel-miş_ means 'Kemal came (apparently / I'm
   told / oh!)'. The surprise reading is DeLancey's (1997, _Linguistic Typology_ 1:33–52) founding
   example of _mirativity_, grammaticalized "huh, didn't expect that". In a CogSci 2024 study by
   Aydın et al. (https://escholarship.org/uc/item/64v7v3b8), deceptive retellings were longer and
   more complex and showed "a heightened use of direct evidential inflection (-dI)". Fakers claim
   eyewitness status. Turkish readers also judge source/evidential mismatches as lies (Arslan et al.
   2024, _Functions of Language_: https://benjamins.com/catalog/fol.22061.ars).

9. **Bulgarian journalists use the "renarrative" to signal distance, and it predicts
   misinformation.** Margova (2023–24: https://ibl.bas.bg/izvestiya/en/?p=1675) finds journalists
   use the reportative form when they doubt a claim, and that it is concentrated in articles later
   flagged by fact-checkers. Headlines, meanwhile, are drifting away from it so as to sound like
   plain truth. There is now a BERT renarrative detector with 0.98 accuracy (Temnikova, Margova et
   al. 2025: https://jclib.dcl.bas.bg/?p=960). German reported-speech mood (Konjunktiv I: _er sei
   krank_) and _sollen_ do the same job in German news; that is standard textbook material, and I
   did not fetch a source for it.

10. **Does it change cognition? Mostly no, with one interesting asymmetry.**
    - Korean 3–4-year-olds produce evidentials early but understand them fragilely. On
      non-linguistic source-monitoring tasks they match English peers: "contrary to relativistic
      expectations" (Papafragou, Li, Choi & Han 2007, _Cognition_ 103:253–299:
      https://web.archive.org/web/20221004172315/http://www.sfu.ca/~chunghye/papers/evidentiality-cognition-103.pdf).
    - Turkish 4-year-olds reportedly beat English 4-year-olds on non-linguistic source monitoring,
      but the gap is gone by age 6 (Aksu-Koç, Ögel-Balaban & Alp 2009, _New Directions for Child and
      Adolescent Development_ 125:13–28, as summarized by Tosun & Vaid).
    - Adults: Turkish speakers recognize and remember _firsthand_-marked sentences better than
      reported ones. English monolinguals show no difference, and they remember non-firsthand
      material _better than Turks do_ (Tosun, Vaid & Geraci 2013, _J. Memory & Language_
      69:121–134). So an evidential grammar may make you down-weight and forget hearsay, rather than
      make you a better source tracker.
    - Turkish and English speakers equally misremember "inferred" events as "seen", and asking for
      explicit source reports doesn't fix it (Ünal, Pinto, Bunger & Papafragou 2016, _JML_
      87:157–176: https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item_2348476).
    - Ünal & Papafragou's 2020 review (_Topics in Cognitive Science_ 12:115–135) concludes that
      evidential categories sit on shared, language-independent concepts of knowledge source.
    - Fun aside: Whorf himself cited Hopi evidential marking as a case where grammar should make
      distinctions easier (quoted in Papafragou et al. 2007).

11. **"Engagement": grammar for whether the listener already knows.** Evans, Bergqvist & San Roque
    (2018, _Language and Cognition_ 10:110–140 and 141–170:
    https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/the-grammar-of-engagement-i-framework-and-initial-exemplification/)
    describe Andoke (Colombia) and Kogi. Their clause-level markers assign "knowing" or "unknowing"
    to speaker _and_ addressee, so speakers must keep scanning the other person's knowledge state.
    That is roughly common knowledge versus private knowledge, as grammar.

12. **How common is it? It depends who's counting.** Aikhenvald (2004) says about a quarter of the
    world's languages have grammatical evidentiality. de Haan's WALS sample (2013, chapters 77–78:
    https://wals.info/chapter/77) has 181 languages with none, 166 with indirect only and 71 with
    both direct and indirect, so 237/418 ≈ 57% have _something_. The gap comes from definitions: de
    Haan counts quotative particles, Turkic/Balkan past forms and even Dutch _moeten_ 'must'.
    Affixes are the commonest coding (131), and Africa has almost none.

## 3. Read these (ranked)

1. **Aikhenvald, "'Me', 'us' and 'others'"** (2018, OUP chapter, ~40 pp).
   http://etnolinguistica.wdfiles.com/local--files/biblio:aikhenvald-2018-self/Aikhenvald_2018_First_person_Arawak_OCR.pdf.
   This is the best single source for the human texture: the liars, the shaman dreams, the radio,
   the "useless person".
2. **Everett, "Cultural Constraints on Grammar and Cognition in Pirahã"** (2005, _Current
   Anthropology_, ~26 pp including peer commentaries).
   https://cr.middlebury.edu/public/usoltan/intd0111a-s09-html/content/everett_05_piraha.pdf. Read
   it to see the original claim stated plainly, and the commentators already pushing back. It
   includes the detail that Pirahã call their language "straight head" and every other language
   "crooked head".
3. **John Colapinto, "The Interpreter"** (2007, _New Yorker_, ~12k words). Mirror:
   https://www.ic.unicamp.br/~stolfi/links/programs/c/projects/piraha/Piraha_Article_colapinto.html.
   This is the gripping narrative version: Jesus, the "gone out of experience" concept (_xibipío_,
   also used for a flickering candle), and the Chomsky feud.
4. **M.J. Hardman, "Data source and person in the Jaqi languages"** (1988–89, ~12 pp).
   https://mason.gmu.edu/~ataylor/dvd_project/Readings/Data%20source%20and%20person%20in%20Jaqi.pdf.
   A short, first-person account of what a grammar of sources does to manners, truth and courtesy.
5. **Guy Deutscher, "Does Your Language Shape How You Think?"** (2010, _NYT Magazine_, ~5k words).
   http://www.nytimes.com/2010/08/29/magazine/29language-t.html. The best careful popular take on
   Whorf: languages shape thought through what they _oblige_ you to say, not what they forbid.
6. **Papafragou, Li, Choi & Han, "Evidentiality in language and cognition"** (2007, _Cognition_, 47
   pp). URL in item 10. The clean null result. Skim the experiments, read the discussion.
7. **Kroeger, _Analyzing Meaning_, ch. 17 "Evidentiality"** (2022 ed., free, ~15 pp).
   https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Linguistics/Analyzing_Meaning_-_An_Introduction_to_Semantics_and_Pragmatics_(Kroeger)/17%3A_Evidentiality.
   Textbook-grade definitions with Tuyuca, Quechua and Cheyenne examples, plus the
   illocutionary/propositional split.
8. **Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues, "Pirahã Exceptionality: A Reassessment"** (2009, _Language_
   85:355–404, ~50 pp; preprint https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/000411), paired with **Everett's
   reply** (2009, _Language_ 85:405–442:
   https://daneverettbooks.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/everett_09_Pirah-Culture-.pdf). Read both
   sides before believing either.
9. **Tosun, Vaid & Geraci** (2013, _JML_). The one adult study with a real effect, and it points in
   the direction of discounting hearsay.
10. **Lojban CLL §13.11 "Evidentials"** (1997, ~3 pp).
    https://lojban.org/publications/cll/cll_v1.1_xhtml-section-chunks/section-evidentials.html. A

### Rationalist angle

Is "I saw a study / I'm told / my inside view is / epistemic status:" a home-grown evidential
system? By Aikhenvald's definition, no. That definition requires grammatical, near-obligatory
marking, and lexical hedges are "evidential strategies". The deeper mismatch is that most
rationalist markers encode **credence** ("70%", "low confidence"), while evidentials encode
**source**. Aikhenvald insists that evidentiality "is not a kind of modality". The Quechua _-si_
example shows the two are independent: you can mark hearsay and openly disbelieve it. A useful
upgrade would be to mark source and credence separately: "ti'e, 30%". On LessWrong, Lojban
evidentials come up in "Should we use qualifiers in speech?" (2020:
https://lesswrong.com/posts/rEKhZQNPpw2NgcdKu/should-we-use-qualifiers-in-speech) and in an earlier
thread arguing that people would be too lazy to use mandatory evidentials
(https://greaterwrong.com/posts/vqbAPjjvWbmEszo6D/linguistic-mechanisms-for-less-wrong-cognition/comment/KuMBMqCDX9LjqvKBo).
The Turkish deception result is a warning: once source marking is a norm, faking "I saw it" becomes
the attack. Ithkuil's validation categories and conlangs in general belong to another researcher's
slice.

## Dead ends / contested

- **Recursion in Pirahã: unresolved.** Everett's own 1983/1986 grammars analyzed embedded clauses,
  and he later abandoned those analyses (Everett 2009 says so explicitly). Futrell et al. (2016,
  _PLoS ONE_, ~1,100-sentence corpus:
  https://news.mit.edu/2016/data-amazonian-piraha-language-debate-0309) found "no unambiguous
  evidence" of embedding, consistent with a regular language but not conclusive. Sauerland's
  false-speech-report experiments (2010; chapter 2018) claim to show embedding; Oliveira et al.
  report null statistics on his data. Gibson's 2024 Festschrift chapter calls the question hard to
  decide and recursion "an irrelevant side-track".
- **IEP.** Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues (2009) say there is "no evidence" for the causal
  culture-to-grammar link. Everett's 2009 restatement explicitly folds in "overheard, deduced". That
  weakens the claim and arguably makes it unfalsifiable. Everett himself warns against the
  circularity of "the language has evidentials because the culture values empirical reasoning, and
  we know the culture values it because it has evidentials".
- **Numbers: the most solid Pirahã finding.** There are no exact number words. _hói/hoí_ behave as
  relative "few/more": in counting-down trials _hói_ was used for up to 6. Pirahã speakers do
  one-to-one matching fine but fail when the task needs memory (Frank, Everett, Fedorenko & Gibson
  2008, _Cognition_ 108:819–824: https://news.mit.edu/2008/language-0624). Gordon (2004, _Science_
  306:496–499) claimed a stronger deficit; I did not open it.
- **Colour terms.** Everett's own early glossary listed colour words. By 2006 he said they were
  descriptive phrases ("like blood"). Disputed.
- **Is Pirahã evidential marking obligatory?** Wikipedia says "they cannot be ambiguous", with no
  specific source: UNVERIFIED. Berlin's 2005 _CA_ commentary lists six suffixes from Everett 1986:
  _-híai_ hearsay, _-xáagauá_ observed, _-sibiga_ deduced, plus three certainty grades, _-áti_
  uncertain, _-haí_ relatively certain, _-há_ certain. The observed suffix is spelled _-xáagahá_
  elsewhere.
- **"Matsés speakers consider the wrong evidential a lie."** This traces only to a popular article:
  UNVERIFIED in Fleck. The verified version of the claim comes from Aymara (Hardman) and Tariana
  (Aikhenvald).
- **Mirativity as a separate category.** DeLancey (2012) conceded that his Tibetan analysis was
  wrong. Hill (2012, _Linguistic Typology_; title, not opened: "'Mirativity' does not exist") argues
  that the "miratives" are just evidentials.
- **Tom Wolfe, _The Kingdom of Speech_ (2016).** He sides with Everett. Reviews were savage (the
  _Guardian_ review was headlined "a bonfire of facts reeking of vanity"), and Chomsky said its
  errors "would take an essay" to review. It's entertaining, but don't use it as evidence.
- **UNVERIFIED / not opened:** the Wintu (Lee 1938/1944) details; the specific Aymara time-metaphor
  result (Núñez & Sweetser 2006, outside this slice); the exact wording of the Jesus exchange in
  _Don't Sleep, There Are Snakes_ (2008). Secondary summaries agree the Pirahã lost interest once
  Everett said he had never seen Jesus.
