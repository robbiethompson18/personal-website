# 07: Do environment, body and society shape which sounds a language has?

## 1. What he was half-remembering

Nobody has published a "cold climate → guttural sounds" claim. What does exist is a 30-year line of
work saying that **warm-climate languages are more sonorous** (more vowels and sonorant consonants,
more simple CV syllables) and **cold-climate languages lean on obstruents and consonant clusters**.
It starts with Munroe et al. (1996) and Fought et al. (2004), and runs through Maddieson & Coupé
(2015), Maddieson (2018) and Wang, Wichmann et al. (2023, PNAS Nexus, 5,293 languages). A layperson
could easily hear "consonant-heavy" as "harsh/guttural." The correlation holds **across** families
and macroareas but **cannot be detected within families** (Wang et al. 2023), so climate as the
cause is contested.

"Guttural" in the strict sense means uvulars (q, χ, ʁ), pharyngeals (ħ, ʕ) and glottals. I tabulated
PHOIBLE myself and it does show a descriptive kernel of his memory: uvular obstruents occur in **~4%
of languages within 15° of the equator but ~35-38% poleward of 30°**. That pattern is almost
entirely areal: the Caucasus, Semitic, Turkic/Siberia and the Pacific Northwest. Australia has zero
uvulars at any latitude. Urban & Moran (2021) show that uvulars spread by **contact**, not by
environment. The French/German/Danish "guttural r" is the classic case: a prestige fashion in Paris
and probably some German cities in the 17th century, not a climate effect.

## 3. Most interesting findings

1. **The sonority–temperature correlation is real across the globe and invisible inside families.**
   Wang et al. (2023) used ASJP basic vocabularies for 5,293 languages. Sonority rises with
   temperature "across macroareas and language families", but the relationship "cannot be discerned
   within language families". They argue that cold weakens sonority rather than heat strengthening
   it. Their own exceptions are Central America and mainland Southeast Asia: hot regions with low
   mean sonority. For a quant this is the whole story: a between-group effect with no within-group
   effect. That fits climate acting slowly at the level of family homelands, and it fits plain
   confounding equally well. [PNAS Nexus 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgad384) ·
   [press release](https://idw-online.de/en/news825432)

2. **Maddieson measured actual speech, not inventories.** He took ~100 languages from Global
   Recordings Network clips (~66 s each, mostly religious retellings) and measured the share of
   speaking time that is sonorant: 41% to 90%, mean 65.5%. In a stepwise regression temperature was
   the only variable kept (R²=0.24). Tree cover and rainfall, the variables in the birdsong-derived
   "acoustic adaptation" story, dropped out. The proposed mechanism is that warm, turbulent air
   degrades high frequencies (i.e. fricative noise). It is argued, not tested.
   [Maddieson, Frontiers in Communication 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2018.00028/full)

3. **The Embers' paper is as wild as you remember.** Ember & Ember (1999, _American Anthropologist_)
   found that how much a society carries its babies predicts its CV-syllable score, even after
   controlling for climate and literacy. Their 2007 follow-up found that more frequent premarital
   and extramarital sex predicts more sonority (p<.001), alongside a cold-months effect (coefficient
   −.49). Their headline was that climate's effect is concentrated in _cold_ climates, which
   contradicted Munroe's warm-climate-outdoors story. Both papers used ~60 societies with no
   phylogenetic control. Treat them as a cautionary exhibit.
   [HRAF summary, 2007 paper](https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/documents/203) ·
   [HRAF summary, 1999 paper](https://hraf.yale.edu/documents/206). UNVERIFIED: the Embers'
   mechanism for baby-holding. Their 2000 reply is titled "regular rhythm or sonority?", which
   suggests a rhythm-entrainment argument, but I didn't read the full text.

4. **Ejectives and altitude: the clusters are a contact story.** Everett (2013) used 567 languages,
   92 of them with ejectives. 87% of the ejective languages lie within 500 km of land above 1,500 m,
   against 43% of the others. The six highland regions are the North American cordillera, the Andes,
   the southern African plateau, the East African rift and Ethiopia, the Caucasus, and the Tibetan
   plateau, which has _no_ ejectives. His mechanism: the model needs ~26% less force to compress the
   pharyngeal air at 2,500 m. Urban & Moran (2021) refit this with a Bayesian logistic mixed model
   on PHOIBLE (2,031 languages, random effects for family and macroarea). Ejectives get a mild
   effect, 1.57 log-odds per tenfold altitude with a 95% CI of 0.15–2.93. Uvulars get nothing. They
   trace the clusters to contact: Ossetic got ejectives from Nakh-Daghestanian, Quechua from Aymara.
   [Everett, PLoS ONE 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0065275)
   ·
   [Urban & Moran, PLoS ONE 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245522)

5. **Tone and humidity: a co-author couldn't replicate it.** Everett, Blasi & Roberts (2015, PNAS)
   found that of 3,700+ languages, the 629 with complex tone sit almost entirely in humid regions.
   Their mechanism is that dry air increases vocal-fold jitter and shimmer. The first issue of the
   _Journal of Language Evolution_ (2016) was a debate on the claim. Hammarström called the
   trade-off hypothesis "empirically false". Collins showed that clustered languages plus contagious
   tone reproduce the pattern in simulation. Then Roberts (2018), one of the original authors,
   re-ran it on alternative data and with a continuous tone measure and found it **not robust**. The
   related humidity → fewer vowels effect (Everett 2017) did survive, but small.
   [PNAS 2015](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1417413112) ·
   [Collins's blog summary of the JoLE debate, 2016](https://humans-who-read-grammars.blogspot.com/2016/02/climate-does-not-affect-way-you-speak.html)
   ·
   [Roberts, Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00166/full)

6. **f and v may be a Neolithic invention.** Blasi et al. (2019, _Science_) revived Hockett's 1985
   idea. Pre-agricultural adults wore their teeth down to an edge-to-edge bite. Soft, processed food
   preserved the juvenile overbite and overjet. Their biomechanical model says labiodentals then
   cost ~30% less muscular effort, and the overbite brings lip and teeth close enough that bilabials
   can slip into labiodentals by accident. In press coverage, hunter-gatherer languages have ~27% of
   the labiodental rate of food producers, and European data show labiodentals rising sharply only
   in the last couple of millennia. Tarasov & Uyeda (2020, bioRxiv) reanalysed the data and argue
   the hunter-gatherer gap is confounded by labiodentals declining with distance from Africa. I
   found no peer-reviewed version of that critique.
   [Science 2019](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aav3218) ·
   [UZH press release](https://news.uzh.ch/en/articles/2019/Sounds.html) ·
   [Tarasov & Uyeda 2020](https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.02.20.957407)

7. **Genes and tone: one leg partially replicated.** Dediu & Ladd (2007) correlated population
   frequencies of derived ASPM and Microcephalin haplogroups with non-tonal languages across 49
   populations (983 alleles, 26 linguistic features). Their mechanism was a weak acquisition bias
   amplified by cultural transmission. Wong et al. (2020, _Science Advances_) tested 400+ Cantonese
   speakers. Carriers of the ASPM TT genotype (~70%) perceived tone better, MCPH1 had no effect, and
   within-genotype variance was large. [PNAS 2007](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0610848104)
   · [Wong et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7253162) ·
   [Ladd on Language Log 2020](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=49741)

8. **Spurious correlations, made to order.** Roberts & Winters (2013) built a deliberately absurd
   causal chain:
   - Linguistic diversity correlates with road deaths across 117 countries: r=0.45 after controlling
     for GDP, population, latitude and more.
   - Presence of the tree _Acacia nilotica_ correlates with tonal languages (617 languages,
     χ²=47.1), and the link survives a latitude control.
   - Acacia also predicts traffic deaths.
   - Siesta-taking cultures have simpler verb morphology (p=0.001) until a phylogeny is added
     (r=0.017, p=0.9).

   Their point: big, noisy cross-cultural datasets _raise_ false-positive rates because of Galton's
   problem.
   [PLoS ONE 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0070902)

9. **Chen's future-tense savings result shrinks under relatedness controls.** Chen (2013, AER) found
   that speakers of weak-future-marking languages save more (~6% of GDP more at the OECD country
   level). In Roberts, Winters & Chen (2015) the correlation "is weaker when controlling for
   relatedness". Under the strictest mixed model with random slopes it is **not significant**,
   although it "remained reasonably robust under a number of tests". Roberts's blog headline was
   blunter: "no correlation when controlling for cultural evolution." Chen co-authoring his own
   stress test is rare and worth noting. [AER 2013](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.103.2.690) ·
   [PLoS ONE 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0132145)

10. **Languages trade speed for density and land near 39 bits/s.** Coupé, Oh, Dediu & Pellegrino
    (2019) recorded 170 speakers of 17 languages. Information density ranged from ~5 to ~8 bits per
    syllable, and Japanese and Spanish speakers produce ~50% more syllables per second than
    Vietnamese and Thai speakers. The product converges on ~39 bits/s. Small phoneme inventories and
    simple syllables get paid for in speed.
    [Science Advances 2019](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaw2594)

11. **Sound symbolism is real at world scale.** Blasi et al. (2016, PNAS) used ASJP lists for about
    two-thirds of the world's languages and found 74 non-arbitrary sound–meaning associations that
    recur independently of descent and borrowing. Examples: "nose" with /n/, "tongue" with /l/,
    "small" with /i/, "full" with /p b/. Ćwiek et al. (2022) ran bouba/kiki on 917 speakers of 25
    languages and found >70% matching, with little dependence on writing system. Mandarin, Turkish,
    Romanian and Albanian speakers were the exceptions.
    [PNAS 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605782113) ·
    [Phil Trans B 2022](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2020.0390)

12. **Guttural r is a fashion, not the weather.** The classic account (Trautmann 1880; Chambers &
    Trudgill 1980) has uvular r spreading from 17th-century Parisian high society city-to-city into
    German, Danish, Dutch and Scandinavian standards. Gorrie (2021) adds evidence that it also arose
    independently in parts of Germany (Yiddish has it, and Yiddish speakers had left before the
    Paris fashion) and in Northumberland (the "burr", 1600s). Urban & Moran (2021) also cite
    Parisian spread to explain European uvular rhotics.
    [Gorrie 2021](https://colingorrie.pages.dev/articles/guttural-r)

## 5. Details

### Baseline facts: my own PHOIBLE tabulation

I computed these from PHOIBLE 2.0 (Moran & McCloy 2019; 3,020 inventories), keeping one inventory
per Glottocode (N=2,176). Data: https://github.com/phoible/dev

**Most common segments.** The ten most common segments are:

| Segment            | /m/   | /i/   | /k/   | /j/   | /u/   | /a/   | /p/   | /w/   | /n/   | /t/   |
| ------------------ | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Share of languages | 96.6% | 93.6% | 90.5% | 90.1% | 89.3% | 88.8% | 85.3% | 84.6% | 81.8% | 72.1% |

/t/ looks low because PHOIBLE splits dental /t̪/ from alveolar /t/. Next come l 70%, s 68%, ŋ 64%, e
63% and b 62%.

**Rare sounds:**

- English "th": /θ/ 3.6%, /ð/ 5.2%, either one 6.9%.
- English /ɹ/: 1.7%.
- Any click: 0.9%.
- Bilabial trill /ʙ/: 0.1%.

**"Guttural" and related classes:**

- Any uvular: 12.0%
- Any pharyngeal: 3.0%
- Any ejective: 8.2%
- Any labiodental: 46.6%. Blasi et al. say "nearly half".

**Gaps (Ohala's aerodynamics).**

- **Missing /ɡ/.** 38.8% of languages have /k/ but no /ɡ/; only 4.7% have /ɡ/ without /k/. Voicing
  needs the cavity behind the closure to expand, and a velar closure leaves the smallest cavity
  (Ohala 1983, in MacNeilage ed., _The Production of Speech_:
  https://www.doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4613-8202-7_9).
- **Missing /p/.** 10.1% have /b/ without /p/, the Arabic pattern.

**Voiced fricative implies voiceless?** Mostly, but the rule is leaky:

| Pair  | Voiced without voiceless | Voiceless without voiced |
| ----- | ------------------------ | ------------------------ |
| v / f | 58                       | 412                      |
| z / s | 13                       | 839                      |
| ɣ / x | 175                      | 246                      |
| ð / θ | 72                       | 38 (reversed)            |

The ɣ/x and ð/θ exceptions are probably lenited stops (Spanish-type [ð ɣ]) coded as phonemes.

**Inventory sizes:**

- Smallest: Pirahã and Rotokas with 11 segments each, Hawaiian 13 (UPSID).
- Largest: !Xóõ 161 segments in one source, !Xu 141, Kildin Saami 128.
- WALS (Maddieson 2013): mean 22.7 consonants, median 21. Rotokas has 6 consonants, !Xóõ 122.
- Vowels: 5 is the modal count (188 of 564 languages, 33%), and 51% have 5–6 vowel qualities.
  PHOIBLE's mean of ~10 vowels counts long and nasal vowels separately.

**No phonemic nasals:** 47 inventories, mostly Amazonian and West African (Rotokas, Pirahã, Quileute
and others), often with nasals present as allophones.

### Explaining the common sounds

- **Dispersion.** Liljencrants & Lindblom (1972, _Language_ 48) predicted vowel systems by
  maximising acoustic distance, which gives /i a u/ first and then five vowels.
- **Self-organisation.** De Boer (2000, _Journal of Phonetics_ 28:441–465) got the same shapes to
  emerge from imitating agents without anyone optimising globally.
- **Phonetic stability.** Stevens's quantal theory (1972/1989) says some articulations sit on
  plateaus where acoustics are insensitive to articulatory error. UNVERIFIED here; cited from
  general knowledge.
- **Mama/papa.** Jakobson (1960, "Why 'mama' and 'papa'?") tied nasal "mama" to the infant's nursing
  hum. Murdock (1959) gave the cross-linguistic kin-term data.

### Society → structure

- **Nettle (1995, _Linguistics_ 33:359–367).** Bigger inventories go with shorter words, from 10
  languages. Moran & Blasi (2014) found the same direction with more data. Wichmann & Holman (2023)
  couldn't confirm significance.
- **Trudgill (2011), _Sociolinguistic Typology_.** Small, isolated, dense-network communities keep
  or grow complexity. The book is mostly about morphology, and a 2004 Trudgill reply predicts adult
  contact gives _medium_ inventories.
  https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/language/v089/89.2.tagliamonte.html
- **Hay & Bauer (2007, _Language_ 83:388–400)** claimed bigger populations have bigger inventories.
  Moran, McCloy & Wright (2012, _Language_ 88:877–893) used PHOIBLE with a hierarchical model and
  found the effect "fluctuates around zero" once genealogy is controlled.
  https://doi.org/10.1353/lan.2012.0087
- **Linguistic niche hypothesis.** Lupyan & Dale (2010, PLoS ONE 5:e8559) link many adult learners
  to less inflection. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0008559 Koplenig (2019, RSOS) found no
  L2-proportion effect (https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.181274). Kauhanen, Einhaus & Walkden (2023,
  JoLE) found one, and Koplenig (2023, arXiv 2305.00217) replied. It hinges on how "L2 proportion"
  is imputed.

### Other environment links

- **Whistled languages.** ~80 cultures whistle their language, mostly in mountains or dense forest
  where whistles carry farther than shouting. All are endangered. Meyer:
  https://knowablemagazine.org/article/mind/2021/whistled-languages (2021)
- **Clicks outside Africa.** The only case is Damin, a Lardil and Yangkaal initiation register in
  Australia with ~150 roots, last used in ceremony in the 1950s. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damin
- **Anatomy.** Moisik & Dediu (2017, JoLE 2:37–51) built a biomechanical model in which a smaller
  alveolar ridge (reported as common in Khoisan palates) eases clicks. They describe the bias as
  weak and individually overridable.
  https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2444826_3/component/file_2444824/Moisik_Dediu_2017.pdf Dediu,
  Janssen & Moisik (2019, _Nature Human Behaviour_ 3:1107–1115) used 107 MRI palates in agent
  simulations of vowel transmission. The general review is Dediu, Janssen & Moisik (2017, _Language
  & Communication_), "Language is not isolated from its wider environment"; I found it cited but did
  not open it.

## 6. Dead ends / contested

- **Atkinson (2011, Science 332:346), phonemic diversity from Africa.** It used 504 languages, with
  inventories largest in Africa and smallest in South America and Oceania, read as a serial founder
  effect. Cysouw, Dediu & Moran (2012, _Science_ comment) showed that the inferred origin moves to
  East Africa, the Caucasus or elsewhere depending on which features are counted, and that tone and
  vowels were over-weighted. Hunley et al. (2012, Proc R Soc B) found the fit is best for a
  _Eurasian_ origin. Nichols noted that the Americas and Pacific are morphologically _more_ complex,
  the opposite of what founder loss predicts. Linguistic Typology 15(2), 2011, ran a commentary set
  (Maddieson et al.; Jaeger et al.; Ross & Donohue and others). **Mostly dead.**
  https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_1094595_12/component/file_1357545/Cysouw_et_al_2012_Comment_Science.pdf
- **Corrections to the brief:**
  - Everett (2013) used 567 languages, not ~600.
  - The Ember baby-holding paper is 1999 (_American Anthropologist_ 101:730–742). HRAF lists it
    as 2000.
  - Everett, Blasi & Roberts appeared in PNAS in 2015. The JoLE debate is 2016.
