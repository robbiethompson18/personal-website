# 05 — Hedging, qualifiers, and confidence marking (English-style)

Scope: the linguistics and psychology of "approximately", "I propose", "I think", "~70%".
Grammatical evidentiality in other languages is another researcher's slice. Where I opened a source
myself I say so. Anything I could not confirm is marked UNVERIFIED.

## 1. The serious words

**Quick classification of Robbie's examples:** "approximately" is an approximator (a rounder; for
Channell, a vague additive). "I propose" is an explicit performative that downgrades an assertion to
a proposal (a hedge on illocutionary force). "I think" and "my guess is" are plausibility shields,
i.e. epistemic stance markers or quality hedges. "Roughly 70%" is a numeric credence with a rounder
attached. An "Epistemic status" header is document-level metadiscourse.

## 2. Most interesting findings

1. **The original "serious possibility" story is better than the usual retelling.** I read Kent's
   essay (CIA, _Studies in Intelligence_, 1964;
   [PDF](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Words-of-Estimative-Probability.pdf)). NIE 29-51
   (March 1951) called a Soviet attack on Yugoslavia "a serious possibility". Kent personally meant
   about 65:35 _in favor_ of an attack. The State Department's Policy Planning Staff read it as
   "very considerably lower". **Correction to the brief:** the 20%–80% spread was among the _members
   of the Board of National Estimates who had agreed on the wording_, not among the readers ("the
   low man was thinking of about 20 to 80, the high of 80 to 20"). Kent was "shaken… more by the
   realization that Board members… had failed to communicate with each other." His proposed scale
   was: almost certain 93% ±6, probable 75% ±12, chances about even 50% ±10, probably not 30% ±10,
   almost certainly not 7% ±5. He called his opponents "poets", as opposed to "mathematicians". He
   also has a section titled "The Lurking Weasel" that attacks "apparently", "seemingly", "suggests"
   and unmodified "reportedly". These words, he writes, make the writer "everybody or nobody at
   all", and they are used to "say something without saying it, in short fudge it."

2. **Heuer's 51% anecdote.** I read Heuer, _Psychology of Intelligence Analysis_, CIA 1999, pp.
   153–155
   ([PDF](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Pyschology-of-Intelligence-Analysis.pdf)). Heuer
   argued for months with a colleague over whether a source was genuine ("probably bona fide") or
   controlled by a hostile service ("probably under hostile control"). When they put numbers on it,
   each said "at least 51%" for his side, and the disagreement ended: "the problem was not a major
   difference of opinion, but the ambiguity of the term probable." The same chapter has a dot plot
   from 23 NATO officers assigning percentages to probability phrases. Only "better than even" got
   consensus. The famous Reddit chart (zonination, 2015, ~46 respondents;
   [GitHub](https://github.com/zonination/perceptions)) replicates this plot.

3. **How wide the spread is, in numbers.** Mauboussin & Mauboussin surveyed ~1,700 people (HBR,
   2018;
   [HBR](https://hbr.org/2018/07/if-you-say-something-is-likely-how-likely-do-people-think-it-is);
   figures via [J. Collins summary](https://corporate.jcx.au/words-of-estimative-probability)). The
   middle 90% of answers for "likely" ran from 55% to 90%. For "real possibility" they ran from 20%
   to 80%, so the phrase is nearly meaningless. Even "always" spanned 90–100%. Women read phrases as
   higher probability than men did. Mosteller & Youtz (_Statistical Science_, 1990;
   [Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ss/1177012242)) pooled 20 studies covering 52
   expressions. They found the _averages_ are fairly stable: "always" ≈98%, "certain" ≈95%,
   "never"/"impossible" ≈1%, and "probable", "likely", "frequent" and "often" give roughly the same
   means. The problem is the spread between individuals, not the population mean. Wallsten, Budescu
   et al. (_JEP:General_ 115:348–365, 1986;
   [PDF via gwern](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1986-wallsten.pdf)) model each
   phrase as a fuzzy _membership function_ over [0,1]. Each person's function is stable over time
   but differs a lot from other people's (Rapoport, Wallsten & Cox 1987).

4. **Medicine: "common" is off by about 5x, "rare" by about 100x.** EU regulators define "common"
   side effects as 1–10% and "rare" as 0.01–0.1%. Knapp, Raynor & Berry (_Qual Saf Health Care_,
   2004; n=120 statin patients; [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1743828)) found
   patients told constipation was "common" estimated 34.2%. Patients told the number (2.5%)
   estimated 8.1%. A systematic review of 33 studies (Andreadis et al., _J Gen Intern Med_, 2021;
   [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8642516)) found the mean lay reading of "rare"
   ≈10% (more than 100x the EC meaning) and of "common" ≈59%. In the two studies that tested the EC
   labels directly, 70% of people misread them. In 10 of 12 samples, most people _preferred_ numbers
   or numbers plus words.

5. **IPCC "very likely" gets pulled toward 50%.** Budescu, Broomell & Por (_Psychological Science_,
   2009; n=223) found that readers took IPCC "very likely" (defined as >90%) to mean far less. About
   three quarters put it below 90%, and the mean was reportedly ~62% (secondary sources:
   [Mother Jones](https://www.motherjones.com/environment/2014/04/ipcc-global-warming-doubt-psychology/),
   [ShapingTomorrowsWorld](https://www.shapingtomorrowsworld.org/uncertainty_in_climate_change_1.html);
   exact mean UNVERIFIED against the paper). Budescu, Por, Broomell & Smithson (_Nature Climate
   Change_, 2014; 25 samples, 24 countries, 17 languages;
   [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcli/v4y2014i6d10.1038_nclimate2194.html)) replicated the
   pull toward 50%. Adding the numeric range next to the word reduced it. Wintle et al. (_PLOS ONE_,
   2019; n=924; [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6469752/)) tested four formats.
   Only putting the numbers _in the text_ helped clearly: 66% of readers matched the ICD 203 meaning
   vs 32% in the control.

6. **Numbers carry real information, even in geopolitics.** I read the draft of Friedman, Baker,
   Mellers, Tetlock & Zeckhauser, "The value of precision in probability assessment" (_ISQ_
   62:410–422, 2018;
   [PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.dartmouth.edu/dist/0/433/files/2017/06/Value-of-Precision-June-17.pdf)).
   The data are 888,328 Good Judgment Project forecasts on 380 questions (2011–15). They took the
   numeric forecasts and rounded them into coarser bins after the fact. Rounding to 2 bins worsened
   Brier scores by 31% on average (median 22%). For superforecasters the penalty was over 500%.
   Rounding to the US intelligence community's 7 words of estimative probability lost accuracy "for
   every subgroup", and the losses were largest for the best forecasters. Per question, the point
   where extra bins stop helping averaged 6.1 bins and exceeded 7 for 42% of questions. **Caveat:**
   GJP never randomized whether people answered in words or numbers. The study shows that numeric
   forecasts contain information that verbal bins would throw away. It does not show that people
   _forced_ to speak in words reason worse.

7. **The professional failure mode can be too much hedging.** Mandel & Barnes (_PNAS_, 2014;
   [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4136586/)) scored 1,514 numeric forecasts by Canadian
   intelligence analysts. Discrimination and calibration were good, and most of the miscalibration
   was **underconfidence**. The underconfidence was worse when a forecast mattered more to
   policymakers. Critics note the questions were easy: over half of Brier scores were 0 or 0.01.

8. **Do hedges cost you socially? It depends on which kind.** Gaertig & Simmons (_Psychological
   Science_ 29:504–520, 2018; 11 studies, N=4,806;
   [Booth summary](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/why-uncertain-advice-might-be-better-overconfidence))
   separate the _advisor's_ confidence ("I'm not sure, but…") from _uncertainty in the advice_ ("57%
   chance", "between 197 and 217"). People penalized unconfident advisors. They did **not** penalize
   uncertain advice: they rated it the same or better, and in head-to-head choices most picked the
   advisor whose advice included uncertainty. Their conclusion: "Advisors benefit from expressing
   themselves with confidence, but not from communicating false certainty." For rationalist speech,
   a confidently stated credence ("70%") is fine, while "I think maybe, idk" is what gets penalized.

9. **Calibration beats confidence, once people can check.** Price & Stone (_J. Behav. Decis.
   Making_, 2004) showed the _confidence heuristic_: students judged more confident stock advisors
   as more knowledgeable. Tenney, MacCoun, Spellman & Hastie, "Calibration trumps confidence as a
   basis for witness credibility" (_Psychological Science_ 18:46–50, **2007**;
   [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17362377/)) found that errors hurt _confident_ witnesses
   more than unconfident ones. Tenney, Spellman & MacCoun (_JESP_, 2008) extended this: credibility
   tracks calibration, not confidence or accuracy alone. So hedging pays off over repeated play, and
   confident bluster pays off in one-shot encounters.

10. **Trust: numeric uncertainty is nearly free, verbal uncertainty costs a little.** van der Bles,
    van der Linden, Freeman & Spiegelhalter (_PNAS_, 2020; 5 experiments, N=5,780, including a BBC
    News field experiment;
    [summary](https://blog.aiimpacts.org/p/paper-summary-the-effects-of-communicating)). A numeric
    range ("between X and Y") slightly lowered trust in the number and left trust in the source
    unchanged. A verbal note ("there is some uncertainty") lowered both a bit more and _raised
    perceived uncertainty more_. In the BBC field experiment no trust effect was significant.
    Separately, Jensen (_Human Communication Research_, 2008; N=910) found that cancer news stories
    with limitations attributed to the scientists themselves made both scientists and journalists
    seem _more_ trustworthy. A 2022 attempt did not replicate this cleanly.

11. **"Powerless speech" is about power, not gender, and hedges do several jobs.** Erickson, Lind,
    Johnson & O'Barr (_JESP_ 14:266–279, 1978) had subjects hear identical courtroom testimony in
    two styles. The "powerless" style (hedges, hesitations, intensifiers, rising intonation) was
    less persuasive and less credible. O'Barr & Atkins (1980) found these features tracked low
    social status, not sex, which rebuts Robin Lakoff's _Language and Woman's Place_ (1975), a book
    built on introspection. Janet Holmes (_Language in Society_, 1986; book 1995) showed that hedges
    like _you know_ often carry warmth toward the listener rather than doubt. Britt, Durik et al.
    (2008) found that a hedge on a _data_ statement hurts persuasion more than one on an
    _interpretation_. Practical upshot: hedge interpretations, not observations.

12. **Science hedges less and hypes more.** Yao, Wei & Wang (_Scientometrics_ 128:4541–4558, 2023;
    [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/scient/v128y2023i8d10.1007_s11192-023-04759-6.html))
    counted ~50 hedge terms in >2,600 _Science_ research articles. Hedges fell from 115.8 to 67.4
    per 10k words between 1997 and 2021, about −40%. _Science_'s editor objected that the format
    sampled used to be reserved for landmark papers, and that editors tone down overclaiming.
    Vinkers, Tijdink & Otte (_BMJ_ 351:h6467, 2015) found positive words in PubMed abstracts rose
    from 2.0% (1974–80) to 17.5% (2014), and specific words like "robust", "novel" and
    "unprecedented" rose by up to 15,000%. **Correction to the brief:** I found no source for a
    "2500%" figure. Across cultures, Hu & Cao (_J. Pragmatics_, 2011; 649 abstracts) found
    English-medium applied-linguistics abstracts hedge more than Chinese-medium ones.

13. **"It _must_ be raining" sounds weaker than "It's raining", but it isn't weaker in meaning.**
    This is Karttunen's puzzle. von Fintel & Gillies, "Must… stay… strong!" (_Natural Language
    Semantics_ 18:351–383, 2010; [MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/65909)) argue that
    epistemic _must_ is logically strong but carries an **evidential** presupposition: the
    conclusion was _inferred_, not observed. That inference is why it _sounds_ hedged. English
    epistemic modals are half evidentials (a pointer to the evidentiality slice).

14. **Same probability, different decision: directionality.** Teigen & Brun's line of work shows
    that "some possibility" points attention at the event happening and "quite uncertain" points at
    it not happening, even when people assign them the same number. Patients are more willing to
    take a drug whose side effects are "doubtful" than one with "some possibility" of side effects
    (summarized in
    [UCL eprint](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10090526/1/Directionality_WithSM.pdf)). Two
    more quirks. Senders like to _give_ words and receivers like to _get_ numbers: the
    "communication-mode preference paradox" (Erev & Cohen, _OBHDP_, 1990). And people read more
    precise numbers as more confident: an answer with more digits gets higher confidence ratings
    (Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo & Oppenheimer, _Psychological Science_, 2014). The study compared digit
    counts, not "73%" vs "70%" specifically, so applying it to rationalist round numbers is my
    extrapolation.

15. **LLMs (short).** Zhou, Jurafsky & Hashimoto (EMNLP 2023;
    [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.335)) injected 50 epistemic markers into QA
    prompts. Accuracy swung by more than 80% depending on the marker, and high-certainty markers
    lowered accuracy by ~7% relative to low-certainty ones. Zhou, Hwang, Ren & Sap, "Relying on the
    unreliable" (ACL 2024; [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.198)): base and SFT models
    prefer weakening phrases, while RLHF models flip to strengthening ones. Among answers the models
    gave confidently, 47% were wrong on average. Human preference data is biased against uncertain
    text, so RLHF trains away hedging. Lin, Hilton & Evans (TMLR, 2022;
    [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14334)) showed GPT-3 can be fine-tuned to state calibrated
    "verbalized probabilities". Belém et al. (EMNLP 2024;
    [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.483/)) found 7 of 10 LLMs map phrases like
    "likely" to numbers the way humans do, but the mapping shifts with whether the model _itself_
    thinks the statement is true, which humans do much less.

## 4. Details

**Current official lexicons.**

- **US ODNI ICD 203** (2015). These ranges come from reproductions; the official PDF's table did not
  extract cleanly ([PDF](https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf)): almost no chance /
  remote 1–5%, very unlikely 5–20%, unlikely 20–45%, roughly even chance 45–55%, likely 55–80%, very
  likely 80–95%, almost certain 95–99%.
- **UK PHIA Probability Yardstick** (gov.uk page dated 24 March 2025;
  [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/explaining-uncertainty-in-uk-intelligence-assessment/explaining-uncertainty-in-uk-intelligence-assessment)),
  which I opened: remote chance >0–≈5%, highly unlikely ≈10–20%, unlikely ≈25–35%, **realistic
  possibility ≈40–<50%**, likely/probable ≈55–75%, highly likely ≈80–90%, almost certain ≈95–<100%.
  The gaps between bands are deliberate. Defence Intelligence says the scale is "not continuous to
  avoid a false impression of accuracy". **Correction:** "realistic possibility" is not 25–35%.
- **IPCC:** very likely ≥90%, likely ≥66%.

**Lineage of "Epistemic status".** The best-documented origin is **muflax**, who put an "epistemic
state" tag on each page of his site saying whether he still believed it and how strongly. gwern
mirrors the page, which shows "last modified 2011-09-03"
([mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/www/web.archive.org/4c513620b41d3bd92a6ee8a220016d03d5debba7.html)).
**gwern** formalized it as "confidence tags" using the _Kesselman_ (2008) list of estimative words:
certain, highly likely, likely, possible, unlikely, highly unlikely, remote, impossible, plus
muflax's "log", "emotional" and "fiction" ([gwern.net/about](https://gwern.net/about)). **Scott
Alexander** turned it into free-text "Epistemic status:" lines on SSC. The lineage "Scott took the
idea from gwern, who, in turn, took the idea from muflax" comes from quanticle in Raemon's "[Feature
Idea] Epistemic Status" (LessWrong, Aug 2018;
[LW](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Hrm59GdN2yDPWbtrd/feature-idea-epistemic-status)). That thread
also has the main criticism: free-text statuses become "a bonus field for the author to signal how
witty and subversive they are." I found no evidence for "Devin Helton" as an originator, and I could
not date Scott's first use (UNVERIFIED; probably 2014).

Other rationalist pieces:

- **Elo, "Hedging"** (LW, Aug 2016;
  [GW](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/Yd8nc9zdaZ9Jxfqtd/hedging)). Distinguishes _confidence_
  hedges ("I think men are evil") from _scope_ hedges ("some men are evil"), with the slogan "be
  confident of weak effects, rather than unconfident of strong effects". This is the Gaertig &
  Simmons distinction rediscovered. A commenter argues that scope hedges can come across as _meaner_
  than "I think".
- **Devon Zuegel, "Epistemic statuses are lazy, and that is a good thing"** (Oct 2018;
  [link](https://devonzuegel.com/epistemic-statuses-are-lazy-and-that-is-a-good-thing)). Argues that
  statuses let you explore odd ideas "without having to stake their identity" on them.
- **Lizka, "Epistemic status: an explainer"** (EA Forum, Aug 2022). Warns a status "might give false
  confidence that we've addressed over-deferral."
- **Scott Alexander, "In Continued Defense of Non-Frequentist Probabilities"** (ACX, Mar 2024;
  [ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/in-continued-defense-of-non-frequentist)). Argues
  probabilities are a linguistically convenient summary and need not describe how much information
  you have.

**Lojban** (brief): it has grammaticalized evidential and attitudinal particles, so confidence
marking is built into the syntax. Hand to the evidentiality researcher.

## 5. Dead ends / contested

- **"Eliezer: say it without the hedge" and "Scott on 'probably'"**: I found no specific post for
  either (UNVERIFIED). Nearest: Yudkowsky, "Infinite Certainty" (2008), on degrees of belief.
  Commenters note that Eliezer and Scott hedge less in their prose than the culture they inspired.
- **Hyland's "one hedge every ~50 words"**: not found (UNVERIFIED). What I did find: in his corpus,
  modals ≈27% and epistemic lexical verbs ≈27% of hedges. Biber & Finegan found ~75% of academic
  texts fall in the "faceless" stance cluster.
- **Lakoff on "technically" / "strictly speaking"**: I recall these from the paper but could not
  confirm them in the text (UNVERIFIED). "Sort of", "very", "pretty", "rather" are confirmed.
- **Prince et al.'s adaptor/rounder and plausibility/attribution subtypes**: this is the standard
  secondary-literature account (e.g. Salager-Meyer 1994). I did not open the 1982 original.
- **Words vs numbers is not settled.** For numbers: Friedman et al. 2018, the medical and IPCC
  misreadings, and van der Bles 2020. For words: the Erev & Cohen preference paradox (senders resist
  numbers). Numbers can also signal false precision (Jerez-Fernandez 2014). The Mandel & Barnes
  analysts were already well calibrated. And Budescu's work shows _words plus numbers_ beats either
  alone for lay readers. The defensible synthesis: say the number, keep the word for readability,
  and put the number next to the word.
- **Hedging decline in science**: the _Science_ sample is confounded by format changes, and no
  causal story has been established. Vinkers measured hype words, not hedges. They are related but
  not the same quantity.
- **Lakoff 1975 gender claims**: built on introspection. Empirical work (O'Barr & Atkins 1980;
  Holmes 1986/1995) shows a power effect and a functional split, not a simple sex difference.
- **Jensen 2008** ("hedging raises trust"): a student sample, and a 2022 attempt found no difference
  between hedging types. Treat it as fragile.
- **Gaertig & Simmons numbers** that circulate secondhand (e.g. "82.4% chose the uncertain
  advisor"): UNVERIFIED against the paper.
