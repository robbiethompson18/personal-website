# Annunciation: research notes

Compiled 2026-11-11 from ten parallel research reports (2 Fable on rationalist and trader slang, 8
Opus on the side questions), then cut to the interesting parts. The full reports are in `raw/`,
about 30k words with every source link. Claims here come from pages a researcher actually opened
unless marked "(unverified)" or "(secondhand)".

Three follow-up reports (11 to 13 in `raw/`, about 19k more words) were added the same day, after
Robbie asked for Yudkowsky's own commentary on language, the "How to Speak Riverian" glossary and
the FX chat logs. They are the sections "Yudkowsky on language", "How to Speak Riverian" and "The FX
chat logs" below. For those three I spot-checked the quotes against the source text the researchers
saved.

On the title: Geoffrey Miller's description of the EA dialect (below) also spells it
"over-annunciated consonants".

## Short answers

- **Why the Ts.** The audible, released word-final /t/ is the best-documented "nerd / learned /
  precise" marker in American sociolinguistics. It was found independently in Bay Area nerd girls,
  yeshiva boys and gay professionals, and people produce it most when making a point in an argument.
  You joined a community that argues for sport and picked up its marker. Adult accent change is
  usually tiny; the adults who do shift are the ones who bond hard with the group.
- **ack.** It is the ASCII acknowledge control character (settled at 0x06 in the 1965/67 revision).
  As something humans say to each other it is first recorded in the Jargon File in June 1990. No one
  has measured which communities use it.
- **The serious words for qualifiers.** _Hedge_ (Lakoff 1973), split into _approximators_ and
  _shields_ (Prince, Frader & Bosk 1982); _epistemic modality_ and _epistemic stance_ in
  linguistics; _words of estimative probability_ (Kent 1964) in intelligence analysis. It has been
  studied a lot.
- **Pirahã.** Half right. Everett's "immediacy of experience principle" (2005) is a contested claim
  about Pirahã culture. The grammar that truly forces you to say how you know something belongs to
  other languages: Tuyuca, Tariana, Aymara, Matsés.
- **Cold and guttural.** Nobody has published that claim. The real one is "warm climates → more
  vowel-heavy languages", and it holds between language families but vanishes within them. Uvular
  sounds really are about 8x more common away from the equator, but that is a handful of regional
  clusters, not climate.
- **Zoomer slang.** The interesting parts are quantitative: words survive by fitting many sentence
  frames, slang spreads along racial rather than geographic lines, and fast-rising words die fast.

## Rationalist slang

**Nobody has studied it.** No linguist, anthropologist or corpus analyst has published on
rationalist or EA speech; everything is insider writing. The nearest academic piece is an STS paper
on the epistemic culture of AI safety (Ahmed et al.,
[First Monday](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/13626), 2024). There is no
LessWrong word-frequency time series either. This is an open research question someone could just
do.

**The one phonetic description.** Geoffrey Miller (2023): EAs adopt "a sort of in-group 'EA dialect'
that involves very fast speaking rate, staccato pace (fast-pause-fast-pause), and slightly
over-annunciated consonants", which he reads "as some sort of IQ-signal". One observer's ear, not a
measurement.

**Rationalists misattribute their own jargon, and it was measured.** A preregistered
[2018 survey](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/no5Fc7Br8G2vCu24h/lesswrong-diaspora-jargon-survey) of
53 LessWrongers asked whether each of 12 terms came from academia or from the community. 77% got
"Chinese Robber Fallacy" wrong and 64% got "motte and bailey" wrong (it is from Nicholas Shackel's
2005 philosophy paper). Small chatroom sample, and the only quantitative data on the dialect.

The credence vocabulary is the surprise: "epistemic status" traces through gwern to US intelligence
analysis, the same tradition as Kent (see the qualifiers section), not to Bayesian statistics or
conlangs.

**The General Semantics ancestry is confirmed by Yudkowsky.** 2011: "I'm explicitly influenced by
Korzybski on language." 2020: the route was "via 'Language in Thought and Action' and the Null-A
novels". Neither "Taboo Your Words" nor "37 Ways That Words Can Be Wrong" mentions Korzybski on the
page. A [2020 LW post](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/r5jMMbW2yN9GzWXcR/time-binders-1) argues
General Semantics was itself a jargon-heavy self-improvement community that decayed into dogma and
spun off Scientology and NLP. Which makes the mesa- row above a small loop.

**Spoken slang turns over fast.** Three time capsules: the 2012 "Shit Rationalists Say" thread, the
[2018 redo](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/kNDLkcqLMcLdrQNzG/shit-rationalists-say-2018), and a
newcomer's 2026 Lighthaven notes
([One Week in the Rat Farm](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/JAR8b9G4NpNM6seTn/one-week-in-the-rat-farm)).
Dead: akrasia, one-boxing, "is that your true rejection?". Survived 14 years: "operationalize",
"outside view", "notice I'm confused", "taboo X", "model".

**Terms drift once they carry status.** "Schelling point" (Schelling 1960: where people converge
_without_ communicating) drifted to "any place we agreed to meet". "Legible" drifted from
"simplified so a state can control it" to "easy to verify at a glance". "Infohazard" drifted from
"true information that enables harm" to "information that hurts whoever holds it". See Raemon,
["Avoiding Jargon Confusion"](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/c882tMwMMSWQbWyBC/avoiding-jargon-confusion)
(2019).

**Heavy hedging is a follower trait.** LW commenters note that Yudkowsky and Scott barely hedge in
their own prose (secondhand). One commenter called qualifiers "epistemic security theater" and was
told their own comment began with "I think"
([2020 thread](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/rEKhZQNPpw2NgcdKu/should-we-use-qualifiers-in-speech)).

**The internal argument about jargon.** For: Nate Soares ("the non-standard phraseology tracks a
non-standard way they're thinking") and gwern (a weird coinage is memorable and searchable; plain
words give an illusion of transparency). Against: gjm (it signals "bunch of weirdos with their own
jargon, avoid") and Logan Strohl (a handed-over term crowds out your own perception of the
distinction). Both sides are in the comments of Duncan Sabien's
["Sapir-Whorf for Rationalists"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PCrTQDbciG4oLgmQ5/sapir-whorf-for-rationalists)
(2023). LessWrong shipped an LLM that auto-generates hover glossaries in Nov 2024, which is an
institutional concession to the critics.

**"So, ..." as a sentence opener.** The NYT (2010) claimed it started with 1990s Valley engineers.
Mark Liberman [checked it against a corpus](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=2570):
sentence-initial "so" is rising gradually over the long run, and the programmer origin is untested.
A good model of how to check a claim like the ones in this file.

**Circling leaked in.** Sabien's
["Basics of Rationalist Discourse"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/XPv4sYrKnPzeJASuk/basics-of-rationalist-discourse)
(2023) recommends "I'm noticing that I have a story about you", which is authentic-relating phrasing
inside a rationalist norms document. No one has traced the route.

## The hacker lineage, and why you absorbed it

**One person links the two cultures.** Eric Raymond has edited the Jargon File since 1990, and in
2021 wrote the standard genealogy of the rationalist movement. He says Yudkowsky got General
Semantics second-hand, through Hayakawa and van Vogt, never the core texts.

**The Jargon File described your new speech style around 1990.**
["Hackish speech generally features extremely precise diction, careful word choice, a relatively large working vocabulary, and relatively little use of contractions or street slang."](http://www.catb.org/jargon/html/speech-style.html)
And the norm on dosage: "use just enough jargon to communicate precisely and identify oneself as a
member of the culture; overuse of jargon... is considered tacky and the mark of a loser." Caveat:
that chapter is Raymond's normative self-portrait. Bucholtz (2001), in the Ts section, is the
independent check, and she found the same register in teenagers.

**Why rationalists say "my brain wants".** The File's explanation of anthropomorphic tech-talk:
hackers "are expressing not a vitalistic view of program behavior but a mechanistic view of human
behavior". It cites Dennett's intentional stance, and adds a quietly therapeutic reading: "by
displacing from 'I got confused' to 'It got confused', the programmer is not avoiding
responsibility, but rather getting some analytical distance"
([Anthropomorphization](http://www.catb.org/jargon/html/anthropomorphization.html)). "My model says"
and "my System 1 is screaming" are the same move.

**The fossil layer is a 1959 model-railroad club.** The MIT Tech Model Railroad Club
[dictionary](https://tmrc.mit.edu/old/dictionary.html) has _hack_ ("an article or project without
constructive end"), _kludge_ ("a crock that works"), _cruft_, and _win_ / _lose_, with _winner_
glossed "very rare at TMRC".

**Checkable etymologies for math-speak.** "For values of" comes from the 1960s MIT language MAD's
`FOR VALUES OF X = 3, 7, 99` loop. "Bandwidth" for human attention "began to go mainstream after the
Internet population explosion of 1993-1994". "Cycles" is "a notional unit of human thought power".
IMHO came "from SF fandom via Usenet". No one has done a corpus study of "modulo" or "orthogonal"
moving into casual English; the Jargon File is the best evidence there is.

**Nerd literalism is in there too.** Asked "are you working on finding that bug now or leaving it
until later?", a hacker answers "Yes!"

**Newcomers absorb, then freeze.** Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al. (2013) tracked two beer-review
communities for 10 years (4.5M posts). Members move toward the community's language for about the
first third of their eventual time there, then stop adapting while the community's norms drift away
from them; most are linguistically conservative by the time they leave. Reviewers who joined in 2003
kept writing "Aroma" after the community switched to "S". Features from a user's first ~20 posts add
2–12 points of F1 to a model predicting when they will leave. How fast you pick up the jargon, and
when you stop, is a measurable signal of how long you will stay.

**The corporate version, with career outcomes.** Srivastava, Goldberg, Manian & Potts
([2018](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8qn146c5)) scored 601 employees' linguistic fit from 10.24M
internal emails. Low-fit employees had a 46% chance of being fired by year 3, four times the median.
The biggest single driver of fit was swearing, not technical vocabulary. Register matters more than
jargon.

**Matching someone's language is a readout of power.** In
["Echoes of power"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.3670) (2012), future Wikipedia admins echoed other
people's wording more _before_ promotion and less after. Lawyers echoed the Supreme Court Justices
who would vote against them more than the ones on their side. A newcomer soaking up group idiom is
showing textbook low-power, high-ambition behaviour.

**Low status causes more jargon, modestly.** Brown, Anicich & Galinsky
([2020](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/2020-brown.pdf)): 40.8% of participants
assigned low status chose the jargon-heavy pitch, against 29.2% of high-status ones (p≈.05). Their
"jargon" is buzzwords and acronyms, not technical slang.

**Jargon repels outsiders even when you define it.** Shulman et al. (2020, N=650): jargon versions
of science texts lowered readers' interest and self-rated ability, and hover-over definitions did
nothing. "The use of difficult, specialized words are a signal that tells people that they don't
belong." LessWrong's 2024 fix for its jargon problem is hover-over definitions.

**You can watch jargon form in 20 minutes.** In Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs's
[1986](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1980s/Clark.Wilkes-Gibbs.86.pdf) task, pairs describing
abstract figures went from 41 words per figure to 8 within six rounds: "looks like an angel flying
away or something. It's got two arms" became "the angel". Only 8 pairs, but a 2020 replication with
83 pairs held up.

**Network shape decides whether one word wins.** In naming-game experiments, fully mixed groups
always converged on one convention and clustered networks split into rival local ones (Centola &
Baronchelli 2015); a committed minority of about 25% could flip an established convention (Centola
et al. 2018; trivial stakes, so treat the number loosely). Dense group houses plus one shared forum
is the fast-converging configuration.

**Mimicry is not affection.** People match each other's function words as strongly in the hostile
Watergate tapes as in friendly talk (Niederhoffer & Pennebaker 2002); matching measures engagement.
The famous claim that mimicry makes people like you (the chameleon effect) failed a preregistered
replication.

## Yudkowsky on language

Yes, he has written about this at length. Philosophy of language is one of the most developed parts
of his output, and his stated position on hedging is close to the opposite of how the community
talks. Full report, with a link for every post: `raw/11-yudkowsky-language.md`.

**He is against hedging, and also against made-up numbers.**

- ["Say It Loud"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/hAfmMTiaSjEY8PxXC/say-it-loud) (2008) is his most
  direct statement. Goofus says: "The sky, if such a thing exists at all, might or might not have a
  property of color, but, if it does have color, then I feel inclined to state that it might be
  green." Gallant says: "70% probability the sky is green." His verdict on hedgers: "They are
  skilled at sounding uncertain, which makes them appear modest; but not skilled at handling
  uncertainty." He reads caveats as blame insurance: "We attached a caveat, didn't we?"
- ["When (Not) To Use Probabilities"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/AJ9dX59QXokZb35fk/when-not-to-use-probabilities)
  (2008) is the counterweight: "Numbers should come from numbers." It also explains his own prose:
  his posts "generally use words like 'maybe' and 'probably' and 'surely' instead of assigning
  made-up numerical probabilities like '40%' and '70%' and '95%'. Think of how silly that would
  look."
- ["I don't know."](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Pm83rA8MTYYeR4Ci4/i-don-t-know) (2006) separates
  the belief, which is always a distribution, from the utterance, which is a social act: "'I don't
  know' is the political utterance".
- He did not coin p(doom) and dislikes it. In an
  [X thread](https://x.com/ESYudkowsky/status/1823530314087718982) (14 Aug 2024) he lists five
  objections, the last being that "the actual social role being played by this number is as some
  sort of weird astrological sign". The thread opens "I spent two decades yelling at nearby people
  to stop trading their insane made-up 'AI timelines' at parties" (that first tweet is quoted from a
  [LessWrong repost](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4mBaixwf4k8jk7fG4/yudkowsky-on-don-t-use-p-doom),
  2025).
- On modesty as a way of talking he is hostile.
  ["Twelve Virtues of Rationality"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7ZqGiPHTpiDMwqMN2/twelve-virtues-of-rationality)
  (2006): "To confess your fallibility and then do nothing about it is not humble; it is boasting of
  your modesty."
  ["The Proper Use of Humility"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/GrDqnMjhqoxiqpQPw/the-proper-use-of-humility)
  (2006) calls it "social modesty" that "has to do with regulating status in the tribe".

So the calibrated-qualifier dialect is not something he prescribes. His rule, as the researcher
summarises it: say a number when it comes from a numerical process or trained calibration, otherwise
say "probably" once and move on. In a
[2021 dialogue](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7im8at9PmhbT4JHsW/ngo-and-yudkowsky-on-alignment-difficulty)
he says readers took away "a vague spirit of how to reason and argue" and not the math. Connecting
that to follower hedging is the researcher's inference, not his claim.

**"Short words are a conserved resource."**
["Entropy, and Short Codes"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/soQX8yXLbKy7cFvy8/entropy-and-short-codes)
(2008) applies coding theory to vocabulary: an optimal code gives the shortest codewords to the most
frequent messages, so a concept you use constantly deserves a short word. That is his licence for
jargon, and (my inference) it is the argument for "ack". The same sequence has an anti-jargon clause
that the community mostly ignores.
["Taboo Your Words"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/WBdvyyHLdxZSAMmoz/taboo-your-words) (2008): "be
careful not to let yourself invent a new word to use instead." The index to the whole sequence is
["37 Ways That Words Can Be Wrong"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/FaJaCgqBKphrDzDSj/37-ways-that-words-can-be-wrong)
(2008).

**He designed a language, in fiction.** Baseline is the engineered language of dath ilan in Project
Lawful (2021 to 2023). The design properties he gives it:

- Phonemes are "a minimum distance away from each other that guarantees people with slightly less
  acute hearing can understand it when spoken under slightly adverse conditions"
  ([reply](https://glowfic.com/replies/1812931#reply-1812931), 2022). This is the only place he
  treats pronunciation as engineering. A fully released /t/ is a move in the same direction (my
  connection, not his).
- Marking how you know something is cheap. His character complains about the other language: "'I
  infer though it was not said explicitly,' oh my ass Taldane how you can you take that many
  syllables to say that, people would just not say it"
  ([reply](https://glowfic.com/replies/1736183#reply-1736183), 2021). The evidential languages in
  the Pirahã section do this with one suffix.
- Short words for frequent concepts: "What kind of language makes confusion a three-syllable word,
  anyways?" ([reply](https://glowfic.com/replies/1660282#reply-1660282), 2021).
- Rationality proverbs rhyme by design so they can be recalled under stress, including "I notice I
  am confused; therefore something I believe is fiction"
  ([reply](https://glowfic.com/replies/1813028#reply-1813028), 2022).
- No word for an accusation with no stated reason: Baseline lacks "antisocial" because nobody added
  "a word that means 'what you're doing is bad for society but I won't tell you why'"
  ([reply](https://glowfic.com/replies/1616248#reply-1616248), 2021).

A conlanger who reconstructed [Baseline's phonology](https://gliese1337.blogspot.com/2022/06) (2022)
notes that the names in the story break the spacing rule: they contain s, θ and f together. Gwern's
["On the Existence of Powerful Natural Languages"](https://gwern.net/language) (2016 to 2019) argues
against the premise that a designed general-purpose language makes speakers smarter.

**The speaker owns the misunderstanding.**
["Illusion of Transparency"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/sSqoEw9eRP2kPKLCz/illusion-of-transparency-why-no-one-understands-you)
(2007) reports Keysar and Henly (2002): "Speakers thought that they were understood in 72% of cases
and were actually understood in 61% of cases."
["Rationality and the English Language"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Lz64L3yJEtYGkzMzu/rationality-and-the-english-language)
(2007): "Whatever the audience thinks you said is what you said, whether you meant to say it or
not".

**Terms get stretched, so he keeps replacing them.** His theory is the Arbital page
["Guarded definition"](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/guarded-definition) (2015): set aside a narrow
term when you expect pressure to stretch it. He rejected "AI safety" in 2015 (there is no separate
theory of "bridge safety"). By 2024 he was saying "alignment" had been "coopted to mean systems that
scold users" and preferring "AI notkilleveryoneism", a word built to resist stretching (quoted via
[Zvi](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/oJp2BExZAKxTThuuF/the-gemini-incident-continues), 2024; the
original tweet was not located).

**Where the theory was tested.** His 2018 tweets and a February 2021 Facebook post argued that
pronoun choice is a protocol decision, not an assertion of fact. Zack M. Davis argues across several
long posts, starting with
["Where to Draw the Boundaries?"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/esRZaPXSHgWzyB2NL/where-to-draw-the-boundaries)
(2019), that this contradicts the 2008 sequence, which says category boundaries are not arbitrary.
Both sides cite the same 2008 posts. (The Facebook post was not fetched; it is quoted via Davis.)

**What he has not written.** The researcher found nothing in his nonfiction about how rationalists

## Trader slang, and the bridge

**Jane Street teaches "prior" and "update" itself.** Its 13-page
["Probability & Markets"](https://www.janestreet.com/static/pdfs/trading-interview.pdf) guide (2017,
revised 2025) bolds both words a few pages before the desk phrasebook: "2 bid for 10", "10 at 4", "2
at 4, 10 up", "sold", "take 'em", "I'm out". Some of what feels like rationalist slang you probably
had already; both communities got it from Bayes independently.

**Pit grammar encodes buy/sell three times.** A bid is price _for_ size ("8 for 10"); an offer is
size _at_ price ("5 at 8"); palm in means buy, palm out means sell. Preposition, word order and hand
each carry direction independently, so a half-heard shout still parses. Only the last digit of the
price is signalled because everyone knows the leading digits, hence "zero is a clenched fist"
(MacKenzie 2007). Brokers also read volume as data: pit noise ran from "controlled loud" to "panic
loud".

**Adverse selection has a traceable route into LessWrong.** Agustin Lebron's _The Laws of Trading_
(2019), Law 2 → an
[ACX book-review finalist](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/your-book-review-the-laws-of-trading)
(2023) → Heicklen's LW post (2024): "conditional on getting to trade, your trade wasn't all that
great." Jane Street's own gloss is the Groucho Marx club line.

**"Bet on it" is an economist import, not trader idiom.** Tabarrok's "a bet is a tax on bullshit" is
2012, Caplan's "What Does the Betting Norm Tax?" is 2009, and Hanson's
["Could Gambling Save Science?"](https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/gamble.html) (written 1990) already
says "put up or shut up". Desks say "make me a market", which is a different speech act: you must
quote both sides, so being loud isn't enough. (That contrast is the researcher's read, not a sourced
claim.)

**SIG and Jane Street disagree about poker.** SIG has trained with poker since at least 2010. Jane
Street invented Figgie in 2013, and Ross Rheingans-Yoo (ex-Jane Street)
[argues on LW](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PypgeCxFHLzmBENK4/poker-is-a-bad-game-for-teaching-epistemics-figgie-is-a)
(2024) that poker gives almost no feedback on whether you were right for the right reasons and that
bluffing instincts are "downright perverse for actual trading".

**The SBF intern coin-flip story was bad EV.** Heicklen reworks Michael Lewis's numbers in
[Asterisk](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/05/michael-lewis-s-blind-side) (2024): "he was giving out
free expectancy in exchange for volatility."

**Nate Silver needed a ~25-page glossary.** "How to Speak Riverian" in _On the Edge_ (2024) treats
poker, Wall Street, EA and rationalists as one speech community. It is the closest thing to a
dictionary of the blend you noticed in yourself. The entries are in the "How to Speak Riverian"
section below.

**Origin stories that held up.** "Fannie Mae" was bond-trader slang in the WSJ on 16 May 1938,
thirty years before it was a brand. "Yard" (a billion) is from French _milliard_. "Cable" (GBP/USD)
is from the 1866 transatlantic cable (the usual story; weakly sourced). "Bill and Ben" is rhyming
slang for yen. "Fat finger" was caught as live speech on a London desk in 2000 (Zaloom,
[_Out of the Pits_](https://press.uchicago.edu/Misc/Chicago/978133.php), 2006), where the right
mouse button was "the danger button".

**Real desk chat.** The 2014 FX-fixing transcripts
([Bloomberg](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/financial-services/inside-the-cartell-chat-room-traders-boasted-of-whacking-fx-market-1.1999324))
are the best public corpus, and the primary regulator documents are excerpted in "The FX chat logs"
below. Confirmations there are "ok", "ta" and "done"; there is no "ack" anywhere in about 265k words
of them. The researcher found one finance attestation of "ack", a 2022 bond-desk blog saying it
spread from one colleague's messages. So "ack" looks like a programmer word that reached trading
through tech-heavy shops.

**Which way the words flowed:**

| Term                        | Direction                                                                       |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| prior / update              | common ancestor (Bayes); both taught it independently                           |
| adverse selection           | trader → rationalist (Lebron 2019, Heicklen 2024)                               |
| EV, edge, Kelly             | gambling → trading → rationalist                                                |
| tilt, resulting             | poker → SIG → Annie Duke's _Thinking in Bets_ (2018) → rationalist (secondhand) |
| bet on it / tax on bullshit | economists → rationalist; not desk idiom                                        |
| make a market / how wide    | trader → rationalist, via Heicklen's bootcamps and Manifold                     |
| overrated / underrated      | Tyler Cowen; functionally a long/short call with no price                       |

**WallStreetBets slang as a volatility signal.** Hayakawa et al.
([Economics Letters](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolet/v244y2024ics0165176524005111.html), 2024)
built a Herfindahl index of phrase concentration over 150k WSB comments. When a few phrases
dominate, volatility rises afterwards, and the effect also runs the other way (abstract only).

More reading: Byrne Hobart's
["Understanding Jane Street"](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/www.thediff.co/2a03539d16f1b51e27268f9fe6ceb794d053ce6b.html)
(2022); Patrick McKenzie's podcast with
[Heicklen](https://www.complexsystemspodcast.com/episodes/teaching-trading-ricki-heicklen/) (2024,
"garbitrage"); ["Trader's Esperanto"](https://fredscorner.substack.com/p/traders-esperanto) (2026),
a European voice-broking phrasebook ("You guys are quoting wider than Drake's Passage!").

**Register.**

- Messages are 1 to 8 words, sent in bursts, lowercase, with typos left in. Capitals are emphasis
  ("YESssssssssssss").
- It is London and Essex working-class speech used by people at global banks: "my son", "have that",
  "blimey", "nowt", "ta", "tenner". The European Commission says one room was called "Essex Express
  'n the Jimmy" because every trader but James lived in Essex and met on the train. The New York
  rand room says "dude", "bucks" and "custys".
- The 1996 dealing screens were the opposite style: all caps, price first, three turns ("CHF 10 PLS"
  / "6267" / "67" / "10 MID AGREED"). The 2008–2013 rooms kept their abbreviations (tks, ntg, mom,
  pls) and added banter.
- Rituals: positions disclosed before the fix in a set form ("lhs in cable at the fix" / "good
  amount"), then "gd lk fellas"; within a minute after, "well done gents", "bravo", "I don my hat",
  then the scores.
- **"ack" is absent.** There are zero tokens of "ack", "acked" or "kk" in about 265,000 words of
  extracted text. Receipt is "ta", "thx", "ok" or "cool"; a filled order is "done".

- The three traders criminally charged over the Cartel room (Usher, Ramchandani and Ashton) were
  acquitted by a Manhattan jury on 26 Oct 2018. The defence was that competing traders sharing
  position information is not illegal. The indictment contains no chat quotes, and the researcher
  found no public trial exhibits.
- The banks paid: the CFTC took $1.475bn and the FCA £1.1bn in November 2014, and in May 2015

- Not obtained: the full European Commission decisions, the Deutsche Bank and Standard Chartered

## Why you started enunciating your Ts

**What you are probably doing.** American word-final /t/ is normally unreleased or glottal ("cat"
with no audible burst), flapped between vowels ("butter"), and deleted in clusters ("mos' people").
Releasing it with an audible burst is the marked variant: in a Raleigh corpus, prepausal /t/ after a
vowel is released only about 17% of the time. "Enunciating Ts" is some mix of releasing final /t/
and not deleting it in clusters. You can test which by recording yourself with old friends and with
rationalists, which is how Podesva studied a doctor in his clinic and at a barbecue.

**Released /t/ means "learned and precise", in three unrelated groups.** Eckert (2008) uses it as
her main example of one sound carrying a field of meanings: articulate, educated, precise, emphatic,
exasperated, prissy. She finds it in nerd girls, Orthodox Jewish boys and gay men, and traces it to
a "school-teachery standard" of clear speech.

**People release /t/ when they are making a point.** Benor (2004): Orthodox males released
phrase-final /t/ 47% of the time, females 19%. In her transcripts a teacher releases /t/ while she
holds the answer and goes glottal once a student challenges her; a boy who normally glottalizes
releases only when contradicting someone ("No, you're not."). Her summary: release marks "adamancy,
authority, and learnedness". A debate culture triggers this constantly.

**Nerds talk like writing.** Bucholtz (2001, [PDF](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0663j2rg))
studied Bay Area high-school nerds in 1995–96 and found "superstandard English": no "gonna" or
"hafta", clusters kept intact, spelling pronunciations. One kid: "most people abbreviate- cut off
half the words? For no particular reason? And I don't do that." Her argument is that careful speech
imitates reading, and reading indexes intelligence. Those kids are about 46 now and some of them are
presumably at Lighthaven.

**A speech therapist said the same about SF fans.** At a 1999 convention, a non-fan speech-language
pathologist reported that fans "articulate more than mundanes": they pronounce the final /k/ in
"talk to" and the "l" in "folk", pause at commas, and use eye contact to hand over the turn.
Anecdotal, reported secondhand on Usenet
([Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/20160303200628/http://www.fantasymaps.com/stuff/fanspeak.html)).
Rationalist culture descends from that fandom.

**Being challenged makes hyperarticulation stick.** Buz, Tanenhaus & Jaeger
([2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4927008)): when a listener occasionally
misunderstands, speakers nearly double how carefully they articulate the confusable sound, and it
carries over to later words. Rare words also erode less: in Bybee's (2002) data "told" lost its /d/
68% of the time and "meant" never lost its /t/. A vocabulary of rare words spoken to people who
nitpick exact wording pushes the same way from two directions.

**Precision is one bundle.** Beltrama, Solt & Burnett (2023): people who say "forty-nine minutes"
rather than "fifty" are rated more articulate and intelligent, and also more pedantic and uptight.
That is the same mix released /t/ gets. Exact numbers, explicit hedges and crisp Ts signal one
persona.

**How unusual your shift is.** Lab accent convergence is "subtle, variable, and inconsistent" (Pardo
et al. 2017, 92 talkers). Eleven people wintering in Antarctica shifted by amounts the author called
"very, very small" (Harrington et al. 2019). In the UK Big Brother house the group did not converge,
but after two contestants became a couple her /t/-/d/ deletion rate dropped below his (Sonderegger,
Bane & Graff,
[2017](https://people.linguistics.mcgill.ca/~morgan/sonderegger_graff_bane_mediumterm_language.pdf)).
The pattern across studies: most adults stay put, and the ones who move are strongly bonded to the
people they move toward.

**The autism angle, which is speculative.** Autistic adults' vowels are acoustically more stable,
the first measured correlate of "over-precise" diction (Kissine & Geelhand 2019, 20 vs 20). A more
autistic-like attention-switching score predicted _more_ phonetic imitation (Yu, Abrego-Collier &
Sonderegger 2013). Autistic pairs align with each other more than non-autistic pairs do (Jameson &
Bean 2025). No one has studied a person dropping a mumbled register after joining an autistic-dense
group.

**It may be spreading generally.** Dodsworth & Mielke
([2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/EA0EDC4C80EDDD5B2FB679E2F3A1C241/core-reader)):
across 233 Raleigh speakers, released /t/ is getting more frequent among White speakers, most among
those with graduate degrees, and the authors link it to educated in-migrants arriving for tech and
university jobs.

One wrinkle: context decides the meaning. Obama was reportedly rated _more_ intelligent when he did
not release /t/, because it matched how he usually talks (Podesva et al. 2015, via a later summary).

## "ack"

**It is younger than it looks as human speech.** The researcher searched the Jargon File archive:
"ACK" is absent from every version from 1976 to 1983, including the 1983 _Hacker's Dictionary_. It
first appears on 12 June 1990, glossed "prob. from _Bloom County_", with the "Ack. Ack. Ack. I get
it now" sense and NAK meaning "I'm not here" added by 1991. No dated human use before 1990 was
found, though Usenet archives weren't searchable.

**Conversation analysts found the Two Generals problem in talk.** Clark & Brennan (1991): every
contribution needs evidence it was understood, but if "uh huh" needs its own acknowledgement the
acks never end. Their way out is that the relevant next turn counts as the ack, which is exactly TCP
piggybacking ACKs on data.

**"Okay" is a stack operation.** Bangerter & Clark
([2003](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/2000s/Bangerter,%20A.%20_%20Clark,%20H.H.%20_Navigating%20joint%20projects%20with%20dialogue_%202003.pdf))
split acknowledgements into vertical ones (okay, all right: enter or leave a sub-task) and
horizontal ones (uh-huh, yeah: keep going), and make the push/pop comparison themselves. A Slack
"ack" closes a thread, so it is the vertical kind.

**Every ack commits you to something different:**

| Token            | What it commits you to                                                     |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Morse R          | received, not necessarily understood                                       |
| Roger            | I received all of your last transmission                                   |
| Wilco            | understood and will comply ("roger wilco" is redundant)                    |
| Aye aye          | understood and will obey                                                   |
| TCP ACK          | bytes reached the peer's stack; says nothing about the application         |
| PagerDuty ack    | I own this; it is a lease that expires                                     |
| Linux `Acked-by` | approval, less than a full review                                          |
| 👍               | a contract signature, per a Saskatchewan court (2023, upheld 2024, ~C$82k) |

Saltzer, Reed & Clark ([1984](https://web.mit.edu/Saltzer/www/publications/endtoend/endtoend.pdf))
make the underlying point: the ack you actually want is "I did it" or "I didn't".

**An ambiguous "OK" killed 101 people.** Eastern 401 (1972): the controller asked "How are things
comin' along up there?", the crew answered "OK" meaning the nose gear, and the controller took it to
mean the altitude. Tenerife (1977) is why "takeoff" is now a reserved word, said only when giving or
cancelling a takeoff clearance. See Cushing,
[_Flight Safety Digest_](https://flightsafety.org/fsd_jul95) (1995).

**Readback is cheap.** Requiring readback on 822 critical lab-result phone calls caught 29 errors
(3.5%) at 12.8 seconds per call (Barenfanger et al. 2004). Railroads were doing repeat-back with a
dispatcher's "Complete" in the 1850s.

**A phone ring is a SYN.** Schegloff
([1968](https://conversationanalysis.org/mp-files/sequencing-in-conversational-openings.pdf))
described the opening of a call as a summons–answer handshake with retransmission and timeout: an
unanswered summons is repeated, and silence licenses "no answer, no person".

**Terse acks cost something outside cultures that use them natively.** Across 8 studies and 5,300+
people, texts with abbreviations read as less sincere and got fewer replies (Fang, Zhang & Maglio
2025). Applying that to "ack" is inference.

**Communities keep turning ACK into a grammar.** Bitcoin Core has Concept ACK, Approach ACK, utACK
and `ACK <commit>`, with a rule that a NACK needs reasons; "Concept ACK" appears in about 8.2k of
roughly 25.5k PRs. LessWrong's reacts include "I Saw This" (pure receipt) and "Bowing Out" (roughly
a FIN).

Unrelated homonym: "ack-ack" for anti-aircraft fire is British WWI signalese for the letter A.

## The serious words for qualifiers

**Your examples, classified:**

| You say                    | Term                                                  | Source                              |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| "approximately", "roughly" | approximator (a "rounder")                            | Prince, Frader & Bosk 1982          |
| "I think", "my guess is"   | plausibility shield; epistemic stance marker          | Prince et al. 1982; Kärkkäinen 2003 |
| "per gwern, ..."           | attribution shield                                    | Prince et al. 1982                  |
| "I propose", "I claim"     | explicit performative; a hedge on illocutionary force | Austin 1962; Brown & Levinson 1987  |
| "might", "probably"        | epistemic modality                                    | Palmer 1986                         |
| "70%", "3:1"               | numeric credence                                      | forecasting literature              |
| "Epistemic status: ..."    | document-level metadiscourse                          | Hyland 2005                         |

The approximator/shield split is the useful one. Approximators change the _content_ of the claim.
Shields leave the content alone and change _your commitment_ to it. "I propose" is a third thing: it
swaps an assertion for a proposal, so you are on the hook for "worth considering", not "true". The
original "hedge" (Lakoff 1973) was about fuzzy category membership ("a penguin is _sort of_ a
bird"), not uncertainty.

**The founding story, corrected.** In 1951 a national intelligence estimate called a Soviet attack
on Yugoslavia "a serious possibility". Kent meant about 65:35 in favour. When he polled the board
members _who had agreed on the wording_, their readings ran from 20:80 to 80:20. The spread was
among the authors, not the readers.

**The 51% anecdote.** Heuer
([1999](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Pyschology-of-Intelligence-Analysis.pdf), pp.
153–155) argued for months with a colleague over whether a source was "probably" genuine or
"probably" hostile-controlled. When they put numbers on it, each said "at least 51%" for his side,
and the argument ended.

**How bad the words are.** For "likely", the middle 90% of answers runs from 55% to 90%; for "real
possibility", 20% to 80% (Mauboussin & Mauboussin 2018, ~1,700 people). EU regulators define a
"common" side effect as 1–10%; patients told "common" estimated 34% (Knapp et al. 2004). Lay readers
put "rare" about 100x above its official definition (Andreadis et al. 2021, 33 studies). Readers
pull the IPCC's "very likely" (meant as >90%) toward 50% (Budescu et al. 2009, 2014).

**Numbers carry real information.** Friedman, Baker, Mellers, Tetlock & Zeckhauser
([2018](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.dartmouth.edu/dist/0/433/files/2017/06/Value-of-Precision-June-17.pdf))
took 888,328 Good Judgment forecasts and rounded them into coarser bins after the fact. Two bins
made Brier scores 31% worse on average, and over 500% worse for superforecasters. Rounding to the
intelligence community's seven official words lost accuracy for every subgroup. Caveat: the rounding
was post hoc, so this shows the numbers contain information, not that people forced to use words
reason worse.

**Does rationalist hedging cost you socially? It depends on which kind.** Gaertig & Simmons (2018,
11 studies, N=4,806): people penalize an unconfident _advisor_ ("I'm not sure, but...") and do not
penalize uncertain _advice_ ("57% chance"). So a confidently stated "70%" is safe and "I think
maybe, idk" is not. An LW post by Elo reached the same split in 2016: "be confident of weak effects,
rather than unconfident of strong effects."

**Calibration beats confidence once people can check.** Confident witnesses lose more credibility
than hedged ones when caught in an error (Tenney et al. 2007). Uncertainty given as a numeric range
barely dents trust; the same uncertainty in words dents it more (van der Bles et al., PNAS 2020,
N=5,780). And hedge your interpretations, not your observations: a hedge on a data statement hurts
persuasion more (Britt, Durik et al. 2008).

**Professionals can over-hedge.** Canadian intelligence analysts' 1,514 numeric forecasts were well
calibrated, and most of the error was *under*confidence, worse on the forecasts that mattered more
(Mandel & Barnes, PNAS 2014).

**Science is hedging less.** Hedge words in _Science_ research articles fell about 40% from 1997 to
2021 (Yao et al. 2023; confounded by format changes). Positive hype words in PubMed abstracts rose
from 2% to 17.5% (Vinkers et al., BMJ 2015).

**"It must be raining" sounds weaker than "it's raining".** von Fintel & Gillies
([2010](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/65909)) argue _must_ is logically strong but signals
that you inferred it rather than saw it. English modals are half evidentials, which leads into the
next section.

**For your day job.** RLHF flips models from hedging to sounding certain, because human preference
data is biased against uncertain text; 47% of the answers models gave confidently were wrong (Zhou,
Hwang, Ren & Sap, [ACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.198)).

## Pirahã and the grammar of evidence

**What you were remembering.** Daniel Everett (2005) claimed Pirahã culture restricts talk to what
the speaker, or someone alive, has directly experienced. The Pirahã response to stories about Jesus
was "Have you met this man?", and when Everett admitted nobody he knew had, they lost interest; he
later became an atheist. Your gloss is fair, with two caveats. It is a claim about culture, not a
grammar rule, and Everett's 2009 restatement explicitly allows "overheard, deduced". And it is
heavily contested (Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues 2009); whether Pirahã has recursion is still
unresolved. The best-supported Pirahã finding is that there are no exact number words.

**The languages that really force it.** In Tuyuca you cannot say "he played soccer" without one of
five verb endings: I saw it, I heard it, I saw traces of it, I was told, or I assume (Barnes 1984).

**Getting it wrong is a moral failing.** Aymara: use the eyewitness form for something you didn't
witness and "the speaker is judged a liar and as one who shows disrespect towards the listener".
Anything you read in a book takes the "they say" form, which is about source, not truth
([Hardman](https://mason.gmu.edu/~ataylor/dvd_project/Readings/Data%20source%20and%20person%20in%20Jaqi.pdf),
1988–89). Tariana speakers call Portuguese-speaking whites liars because "they never tell you how
they know things", and have a word, _medite_ ("a useless person"), for someone whose evidential
choice is off (Aikhenvald 2018).

**Dreams.** Ordinary Tariana speakers report dreams with the "I didn't see it" evidential. A
powerful shaman uses the eyewitness form: "He is a real shaman, he sees everything." The grammar
encodes who counts as having access.

**Source and credence are separate axes.** In Cuzco Quechua, "It is raining (they say), but I don't
believe it" is a fine sentence (Faller 2002). Evidentials mark where a claim came from, not how
confident you are. Most rationalist qualifiers mark confidence ("70%", "low confidence"); "I'm told
X" is the closest thing to an evidential. Marking both separately ("hearsay, 30%") would be an
upgrade over either.

**Matsés.** Asked how many wives he has, a Matsés man whose wives are out of sight answers in the
past tense, roughly "there were two last time I checked" (Deutscher's popularization of Fleck 2007).
The viral claim that Matsés speakers count the wrong evidential as a lie could not be traced to
Fleck (unverified); the verified versions of that claim are Aymara and Tariana.

**Other minds are grammatically inferred in Japanese.** You can say "(I'm) sad", but not flatly "he
is sad"; you need "he appears sad".

**Liars over-use the eyewitness form.** In Turkish, deceptive retellings use more of the direct past
_-dI_ (Aydın et al., [CogSci 2024](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/64v7v3b8)). Once source-marking
is a norm, faking "I saw it" becomes the attack. Bulgarian journalists use the reported-speech form
when they doubt a claim, and it clusters in articles later flagged by fact-checkers.

**It mostly does not change cognition.** Korean children track sources no better than
English-speaking children (Papafragou, Li, Choi & Han 2007). Turkish 4-year-olds are briefly ahead;
the gap is gone by 6. The one adult effect points an unexpected way: Turkish speakers remember
firsthand-marked sentences better and hearsay _worse_ than English speakers do (Tosun, Vaid & Geraci
2013). The grammar may train you to discount and forget hearsay, not to track sources better.

**There is grammar for common knowledge.** Andoke and Kogi mark whether the _listener_ already knows
the thing, so speakers must keep modelling the other person's knowledge state (Evans, Bergqvist &
San Roque 2018).

How common is all this: about a quarter of languages by Aikhenvald's strict definition, 57% (237
of 418) in the WALS sample by a loose one.

## Climate, bodies and phonemes

**What you were half-remembering.** A 30-year line of work says warm-climate languages are more
sonorous (more vowels, simpler syllables) and cold-climate ones more consonant-heavy. The latest
test, Wang, Wichmann et al. ([2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgad384), 5,293 languages),
finds it holds across language families and _cannot be detected within them_. A between-group effect
with no within-group effect fits slow climate pressure and plain confounding equally well.

**"Guttural" by latitude.** The researcher tabulated PHOIBLE (2,085 languages with coordinates).
This is descriptive only, with no control for relatedness:

| Distance from equator | Languages | Have a uvular (q, χ) | Have an ejective |
| --------------------- | --------- | -------------------- | ---------------- |
| 0–15°                 | 1,207     | 4.4%                 | 5.1%             |
| 15–30°                | 515       | 10.5%                | 7.0%             |
| 30–45°                | 229       | 37.1%                | 22.7%            |
| 45°+                  | 134       | 34.3%                | 17.2%            |

So your memory has a factual basis, but it is a handful of regional clusters (Caucasus, Semitic,
Turkic and Siberian, Pacific Northwest), and Australia has zero uvulars at any latitude. The
European "guttural r" is the clearest case of the real mechanism: a fashion that spread from
17th-century Paris, city to city ([Gorrie 2021](https://colingorrie.pages.dev/articles/guttural-r)).

**Why the genre is shaky.** Languages are not independent data points; they share ancestors and
borrow from neighbours (Galton's problem). Roberts & Winters (2013) show what that does:
siesta-taking cultures have simpler verb morphology at p=0.001, until you add a family tree and it
goes to p=0.9.

**Which sounds are common** (PHOIBLE, one inventory per language, 2,176 languages): /m/ 96.6%, /i/
93.6%, /k/ 90.5%, /j/ 90.1%. English "th" is in 6.9% of languages, English /ɹ/ in 1.7%, clicks in
0.9%. Five is the most common number of vowels. Pirahã and Rotokas have 11 phonemes each; !Xóõ has
161 in one source.

**The neatest regularity.** 38.8% of languages have /k/ but no /g/, against 4.7% the other way
round. Voicing needs air to keep flowing into the space behind the closure, and a closure at the
back of the mouth leaves the smallest space (Ohala 1983).

**Languages trade speed for density.** Across 17 languages, information per syllable varies from
about 5 to 8 bits, speech rate varies the other way, and the product lands near 39 bits/s (Coupé et
al., [Science Advances 2019](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaw2594)).

**Sound symbolism is real at world scale.** Across about two-thirds of the world's languages, "nose"
tends to have /n/, "tongue" /l/, "small" /i/ (Blasi et al., PNAS 2016).

## Zoomer slang

**Words survive by fitting many sentences, not by having many users.** Stewart & Eisenstein
([2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1709.00345)), on Reddit: the number of distinct contexts a word
appears in predicts growth and survival; spread across users and subreddits adds nothing (p=0.80).
"Kinda" beat "sorta" this way. It explains why "-maxxing" and "based" lasted: they attach to
anything.

**Online slang follows race more than distance.** Across 107M tweets, the best predictor of which US
cities pass words to each other is similarity in their Black population share (Eisenstein et al.,
[PLoS ONE 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4237389/)). Grieve et al. (2018) call
African American English "the main source of lexical innovation on American Twitter"; Atlanta
outweighs much bigger cities.

**Most "new" slang sat dormant for years.** "Bruh" (2003) and "bae" (2008) had Urban Dictionary
entries long before they took off in 2014 (Grieve, Nini & Guo 2017). "On fleek" traces to one Vine;
its originator, Kayla Newman, was never paid.

**Fast rise means fast death.** Shown on 2,570 French first names over a century (Berger & Le Mens,
[PNAS 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2688844)). The companion result is the "parents
say rizz" mechanism: when outsiders adopted Livestrong wristbands in a Stanford dorm, wearing fell
to 58% against 88%.

**Algospeak may be dodging filters that don't exist.** After Instagram banned pro-eating-disorder
tags, new spellings appeared and participation _rose_ 15–30% (Chancellor et al. 2016). On TikTok
there is no public data on which words are suppressed; creators guess. "Le dollar bean" is TikTok's
text-to-speech reading "le$bian".

**"Unalive" is now sincere.** Middle schoolers say "I feel so unalive"; in 2024 a Seattle museum
placard said Cobain "un-alived himself" and called the wording respectful. An evasion code became a
polite register.

**"Maxxing" is Dictionary.com's 2026 Word of the Year**, with "tokenmaxxing" (using as much AI at
work as possible) among its examples. The dictionary's etymology leaves out the incel forums. The
2025 pick was "6-7", which means nothing; an empty token fits every sentence, which by the Stewart &
Eisenstein result is what makes it spread.

**"Based" got vaguer as it spread**: crack-user insult → Lil B reclaiming it → 4chan's far right →
generic approval (Hagen & de Zeeuw 2023).

Nobody has shown slang turnover is actually faster now. Google n-gram work finds a new word takes
about 40 years to settle (Petersen et al. 2012).

## Can you engineer language for better thinking?

**Nobody has ever run the experiment.** Loglan (1955) was designed explicitly to test Sapir-Whorf
and instead forked into Lojban over its inventor's ownership claims. Suzette Haden Elgin built
Láadan (1984) with an obligatory evidence particle on every sentence, including one for "perceived
in a dream" and one for "assumed false because the source is distrusted", and concluded it had
failed when almost nobody learned it. A father spoke only Klingon to his son for about three years;
the boy spoke none by high school. The bottleneck has always been recruiting speakers, not designing
grammar.

**Lojban's evidentials came from Native American languages and from Láadan** (its own grammar says
so): _za'a_ I observe, _ti'e_ I hear, _ja'o_ I conclude, _pe'i_ I opine, _ru'a_ I postulate
([CLL §13.11](https://lojban.org/publications/cll/cll_v1.1_xhtml-section-chunks/section-evidentials.html)).
A 2012 LessWrong post proposed adding one that tags a claim with its log-odds in decibels. It did
not catch on; gwern's intelligence-analyst words did.

**Borges's objection covers all of them.** Wilkins's 1668 philosophical language spelled each word
by its place in a taxonomy, and filed the whale as "a viviparous, oblong fish". A language that
encodes your current map in its words freezes the map's errors into the grammar.

**E-Prime (English without "to be") has no evidence.** The only empirical test found is a 2012
thesis, correlational, which found no support.

**Linguistic relativity, the honest version.** The strong claim is dead. What survives is small,
task-specific, and switches off when your verbal system is busy: Russian speakers discriminate their
two blues faster, but not while holding an 8-digit number in mind (Winawer et al. 2007). Pirahã
speakers match large sets exactly unless the task needs memory, so number words are "a cognitive
technology for keeping track", not a change in perception (Frank, Everett, Fedorenko & Gibson 2008).
Most famous effects failed replication: gendered "bridge/key" adjectives, Mandarin vertical time,
Chinese counterfactuals. One that did replicate: "she broke the vase" raises blame over "the vase
broke" (preregistered, 2022).

The model this supports for jargon: a word is a handle for storing, retrieving and passing on a
concept. It does not change what you perceive.

**Does having the word make you use the concept?** Partly, and practice matters more than the name.
One session of a debiasing game cut named biases by about 32% immediately and 24% two months later;
a video that only explained them managed about 19% (Morewedge et al.
[2015](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/12324/); no independent replication found). Two years
of psychology or medical grad school improves statistical reasoning; chemistry improves nothing
measured (Lehman, Lempert & Nisbett 1988). Under an hour of Good Judgment Project training improved
Brier scores 6–11%. CFAR has no controlled evidence: its 2015 study had no control group, and a
small 2012 randomized trial was never reported.

**The failure mode has a name.** Robert Levy (1973) coined _hypocognition_ for lacking a concept and
_hypercognition_ for over-applying one you have. Scott Alexander's
["Concept-Shaped Holes Can Be Impossible To Notice"](https://slatestarcodex.com/2017/11/07/concept-shaped-holes-can-be-impossible-to-notice/)
(2017) is the rationalist statement of the first. Seeing Moloch and motte-and-bailey everywhere is
the second.

More: Arika Okrent, _In the Land of Invented Languages_ (2009); Joshua Foer on Ithkuil,
["Utopian for Beginners"](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2012/12/24/utopian-for-beginners) (New
Yorker, 2012).

## What this doesn't establish

- No one has measured rationalist speech. Everything connecting your Ts to the community is
  inference from studies of other nerd-coded groups plus one EA Forum comment.
- No corpus data exists on who says "ack" or since when.
- Several New Yorker, NYT and paywalled sources could not be fetched; items resting on them are
  marked in the raw reports.
- The latitude table and the PHOIBLE percentages are a researcher's own tabulation, uncontrolled for
  language relatedness.
- The K-pop / stan Twitter slang pipeline was not covered.
