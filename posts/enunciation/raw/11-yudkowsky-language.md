# 11. Yudkowsky on language: what he actually wrote

Method: every quote below was pulled from page text fetched in this session (LessWrong GraphQL API,
glowfic.com, X's public embed endpoint, consc.net, gwern.net). Only whitespace and quote-mark style
are normalized. Anything not fetched is tagged UNVERIFIED. Reading times assume 250 wpm.

## 1. Short answer

Yes, a lot, and it is one of the most developed parts of his output. Five points cover your
question.

- **Words:** a full philosophy of language ("A Human's Guide to Words", Feb–Mar 2008). Words are
  compressed pointers to clusters in "thingspace", definitions can be wrong, and word length should
  track how often you need the concept.
- **Hedging:** he is against it. His position, paraphrased: state the uncertainty compactly or don't
  bother. He prefers "70%" to a paragraph of timid qualifiers, but he also says invented numbers are
  worse than a plain "probably", and in 2024 he called p(doom) "some sort of weird astrological
  sign".
- **The follower gap:** by his own account (2021) readers absorbed "a vague spirit of how to reason
  and argue" and not the math. That is the nearest thing he has said to the "followers hedge, he
  doesn't" observation. He does not mention hedging there, so the link is my inference.
- **Engineered language:** in Project Lawful (2021–2023) he specifies a designed language, Baseline,
  with short words for common concepts, phonemes spaced for intelligibility, and proverbs that rhyme
  by design.
- **Speech:** I found nothing in his nonfiction about how rationalists sound or enunciate. The
  Baseline phoneme passage is the only place he treats pronunciation as an engineering variable.

## 2. Ranked reading list

**1. "Say It Loud" (2008, with a 2015 edit).**
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/hAfmMTiaSjEY8PxXC/say-it-loud (2 min) His most direct statement on
hedging. Vague language communicates uncertainty badly, and caveats mostly work as blame insurance.
A stated probability sounds more arrogant but carries more information.

> Goofus says: "The sky, if such a thing exists at all, might or might not have a property of color,
> but, if it does have color, then I feel inclined to state that it might be green." Gallant says:
> "70% probability the sky is green."

> They are skilled at sounding uncertain, which makes them appear modest; but not skilled at
> handling uncertainty.

**2. "When (Not) To Use Probabilities" (2008).**
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/AJ9dX59QXokZb35fk/when-not-to-use-probabilities (7 min) The
counterweight to #1. Don't run a non-numerical gut feeling through your mouth and call it a
probability. This is also where he explains why his own prose uses verbal qualifiers.

> Numbers should come from numbers.

> Which is why my Overcoming Bias posts generally use words like "maybe" and "probably" and "surely"
> instead of assigning made-up numerical probabilities like "40%" and "70%" and "95%". Think of how
> silly that would look.

**3. "Entropy, and Short Codes" (2008).**
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/soQX8yXLbKy7cFvy8/entropy-and-short-codes (5 min) The
information-theory argument for jargon. An optimal code gives short codewords to frequent messages,
so word length is not arbitrary. It includes his only nonfiction remark on how words sound.

> The moral is that short words are a conserved resource.

> And so even the labels that we use for words are not quite arbitrary. The sounds that we attach to
> our concepts can be better or worse, wiser or more foolish.

**4. Baseline passages in Project Lawful / planecrash (2021–2022).** Start at
https://glowfic.com/replies/1812931#reply-1812931 (2022), then

https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/FaJaCgqBKphrDzDSj/37-ways-that-words-can-be-wrong (11 min) The index

## 3. Themes

### 3a. Words and definitions

The sequence's core claim is that a word is a compression of a statistical regularity, so it carries
inferences, and those inferences can be false.

- **Words as pointers.** "Words as Mental Paintbrush Handles" (2008),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/YF9HB6cWCJrDK5pBM/words-as-mental-paintbrush-handles: "The labels
  are just pointers—"look in memory area 1387540"."
- **When a word earns its existence.** "Mutual Information, and Density in Thingspace" (2008),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yLcuygFfMfrfK8KjF/mutual-information-and-density-in-thingspace:
  "Because it's helpful to have shorter words for things that you encounter often." A word for a
  conjunction of properties pays only if the properties co-occur more often than chance.
- **Boundaries are a scientific question.** "Where to Draw the Boundary?" (2008),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/d5NyJ2Lf6N22AD9PB/where-to-draw-the-boundary: "Figuring where to
  cut reality in order to carve along the joints—this is the problem worthy of a rationalist."
- **"I can define it however I like" is a conversation stopper.** "Conversation Halters" (2010),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/wqmmv6NraYv4Xoeyj/conversation-halters: "Generally, anything that
  people are motivated to argue about is not arbitrary."
- **Names are not understanding.** "Say Not 'Complexity'" (2007),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/kpRSCH7ALLcb6ucWM/say-not-complexity: "It is far better to say
  "magic" than "complexity" or "emergence"; the latter words create an illusion of understanding."
  "Truly Part Of You" (2007), https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/fg9fXrHpeaDD6pEPL/truly-part-of-you,
  borrows the test from Drew McDermott's paper "Artificial Intelligence Meets Natural Stupidity"
  (1976): scramble the "suggestively named lisp tokens" and see whether anything is left. "Semantic
  Stopsigns" (2007), https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/FWMfQKG3RpZx6irjm/semantic-stopsigns, describes
  a word used as "a cognitive traffic signal: do not think past this point."
- **Deflate your own house vocabulary.** "Rationality: Appreciating Cognitive Algorithms" (2012),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/HcCpvYLoSFP4iAqSz/rationality-appreciating-cognitive-algorithms:
  "As with the word 'true', there are very few sentences that truly need to contain the word
  'rational' in them." He applies taboo to the community's favorite word.

Lineage, in his own words: "Hero Licensing" (2017),
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/dhj9dhiwhq3DX6W8z/hero-licensing, says he read Hayakawa's _Language
in Thought and Action_ "at age eleven or so", and "Null-A didn't just teach me the phrase "the map
is not the territory"". "The 5-Second Level" (2011) credits Hayakawa for the concrete/abstract
habit.

### 3b. Hedging and probabilities

His position has four parts, and they are consistent across 18 years.

1. **Beliefs are always distributions, and "I don't know" is an utterance, not a belief.** From "I
   don't know." (2006): ""I don't know", which is always something to say, not to think".
2. **Verbal hedges are low-bandwidth and often self-serving.** "Say It Loud" (2008): "Often the
   caveats we attach to our speech have little to do with any actual humility", and "We attached a
   caveat, didn't we?" The 2015 edit narrows the claim: "I am saying that it is okay to communicate
   uncertainty by saying "60% probability" rather than two paragraphs of timid language." He also
   names the cost: stating a number "may cause some who know not the Way to criticize your
   status-overreaching".
3. **Invented numbers are not better than words.** "When (Not) To Use Probabilities" (2008): "In
   fact, I generally advise against making up probabilities, unless it seems like you have some
   decent basis for them. This only fools you into believing that you are more Bayesian than you
   actually are." The same post contains the joke "I cannot be sure of this statement, of course,
   but it has a probability of 75%."
4. **Numbers as identity badges are bad.** The 2024 thread opens "I spent two decades yelling at
   nearby people to stop trading their insane made-up "AI timelines" at parties." Objection (4) is
   that "most people haven't had the elementary training in calibration and prediction markets that
   would be required for them to express this number meaningfully". When Orthonormal replied that
   p(doom) grew out of his "Death with Dignity" post (2022), he answered "the post was about
   fighting for changes in log-odds and at no point tried to give an absolute number".

So the calibrated-qualifier dialect ("fairly confident", "~70%", "epistemic status") is not
something he prescribes. His rule is: say a number when it comes from a numerical process or trained
calibration, otherwise say "probably" once and move on.

On humility as a speech register he is hostile:

- "Twelve Virtues of Rationality" (2006),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7ZqGiPHTpiDMwqMN2/twelve-virtues-of-rationality: "To confess your
  fallibility and then do nothing about it is not humble; it is boasting of your modesty."
- "The Proper Use of Humility" (2006),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/GrDqnMjhqoxiqpQPw/the-proper-use-of-humility: "This is social
  modesty, not humility. It has to do with regulating status in the tribe, rather than scientific
  process."
- _Inadequate Equilibria_ (2017) extends this into a theory that modest-sounding epistemology is
  status regulation ("Status Regulation and Anxious Underconfidence",
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/o28fkhcZsBhhgfGjx/status-regulation-and-anxious-underconfidence).
  I did not find a passage there specifically about verbal qualifiers.

He also explains why he sounds unhedged: "I am often accused of overconfidence because my audience
is not familiar with the concept of there being iron laws that govern the manipulation of
uncertainty" ("Say It Loud").

On literal truth-telling, "Meta-Honesty" (2018),
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xdwbX9pFEr7Pomaxv/meta-honesty-firming-up-honesty-around-its-edge-cases-1,
describes his own practice of asking of every spoken sentence "Is this statement actually and
literally true?" He declines to prescribe it, because the code "only lets people navigate anything
like ordinary social reality to the extent that they are very fast on their verbal feet". This is
the closest he comes to explaining the careful-literalism register: it is a personal discipline that
he thinks requires unusual verbal skill.

### 3c. Jargon and naming

He never wrote a defense of rationalist jargon as such. What exists:

- **The compression argument** (reading list #3) is an implicit licence: if you say a concept often,
  it deserves a short name. "37 Ways" lists the reverse as a failure mode: "You use a short word for
  something that you won't need to describe often, or a long word for something you'll need to
  describe often."
- **The cost side is stated equally plainly.** "Taboo Your Words" warns against inventing a
  replacement word. "Replace the Symbol with the Substance" (2008),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/GKfPL6LQFgB49FEnv/replace-the-symbol-with-the-substance, uses the
  phrase "the standard concept-handle" for the thing you must get behind. That predates the
  community's later "concept handles" usage.
- **Reserved terms as engineering.** "Linguistic conventions in value alignment" (Arbital, 2015),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/w/linguistic-conventions-in-value-alignment, assigns "value", "utility",
  "preference", "desire" and "goal" to five distinct referents: "We need a language rich enough to
  distinguish at least the following as different intensional concepts, even if their extensions end
  up being identical".
- **Regret about transmission, not about jargon.** In "Ngo and Yudkowsky on alignment difficulty"
  (2021),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7im8at9PmhbT4JHsW/ngo-and-yudkowsky-on-alignment-difficulty: "What
  they got instead was something much more meta and general, a vague spirit of how to reason and
  argue, because that was what they'd spent a lot of time being exposed to over and over and over
  again in lots of blog posts."
- **Regret about tone.** Preface to _Rationality: From AI to Zombies_ (2015),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/uXn3LyA8eNqpvdoZw/preface: "Today I would write more courteously,
  I think."

I found no statement where he regrets jargon as a barrier to outsiders. The nearest is "Explainers
Shoot High. Aim Low!" (2007),
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/2TPph4EGZ6trEbtku/explainers-shoot-high-aim-low: "This is not
because your audience is more stupid than you think, but because your words are far less helpful
than you think."

### 3d. Why communication fails

- **Measured overconfidence in being understood.** "Illusion of Transparency" reports Keysar and
  Henly (2002): "Speakers thought that they were understood in 72% of cases and were actually
  understood in 61% of cases."
- **Missing prerequisites.** "Expecting Short Inferential Distances": "If you don't recurse far
  enough, you're just talking to yourself."
- **Echo is not comprehension.** "Double Illusion of Transparency" ends with the realisation "You
  couldn't just tell people."
- **Meaning lives in the listener.** "I don't know." (2006): "the meaning of the sounds that issue
  from your lips is whatever occurs in the mind of the listener."
- **Register mismatch.** "Absolute Authority" (2008),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PmQkensvTGg7nGtJE/absolute-authority, has him drafting a line for
  lay audiences: "Scientists don't use the phrase 'not absolutely certain' the way you're used to
  from regular conversation." He sees calibrated speech as a dialect that misfires on outsiders.

### 3e. Engineered language: dath ilan's Baseline

Dath ilan is his fictional high-coordination Earth. In the 2021 Facebook AMA
(https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/gvA4j8pGYG4xtaTkw/i-m-from-a-parallel-earth-with-much-higher-coordination-ama),
asked about Esperanto-style optimized languages, he answered in character: "Finished over a hundred
years ago. The benefits of having one shared planetary language were just too obvious".

Design properties he gives Baseline, each from a reply he wrote:

- **Short codes for frequent concepts.** "(*This sentence takes less than half as many syllables to
  say in Baseline as in Taldane.)" (2021, https://glowfic.com/replies/1616079#reply-1616079). And:
  "What kind of language makes confusion a three-syllable word, anyways? One that has no idea what
  its nearly neural-level cognitive primitives are, presumably." (2021,
  https://glowfic.com/replies/1660282#reply-1660282)
- **Phonemes spaced for noise robustness.** "all the phonemes are a minimum distance away from each
  other that guarantees people with slightly less acute hearing can understand it when spoken under
  slightly adverse conditions." The in-between phonemes are "reserved for constructing 'conlangs'"
  that "add new short words using the expanded phoneme set." (2022,
  https://glowfic.com/replies/1812931#reply-1812931)
- **No words for underspecified accusations.** "Baseline doesn't have a word for 'antisocial' any
  more than it has a word for 'nonapples'", because "it hasn't been deemed wise to add a word that
  means 'what you're doing is bad for society but I won't tell you why.'" (2021,
  https://glowfic.com/replies/1616248#reply-1616248)
- **Cheap evidential markers.** ""I infer though it was not said explicitly," oh my ass Taldane how
  you can you take that many syllables to say that, people would just not say it" (2021,
  https://glowfic.com/replies/1736183#reply-1736183). The point is that expensive epistemic marking
  does not get said.
- **Audible marking of compounds and technical senses.** "A slight lilt or click of spoken Baseline;
  a common punctuation-marker in written Baseline." (2021,
  https://glowfic.com/replies/1619639#reply-1619639)
- **Precision as a property of the language.** "Taldane is a great language to speak instead of
  Baseline if you don't want your words to narrow down possible realities." (2021,
  https://glowfic.com/replies/1630397#reply-1630397)
- **Proverbs that rhyme by construction.** "Baseline's Central Cheating Poetry is mostly homilies of
  rationality that Civilization wanted people to be able to keep in mind even under moments of
  stress". One of them is "I notice I am confused; therefore something I believe is fiction." (2022,
  https://glowfic.com/replies/1813028#reply-1813028)
- **A Whorfian premise.** "Speaking in Baseline, not just thinking in it, helps reimpose
  abstraction, which is a form of distance; some part of him was starting to go a little Taldane
  from speaking it all the time." (2021, https://glowfic.com/replies/1724101#reply-1724101)

Outside fiction I found almost nothing on constructed languages. There is one 2009 comment on an
E-Prime thread, a question: "Does E-Prime allow "might be useful" or do you need to say "thought you
could use this"?"
(https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/K9mSWuKpZSk7t8FaH/e-prime?commentId=rJpbj5xDNDMTLkDRw). Searches of
roughly 100 fetched posts for "Lojban", "Sapir" and "Whorf" returned zero hits.

### 3f. Term drift and renaming

His theory is the Arbital "Guarded definition" page (2015): "A guarded definition is one where at
least one position suspects there will be pressure to stretch a concept and make it cover more than
it ought to, and so they set aside a term meant to refer narrowly to the things inside the concept."
Stretching such a term is "an unusually strong discourtesy".

Applications:

- **"AI safety" rejected early.** The Arbital "AI alignment" page (created 2015),
  https://arbital.greaterwrong.com/p/ai_alignment: "Other terms, like 'AI safety', understate the
  advocated degree to which alignment ought to be an intrinsic part of building advanced agents.
  E.g., there isn't a separate theory of "bridge safety" for how to build bridges that don't fall
  down." "Control problem" is rejected because it "can sound adversarial".
- **"Alignment" then drifted too.** A tweet quoted in Zvi Mowshowitz's "The Gemini Incident
  Continues" (27 Feb 2024),
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/oJp2BExZAKxTThuuF/the-gemini-incident-continues: "I've given up
  (actually never endorsed in the first place) the term "AI safety"; "AI alignment" is the name of
  the field worth saving. (Though if I can, I'll refer to it as "AI notkilleveryoneism" instead,
  since "alignment" is also coopted to mean systems that scold users.)"
- **"Paperclip maximizer" to "squiggle maximizer".** The LessWrong wiki
  (https://www.lesswrong.com/w/squiggle-maximizer-formerly-paperclip-maximizer, page created 2020)
  says readers took the paperclip example as a badly specified factory goal, and that Yudkowsky has
  since said the intended lesson was an AI converging on an arbitrary internal goal. That is the
  wiki's summary. His own statement is UNVERIFIED here.
- **"Rational".** In the 2012 post above he notes "RationalWiki really needs to rename itself to
  SkepticWiki."

The pattern is a treadmill: coin a narrow term, watch it stretch, coin an uglier one that is harder
to stretch. "Notkilleveryoneism" is deliberately unstretchable. A 2023 LessWrong post argues it
backfires ("'notkilleveryoneism' sounds dumb",
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/jMzBhCRrr7otmqcvK/notkilleveryoneism-sounds-dumb).

### 3g. The pronouns and categories dispute

This is the main case where his philosophy of language was tested in public.

On 26 Nov 2018 he tweeted (https://x.com/ESYudkowsky/status/1067183500216811521): "Some people I
usually respect for their willingness to publicly die on a hill of facts, now seem to be talking as
if pronouns are facts". In a reply the same day
(https://x.com/ESYudkowsky/status/1067198993485058048): "you're not standing in defense of truth if
you insist on a word, brought explicitly into question, being used with some particular meaning."

In a February 2021 Facebook post (https://www.facebook.com/yudkowsky/posts/10159421750419228, not
fetched) he proposed, as quoted by Davis, that "the simplest and best protocol is, '"He" refers to
the set of people who have asked us to use "he", with a default for those-who-haven't-asked that
goes by gamete size'".

Zack M. Davis argues across several long posts that this contradicts the 2008 sequence, which says
category boundaries are not arbitrary and that "I can define a word any way I like" is an error. His
pieces: "Where to Draw the Boundaries?" (2019), "Unnatural Categories Are Optimized for Deception"
(2021,
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/onwgTH6n8wxRSo2BJ/unnatural-categories-are-optimized-for-deception),
"Challenges to Yudkowsky's Pronoun Reform Proposal" (2022,
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/juZ8ugdNqMrbX7x2J/challenges-to-yudkowsky-s-pronoun-reform-proposal)
and the memoir "A Hill of Validity in Defense of Meaning" (2023,
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ZjXtjRQaD2b4PAser/a-hill-of-validity-in-defense-of-meaning).

Yudkowsky's side is that pronoun choice is a speech act and a protocol decision, not an assertion of
fact, so it falls outside what the 2008 theory governs. Scott Alexander's "The Categories Were Made
for Man, Not Man for the Categories" (2014),
https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aMHq4mA2PHSM2TMoH/the-categories-were-made-for-man-not-man-for-the-categories,
makes the related claim that "An alternative categorization system is not an error, and borders are
not objectively true or false." Davis's reply is that categories serve prediction, so a boundary
chosen for other reasons costs predictive accuracy whether or not anyone states a falsehood. Both
sides cite the same 2008 posts.

### 3h. Speech, pronunciation, enunciation

I found no nonfiction statement by Yudkowsky about how rationalists sound, about enunciation, or
about his own speaking style. What exists is adjacent:

- "Say It Loud" opens with Strunk's line "If you don't know how to pronounce a word, say it loud!"
  He uses pronunciation as a metaphor for stating uncertainty: "Any more than mumbling helps them
  understand a word you aren't sure how to pronounce?"
- "Entropy, and Short Codes" has one aside on prosody: "Tolkien once observed what a beautiful sound
  the phrase "cellar door" makes; that is the kind of awareness it takes to use language like
  Tolkien."
- Baseline's phoneme spacing (3e) shows he treats intelligibility under noise as a design target for
  a language.
- "Meta-Honesty" (2018) says a literal-truth code is only workable for people who are "very fast on
  their verbal feet", and he counts himself as one.

The precise-enunciation habit is not something he has theorized. If it has a source in his writing,
it is the indirect one: a norm of literal truth plus a norm that the speaker owns the listener's
misunderstanding.

### 3i. Critiques

- **"It reinvents existing philosophy."** S. Reason, "Situating the Contributions of LessWrong to
  the Philosophy of Language" (2021),
  https://theinexactsciences.github.io/docs/Situating%20the%20Contributions%20of%20LessWrong%20to%20the%20Philosophy%20of%20Language.pdf,
  surveys this charge. The moderate version is that "his philosophy of language, as laid out in "A
  Human's Guide To Words," is wholly reinvention." The author's answer is that the sources (Peirce,
  James, Korzybski, Hayakawa) "are openly cited by Yudkowsky himself", and that Wittgenstein's
  family resemblance anticipates the cluster view. The paper says these criticisms "typically come
  from highly online philosophy graduate students", not from published philosophers. I found no
  peer-reviewed linguist or philosopher response to the sequence.
- **Parallel invention in the academy.** David Chalmers, "Verbal Disputes" (2011),
  https://consc.net/papers/verbal.pdf, proposes a "method of elimination": "one eliminates use of
  the key term, and one attempts to determine whether any substantive dispute remains." That is
  Taboo Your Words three years later. The PDF contains no mention of Yudkowsky or LessWrong (checked
  by text search).
- **The Baseline premise.** Gwern, "On the Existence of Powerful Natural Languages" (2016–2019),
  https://gwern.net/language, argues that designed general-purpose languages do not make speakers
  smarter: "This dream fails because such languages gain power inherently from specialization." A
  conlanger's reconstruction, "The Phonology of Baseline" (2022),
  https://gliese1337.blogspot.com/2022/06, notes the attested Baseline names contradict the
  phoneme-spacing claim: "it appears to contain all three of s/θ/f, which are easily confusable in
  low-fidelity audio environments."
- **The word-length claim holds up, with a correction.** Piantadosi, Tily and Gibson, "Word lengths
  are optimized for efficient communication" (_PNAS_, 2011),
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3048148, found that a word's average information content
  predicts its length better than raw frequency does. I confirmed this from a search summary only
  and did not read the paper.
- **Internal consistency.** Davis (3g).
- **Style.** His prose is criticized on LessWrong itself for length ("The low Information Density of
  Eliezer Yudkowsky & LessWrong", 2024,
  https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/zpCFtGmFzbnfNtvjF/the-low-information-density-of-eliezer-yudkowsky-and),
  which sits oddly beside the short-codes argument.
