# 02: Trader / quant slang and its overlap with rationalist slang

Verification key: claims are sourced to pages I opened unless tagged. **[snippet]** = seen only in a
search-result excerpt, page itself blocked or not opened. **[UNVERIFIED]** = from memory or
secondhand, not confirmed. "My read" = my inference, not a sourced claim.

## 1. Most interesting findings

**1. Jane Street's own interview guide teaches "prior" and "update" as vocabulary, in bold, next to
"sold" and "take 'em".** The 13-page "Probability & Markets" guide (posted 2017, PDF last modified
Aug 2025) has a jargon section ("I'm **2 bid for 10**", "I have **10 at 4**", "I'm **2 at 4, 10
up**", "I'm **out**", say **"sold"** to hit a bid, **"take 'em"** to lift an offer) and, three pages
earlier: "This is my **prior**... As I receive more information... I **update** my beliefs
accordingly." So some of what feels like rationalist slang was already in prop-shop onboarding. A
2019 LessWrong linkpost of the guide says it has "a lot of concepts that are popular here on LW"
(expected value, priors, adverse selection).
[PDF](https://www.janestreet.com/static/pdfs/trading-interview.pdf) (2017/2025);
[LW linkpost](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/RjxmjLKwaXfyenucC/jane-street-inteview-guide-notes-on-probability-markets-etc)
(2019).

**2. Pit grammar is an error-correcting code: direction is encoded three times.** A bid is
price-then-quantity with "for" ("8 for 10"); an offer is quantity-then-price with "at" ("5 at 8").
Palm toward your face means buy, palm out means sell. Fingers show only the last digit of the price
because "everyone knows what the leading digits are", so "zero is a clenched fist". Preposition,
word order and palm each independently carry buy/sell, which is why a half-heard shout was still
parseable. Jane Street still teaches the "for/at" form although CME closed most pits in 2021
[snippet for the 2021 date].
[MacKenzie, LRB](https://www.lrb.co.uk/v29/n21/donald-mackenzie/zero-is-a-clenched-fist) (2007);
[Wikipedia, CBOT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Board_of_Trade) (citing Ryan Carlson's
tradingpithistory.com).

**3. "Adverse selection applied to everyday life" has a traceable route from Jane Street into
LessWrong.** Agustin Lebron (ex-Jane Street) made it Law 2 of _The Laws of Trading_ (2019): "You're
never happy with the amount you traded." An anonymous review of the book was a finalist in the
Astral Codex Ten book review contest (2023). Then Ricki Heicklen (Jane Street equities trader for
2.5 years, now runs trading bootcamps that grew out of Manifest Summer Camp at Lighthaven) posted
"Toward a Broader Conception of Adverse Selection" to LessWrong (2024): the suspiciously empty
subway car, the open NYC parking spot, Thanksgiving leftovers. Her thesis: "conditional on getting
to trade, your trade wasn't all that great." The Jane Street guide's version: "Groucho Marx's
oft-quoted comment 'I don't want to belong to any club that will accept me as a member' is an
appreciation of adverse selection."
[LW post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/vyAZyYh3qsqcJwwPn/towards-a-broader-conception-of-adverse-selection)
(2024); [ACX review](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/your-book-review-the-laws-of-trading) (2023).

**4. The two famous "games" firms disagree about poker.** SIG has used poker in trader training
since at least 2010 (it "teaches and tests decision-making"; about 30% of trainees washed out of
training overall), and a 2024 WSJ piece reportedly says new traders play at least 100 hours of cards
in a ten-week program [secondhand, WSJ original not read]. Jane Street instead invented Figgie in
2013, a card game that simulates open-outcry trading. Ross Rheingans-Yoo (Jane Street 2016-2022, won
its 2021 inter-office Figgie championship) argued on LessWrong that "Poker is a bad game for
teaching epistemics": "Most decisions don't give you feedback on whether you were right for the
right reasons", players "spend the supermajority of their time at the table not playing", and
bluffing instincts are "downright perverse for actual trading." The name: "Figgie is a nickname for
a stock Jane Street traded at the time."
[LW](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PypgeCxFHLzmBENK4/poker-is-a-bad-game-for-teaching-epistemics-figgie-is-a)
(2024);
[Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/philly/business/20100514_PhillyDeals__Poker_games_as_trader_s_test_for_making_quick_decisions.html)
(2010); [figgie.com FAQ](https://figgie.com/faqs).

**5. The canonical SBF-at-Jane-Street betting story is, per an ex-Jane Street trader, a story about
bad EV.** Michael Lewis's _Going Infinite_ (2023) says interns bet each other with losses capped at
$100/day, and that "A Jane Street intern had what amounted to a professional obligation to take any
bet with a positive expected value"; SBF offers "I'll pay a dollar to anyone who will flip me for
ninety-nine dollars" to push a rival to the cap. Heicklen reworks the numbers: after the first flip
his hedge had paid out, so the later flips were negative expectancy; "he was giving out free
expectancy in exchange for volatility." She also translates Lewis into desk-speak ("In trader speak,
Sam buys this contract for $65").
[Asterisk](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/05/michael-lewis-s-blind-side) (2024);
[supplement](https://bayesshammai.substack.com/p/what-to-expect-when-youre-expecting) (2023).

**6. "A bet is a tax on bullshit" came from GMU economists, not traders, and the tax metaphor
predates the famous post.** Tabarrok's line is from 2 Nov 2012, defending Nate Silver's offer to bet
a critic. But Bryan Caplan's "What Does the Betting Norm Tax?" is from March 2009 (the objection he
answers is that a betting norm is "a tax on ideas") [snippet for content], and Robin Hanson's "Could
Gambling Save Science?" (written 1990, published 1995) already says academics should "put up or shut
up" and coins "idea futures". My read: the "bet on it" norm is an economist import that rationalists
adopted; real trading desks have "make me a market", which is a different speech act (you must quote
both sides, so you can't just be loud).
[Tabarrok](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2012/11/a-bet-is-a-tax-on-bullshit.html)
(2012); [Caplan](https://www.econlib.org/archives/2009/03/what_does_the_b.html) (2009);
[Hanson](https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/gamble.html) (1990/1995).

**7. Nate Silver had to append a ~25-page glossary, "How to Speak Riverian", to _On the Edge_
(2024).** He treats poker players, Wall Street, sports bettors, crypto, EA and rationalists as one
community, "the River", defined by "decoupling, contrarianism and a high risk tolerance", against
"the Village" (media, academia, government). A quant reviewer: "They use so many phrases borrowed
from Bayesian statistics and economics... My friends and family laugh at me for not translating out
of River speak before I talk to them." Zvi, who has been in four of those tribes, glosses it as
"Riverians are the Advantage Players of life." This is the closest thing to a published dictionary
of exactly the blend Robbie noticed in himself.
[Silver](https://www.natesilver.net/p/welcome-to-the-river) (2024);
[xquant review](https://xquant.substack.com/p/on-the-edge-a-riverian-memoir-written) (2024);
[Zvi review](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/book-review-on-the-edge-the-fundamentals) (2024).
Glossary entries themselves: not read, see Dead ends.

**8. "Fannie Mae" was bond-desk slang 30 years before it was a brand.** WSJ, 16 May 1938, "Bonds and
Bond Men": "So last week traders tentatively dubbed them the 'Fanny Mays,' an adoption of the
initials of the title." Contested: _Architectural Forum_ the same year credits "Washington's bureau
wags". Ginnie Mae (1968), Sallie Mae and Freddie Mac are copies of the pattern.
[Barry Popik](https://barrypopik.com/blog/fannie_mae_federal_national_mortgage_association).

**9. The 2014 FX-fixing chat transcripts are the best public corpus of real desk chat.** Bloomberg's
write-up quotes: "Don't want other numpty's to know", "add huge value to this cartell", "mess this
up and sleep with one eye open at night", "lets double team it. How much you got" / "ok. 300. U?" /
"ok ill give you 500 more", "Well done gents". Rooms were called "The Cartel", "the 3 musketeers",
"1 team, 1 dream", "the A-team". Regulators had to gloss "ammo" (orders you hold into the fix) and
"taking out the filth" (pre-trading against banks with opposing orders). Note what is absent in
these excerpts: no "ack"; confirmation is "ok" and "done".
[Bloomberg via Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/financial-services/inside-the-cartell-chat-room-traders-boasted-of-whacking-fx-market-1.1999324)
(2014).

**10. FX number/pair slang is a mix of telegraph history, French, and Cockney rhyme.** "Cable"
(GBP/USD) from the transatlantic cable, working from 1866; "yard" (a billion) from French
_milliard_; "Bill and Ben" for yen from 1950s children's TV puppets; the trade "has traditionally
drawn heavily on Cockney rhyming slang" plus bookmakers' terms. Caveat: Wikipedia's cable page cites
no source for the 1866 story, and the first _Times_ quote it mentions (10 Aug 1866) I couldn't
check.
[CS Monitor](https://csmonitor.com/The-Culture/Verbal-Energy/2016/0825/Taking-care-lest-you-trip-over-cable)
(2016).

**11. Zaloom caught "fat finger" as live speech in London in 2000.** In the excerpt from _Out of the
Pits_ (2006), a trader who mis-clicks cries "Ahh, I've fat-fingered it"; the right mouse button is
"the danger button"; the cocky young trader is "The Fetus"; German futures are "the Bund, Bobl, and
Shaz"; a squawk box pipes in a Midwestern voice calling "Merrill's a seller." From MacKenzie's
review: brokers said "a big German" for Deutsche Bank, and pit noise ran from "controlled loud" to
"panic loud", i.e. volume itself was market data.
[UChicago excerpt](https://press.uchicago.edu/Misc/Chicago/978133.php) (2006).

**12. SIG's training vocabulary is rationalist vocabulary.** Notes on SIG's Todd Simkin list "update
hard!", "resulting" (judging a decision by its outcome), "disconfirming propositions", and a
"humility credo" tied to a strong prior on market efficiency. "Resulting" was popularized by Annie
Duke's _Thinking in Bets_ (2018), whose text reportedly says "Poker players have a word for this:
'resulting'" [secondhand reader notes]. Direction: poker room to trading firm to decision-science
books to LessWrong. [Moontower](https://moontowermeta.com/lessons-from-susquehanna/) (2022).

**13. "ack": programmer first, trader by contagion.** The term is the ASCII acknowledge character
(code 6) via the Jargon File. I found exactly one first-person finance attestation: a bond-desk
blogger (2022) lists "Ack" beside "color" and "dry powder", says her team uses it spoken and
written, and that it "spread from a colleague's instant messages." In electronic trading it is also
literal: the exchange's order acknowledgement, and "order-to-ack" is a latency metric [snippet]. My
read: at tech-heavy prop shops it is both at once, but it is not classic voice-desk idiom, where the
confirmations are "done", "you're done", "mine/yours", "sold", "take 'em", "filled", "lifted/given".
[FOLDOC](https://www.foldoc.org/ACK) (entry dated 1997);
[blog](https://travelspot06.blogspot.com/2022/02) (2022).

### Etymology notes

- **bear**: "speculator for a fall" by 1709, short for "bearskin jobber", from the proverb about
  selling the skin before catching the bear ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/bear)).
  "Bull" has no solid origin.
- **blue chip**: from the top-value poker chip; credited to Oliver Gingold of Dow Jones, 1923
  or 1924. Company folklore; Popik says the phrase is in financial pages by 1888 [snippet].
- **dead cat bounce**: earliest known print use _Financial Times_, 7 Dec 1985
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_cat_bounce)).
- **tilt**: from pinball's TILT lockout, then poker
  ([Wikipedia](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilt_(poker)>)); no dated first poker use found.
- **"Betty"**: the Guardian (2014) says cable is "also known as 'betty'" [snippet], presumably Betty
  Grable rhyming with cable [UNVERIFIED]; IG's glossary instead gives Betty = EUR/RUB (Betty Rubble)
  and Barney = USD/RUB [snippet]. Same word, two desks, two meanings.
- **hand signals**: dating is contested. Carlson's site says early 1900s, with "arb" and complexity
  arriving after CME financial futures in 1972; StoneX says early 1970s; another source says 1870s
  [snippets]. https://tradingpithistory.com/

### Human-factors numbers

- Average CME floor trader executed about 60 trades an hour; CFTC Reg 1.38 required trades "executed
  openly and competitively by open outcry"
  ([Parikh & Lohse, CHI '95](https://chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/jll_bdy.htm),
  1995).
- About 1% of trades produce an incident; slips/lapses in 61% of 750 incidents
  ([Leaver & Reader, _Human Factors_](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/66307), 2016). This
  is the aviation human-factors method applied to a trading floor.

## 4. Dead ends / contested

- **Silver's glossary text**: confirmed to exist (title, ~25 pages; one reviewer says 30) but I did
  not read entries; only pirated copies surfaced. Check the book.
- **ACI Model Code, Chapter XI "Market Terminology"** (code first published 1975): this is where
  "mine", "yours", "done", "my risk", "under reference" are formally defined, but only Scribd stubs
  loaded. Worth pulling; it is the closest thing to ICAO phraseology for dealers.
- **Why "mine/yours" instead of "buy/sell", "fifteen" vs "fifty", read-backs**: found no source
  making the error-avoidance argument for trading explicitly. The aviation parallel ("niner",
  "tree", "fife", readback-hearback) is well documented on the aviation side only. The pit
  redundancy in finding 2 is the best evidence I have.
- **Origins of "axe", "color", "hit" vs "lift", "pennying", "pick-off", "puke"**: nothing beyond
  glossary definitions.
- **"ack" on trading desks**: one blog. No evidence either way for Jane Street-style firm chat.
- **"priced in", "bullish on X", "alpha" going mainstream, with dates**: no datable source found.
  Jensen's alpha (1968) and Kelly (1956) are [UNVERIFIED] from memory.
- **Matt Levine coinages**: confirmed "Everything Everywhere Is Securities Fraud" as a column title
  (26 Jun 2019), "Elon Markets Hypothesis" (Feb 2021), and Money Stuff starting about Feb 2015 [all
  snippet]. No compiled list of running jokes exists that I could find.
- **Signals and Threads**: Ron Minsky's Jane Street podcast is engineering (clock sync, multicast,
  build systems). Not a source for trader language.
- **Two Sigma "epsilon"/"omega"**: a Two Sigma article reportedly says staff say "This is just an
  Epsilon" and "What's your Omega?" [snippet, page not opened]. Could not attest "epsilon" or
  "modulo" at other firms.
- **Knorr Cetina/Bruegger, Heath et al. (1994, CSCW 3(2):147-165), Beunza (2019; fieldwork
  1999-2003), Karen Ho (2009)**: bibliographic details confirmed, contents not read. Beunza and Ho
  look to be about models and banker culture more than language.
- **SIG "100 hours of poker in ten weeks"**: attributed to Gunjan Banerji, WSJ, 2024, via Montco
  Today; other sources say a three-month program. Risk.net (2022) calls the
  poker-tournament-recruiting story "something of a modern myth" though "not _too_ far off the
  mark."
- **Caplan's bet record**: reported 23-0 as of 2022 [snippet]; self-reported.
