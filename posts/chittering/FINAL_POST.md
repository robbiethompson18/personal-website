Exiting the cinema at the end of _You Can See Everything_, I felt a distinct sensation from high
school. The crowd was chittering, finger-pointing, gleeful to have spent three hours watching Nathan
Fielder cut down Elizabeth Holmes, Billy Evans, and every other tall, stupid, self-important poppy
in the San Francisco Bay Area.[^selection]

[^selection]:
    This was partly selection. Tuesdays are half-price at AMC. The crowd has few rich young tech
    yuppies, and lots of students and (for lack of a better term, said in jest) future members of
    the permanent underclass.

---

My main takeaway from _You Can See Everything_ was: "Wow, I expected Elizabeth to be a better liar."
It took about 45 seconds for the first unconvincing bout of crocodile tears, at which point I lost a
lot of interest.

It was also striking how _dumb_ Elizabeth and Billy looked. Early in the movie there's a shot of
Billy on the couch listening to a YouTube video explaining biology on the level of "... the
mitochondria, which you'll recall is the powerhouse of the cell."

I am confident that Elizabeth was clueless and scientifically unconvincing. A close family friend
who is a biotech founder and doctor stated very confidently years before the blowup that Theranos
was fraudulent. Theranos would have needed to make massive advances across four fields of
diagnostics to be able to do what they were claiming, and this was implausible given that they had
no peer-reviewed papers. You could get away with this in software, but not in medicine. The FDA
wants papers.

I know startup founders like this, people who are clueless about their own business and focus
exclusively on hoodwinking gullible investors. It is far too common. But I still bristle at the
extent to which the crowd in that theatre drew conclusions about all of Silicon Valley. Trickery
works for your seed round, but big public companies cannot operate in this way.[^accounting] They
have to deliver value. Nothing about Elizabeth Holmes should make you change your opinion of Sergey
Brin or Dario Amodei; they are not the same species.[^crit]

[^accounting]:
    There are many examples of big public companies committing accounting fraud and few examples of
    them faking their technology. (Though some exist, eg Dieselgate.)

[^crit]:
    I have a very different set of criticisms for Dario and Sergey! Possibly they are too ruthless
    and consequentialist, but they are not naked liars larping as technologists like Elizabeth.

The contrast to _The Wolf of Wall Street_ is stark. Jordan Belfort made his fortune from deception.
But he is a beloved character; people [make raps](@belfort-song) in his honour. He is a powerful and
impressive liar, not a bungling and tragicomic one. Powerful and impressive liars imply that the
people they trick aren't stupid. _You Can See Everything_, by highlighting just how clueless
Elizabeth and Billy are, begs the question: "just how dumb is the rest of Silicon Valley?" I dislike
this message. The Silicon Valley of my lived experience does include many VCs who don't know much
about technology,[^vcs] but genuinely brilliant engineers are much more common.[^jb]

[^vcs]:
    My mental model of VCs: tremendously good at reading people, leveraging connections to discover
    things, and getting people to cooperate. Bad at futurism and engineering. They know which people
    they invest in are overconfident and slippery with the truth; both of these things are strongly
    positively correlated with being a successful founder.

[^jb]:
    In my gut I watch _The Wolf of Wall Street_ and I laugh: the guy ripped off idiots who should've
    known better and then acted like a playboy. It's a comedy. In my head I know I shouldn't be
    happy that we're glorifying fraud. Clearly this is bad; see the SBF section below. Yet somehow I
    don't have an emotional response to this; I feel confident that we will keep catching and
    punishing fraud. Maybe the Trump presidency is a counterexample and I should be more upset.

---

_The Altruists_ was upsetting for different reasons. I confess I only watched half of the first
episode.

There are many parallels between my life and SBF's, which makes my family cackle. We both took a job
at Jane Street right out of college. Both of us are effective altruists and polyamorists and former
vegans.

The conclusions you're meant to draw from that show:

- Being calculating and rational inevitably leads to ruthless consequentialism.
- Being calculating and rational means you're socially inept.
- Being calculating and rational leads you to take excessive risks, often on others' behalf.

There's a lot to dislike about SBF. He is a ruthless lying power-seeking consequentialist. Unlike
Elizabeth Holmes, he's smart enough to be extremely convincing to an educated interlocutor. His
story merits cinema much more than Holmes'. But I dislike the implication that him being evil is
downstream of his calculation and rationality. The world is full of calculating and rational people
who truly are altruists. Even the title of the show implies otherwise.

---

This essay reads as: "why can't Hollywood be pushing _my_ particular agenda?!?" That's too much to
ask, I understand. Just know, 'Hollywood,' and friends who would ask me to see a movie with them:
this is why I don't want to watch.
