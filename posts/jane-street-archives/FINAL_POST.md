## Nerd Heaven

I spent the first two weeks of my Jane Street internship at the Sheraton in Parsippany, New Jersey.
The previous summer, Jane Street began its internship by renting out a hotel in upstate New York to
use as a COVID bubble. Summer camp went so well that they decided to do it again the next year, for
fun.

I found myself in something akin to Rick Riordan’s Lotus Hotel, but designed for nerds. Pinball
machines and old Atari video games cluttered the lobby. Row upon row of computer terminals,
monitors, headsets, custom keyboards, and wifi repeaters filled the ballroom. At the end of every
hallway stood tables filled with snacks and well-stocked refrigerators: Ito-En, Cold Brew, Tony’s
Chocolonely, That’s It energy bars.

The only thing I couldn’t find was my fellow interns. The hotel’s least popular attraction, the pool
and hot tub, contained only two or three out of 180. At last, I found them: in a windowless
conference room, the rules to a made-up poker variant were written down on a massive whiteboard.
Hundreds or thousands of dollars were riding on each game.

Everyone spoke using vocabulary borrowed heavily from the symbology of math papers (‘epsilon,’
‘Nash,’ ‘exploitable,’ ‘Beta’). I had instant fluency in a language I had read but never spoken. It
was thrilling; like Harry Potter must've felt speaking Parseltongue for the first time.

I arrived in nerd heaven expecting to feel at home like I never had before. I wrote hundreds of
lines of code to optimize drafting my fantasy football team. During my free time on my gap year I
worked through the problems in Knuth’s _The Art of Computer Programming_. My siblings called me
‘Robotie’ instead of ‘Robbie’ growing up after they caught me reading an anthropology book about
facial expressions, unfortunately titled _Emotions Revealed._

In college I was welcome in many places but normal nowhere, my personality too eccentric and
contradictory. I foraged for weeds on my barefoot runs to class and raced pledges in consuming beer
and raw onions and gave up toothpaste for a year on a hunch that nothing bad would happen. So trust
me when I tell you that the nerdy and bizarre responses of my fellow interns on our get-to-know-you
questionnaire elated me. At Jane Street I was downright mainstream. I would later learn that a
fellow intern said this about me: “That kid Robbie, he doesn’t wear shoes, yet somehow he’s like the
third most-normal guy here.”[^1]

## It’s Plus EV

The first thing you learn as an intern is that the only important thing about a bet is its
[expected value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_value) (almost). For small amounts of money,
risk is irrelevant. And that’s ‘small’ by Jane Street standards. There’s a man who walks around the
Christmas party every year with about
$40k in hundred dollar bills who will flip a coin for as much
money as you like,[^6] with an informal minimum of $100.
He gets hundreds of takers.

[^6]: As long as it’s less than what’s in his hand.

Tyler’s birthday fell on the third day of the internship.[^7] We sang him happy birthday at dinner.
Doug, a fulltimer, had a Cheshire Cat grin on his face as soon as the singing began. Once the final
note sounded he promptly got up, telling our table “I’m going to go give that kid some money.”

[^7]: Tyler and Doug are fake names.

Doug sauntered over to Tyler and told him the deal: “I’m going to flip a coin. If it’s heads, you’ll
pay me $n$ dollars. If it’s tails, I’ll pay you $3n$ dollars. You get to pick $n$.” Tyler greeted
this proposition not with a slack jaw or a furrowed brow, but with a grin. This was by far the most
generous bet anyone proposed in my time at Jane Street. Doug knew how good this deal was, and to
Doug’s surprise, Tyler did too.

“Up to a thousand!” Doug blurted.

“Done. One thousand it is.”

When Doug came back to our table, he told us this story. “Thank God the kid won,” he sighed. He
opened his wallet, counted 27 Benjamins, and called over another fulltimer to get the rest of the
cash he needed. He insisted on $100 bills. Within 3 minutes of losing the flip, he handed Tyler
$3000
in cash.

Any intern unwilling to take bets like this was a schmuck. At work dinners, fulltimers would
frequently offer interns coin flips where they stood to win $102 or lose $100. If you said no, you
would get grilled, implicitly accused of behaving irrationally.

It was OK to accept a -EV bet that had entertainment value. Sebastian, a friend of mine, found
several people willing to gamble that he couldn’t eat an orange peel in a minute. Once all the bets
were in, he tried and failed. I was at the height of my frat era, so an orange peel was light work.
I resolved to try the next day.

After finishing work, my mentor asked me where I was headed. I told him about the orange peel I was
about to eat. He came along to watch, and wagered a bunch of money on me. “I want to support you!”
he said.

The peel was easy to swallow in a minute, as I had expected. But the huge number of people willing
to bet against me made me too conservative. I made about $250 from my wagers, my mentor about $350.

I tell you all this, and yet one of the most frequent topics of discussion around lunch in
Parsippany was ‘interns are so conservative with their gambling these days. Back when I was an
intern…’ The previous summer, a girl had made five figures betting on how many free throws she’d
make out of 50.

## Out of Your League

About 1/3 of the Jane Street internship is lectures and homework, and about 1/3 is spent on research
projects. But the most fun part by far is mock trading.

If you want a taste of what mock trading games are like, I suggest you go to
[figgie.com](http://figgie.com). There you can learn and play a card game designed to teach Jane
Street interns how to trade. It will only take you ten minutes to learn the rules, but there’s
enough complexity in strategy to keep interns thinking for weeks.[^8]

[^8]: Figgie is a great game and I would still play it for fun.

As the summer goes on, games more and more resemble real markets. You play in a team of four on a
simulated electronic exchange, with both bots and other Jane Street teams trading against you. The
rules are different every time, but usually your job is to figure out which bot is making bad trades
and transact with it before the other teams do.

Good mock traders can do math and game theory in their head. They shout whenever good opportunities
arise. They type at blazing fast speed. Their brains simultaneously process several information
streams: order books, price graphs, two teammates’ unrelated mathematical arguments, a rule change
from over the loudspeaker, a sigh of displeasure from a coach. In the mock trading room there is a
3-foot-diameter gong that the organizers strike as each round begins. In bad mock traders this
sound, and everything else about the experience, induces panic.

Many a genius has been humbled by mock. I once sat down excited to trade, until a teammate pointed
behind us and said “you see that team we’re up against? Three out of four won a gold medal at the
IMO.”[^9] During my internship, someone looked up the top 100 eighth graders at math when we were
all in eighth grade, and discovered that 35 of them were current interns.

[^9]:
    About 50 kids win an IMO (International Math Olympiad) gold medal each year, and the IMO is
    _the_ competition for youth math.

## Think Like a Streeter

When trading, you almost always think in terms of expected value, not risk. So I’ve never split the
bill at a dinner with Streeters. We pull straws[^10], and the loser pays. You pay the same amount as
you would splitting the bill, on average.

[^10]:
    Really, we use
    [Chwazi](https://www.reddit.com/r/boardgames/comments/180tf5l/chwazi_is_an_amazing_free_app_that_helps_randomly/),
    an iPhone app. Everyone puts down a finger on the screen, and after a couple seconds one
    person’s finger lights up.

Trading requires paranoia. If a stock is trading below its true value, why hasn’t someone else
bought it already? And who is stupid enough to sell it to you below market value? Are you the
sucker? This effect is called adverse selection. Trading teaches you to see it everywhere. An empty
restaurant is adverse. So is the Citibike closest to the subway station. The bar that let you in
without a cover, the out-of-your-league girl you matched with on Hinge, the food left over at the
summer party. If these are good deals, why hasn’t someone taken them already? You’ll make better
decisions thinking this way, but you’ll also be cynical.
