## Nerd Heaven

I spent the first two weeks of my Jane Street internship at the Sheraton in Parsippany, New Jersey.
The previous summer, Jane Street began its internship by renting out a hotel in upstate New York to
use as a COVID bubble. The interns had liked adult summer camp so much that they decided to do it
again the next year, for fun.

When I arrived, I found myself in something akin to Rick Riordan’s Lotus Hotel, but designed for
nerds. Pinball machines and old Atari video games cluttered the lobby. Row upon row of computer
terminals, monitors, headsets, custom keyboards, and wifi repeaters filled the ballroom. (I would
later learn that this was the ‘trading floor’). At the end of every hallway stood tables filled with
snacks and well-stocked refrigerators: Ito-En, Cold Brew, Tony’s Chocolonely, That’s It energy bars.
The only thing I couldn’t find were my fellow interns. The hotel’s least popular attraction, the
pool and hot tub, contained only two or three out of 180. At last, I found them: in a windowless
conference room, the rules to a made-up poker variation were written down on a massive whiteboard.
Only Jane Street branded chips and cards were on the table, but hundreds or thousands of dollars
were riding on each game. I felt like I was hearing a new language for the first time that I
understood instantly: spoken vocabulary borrowed heavily from the symbology of mathematical papers
(‘epsilon,’ ‘Nash,’ ‘exploitable,’ ‘Beta’).

I arrived in nerd heaven expecting to feel at home like I never had before. I wrote hundreds of
lines of code to optimize drafting my fantasy football team. During my free time on my gap year I
worked through the problems in Knuth’s _The Art of Computer Programming_. My siblings call me
‘Robotie’ instead of ‘Robbie’ growing up after they caught me reading an anthropology booked about
facial expressions, unfortunately titled _Emotions Revealed._

In college I was welcome many places but normal nowhere, my personality too eccentric and
contradictory. I foraged for weeds on my barefoot runs to class and raced pledges in consuming beer
and raw onions and gave up toothpaste for a year on a hunch that nothing bad would happen. So
hopefully you can trust me when I tell you that the nerdy and bizarre responses of my fellow interns
on our get-to-know-you questionnaire elated me. At Jane Street I was downright mainstream. I would
later learn that a fellow intern said this about me: “That kid Robbie, he doesn’t wear shoes, yet
somehow he’s the like the third most-normal guy here.”[^1]

Here are some “hobbies and interests” from our the questionnaire. [^2]

- “CTFs (the non-physical kind)[^3], video games (CSGO, Final Fantasy, Last of Us, etc.), drinking
  OJ”

- “AR/VR, Retrocomputing, Cooking/Baking and video production”
- “Sport Climbing, Custom Keyboards, Crypto”

My hobbies and interests were “Climbing, running, foraging, yoga, reading, teaching, all things

## It’s Plus EV

The first thing you learn as an intern is that the only important thing about a bet is its
[expected value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_value) (almost). For small amounts of money,
risk is irrelevant. And that’s ‘small’ by Jane Street standards. There’s a man who walks around the
Christmas party every year with about
$40k in hundred dollar bills who will flip a coin for as much money as you like,[^6] with an informal minimum of $100.
He gets hundreds of takers. But I’m getting ahead of myself.

Tyler’s birthday fell on the third day of the internship.[^7] We sang him happy birthday at dinner.
Doug, a fulltimer, had a Cheshire Cat grin on his face as soon as the singing began. Once the final
note sounded he promptly got up, telling our table “I’m going to go give that kid some money.”

Doug sauntered over to Tyler and told him the deal: “I’m going to flip a coin. If it’s heads, you’ll
pay me _n_ dollars. If it’s tails, I’ll pay you _3n_ dollars. You get to pick _n_.” Tyler greeted
this proposition not with a slack jaw or a furled brow, but with a grin. This was by far the most
generous bet anyone proposed in my time at Jane Street. Doug knew how good this deal was, and to
Doug’s surprise, it seemed Tyler did too.

“Up to a thousand!” Doug blurted.

“Done. One thousand it is.”

When Doug came back to our table, he told us this story. “Thank God the kid won,” he sighed. He
opened his wallet, counted 27 Benjamins, and called over another fulltimer to get the rest of the
cash he needed. He insisted on
$100 bills. Within 3 minutes of losing the flip, he handed Tyler $3000 in cash.

Any intern unwilling to take bets like this was a schmuck. At work dinners, fulltimers would
frequently offer interns coin flips where they stood to win $102 or lose $100. If you said no, you
would get grilled, implicitly accused of behaving irrationally.

Not all intern bets were so mathematical. People constantly looked for deals that had provide
entertainment value. Sebastian, a friend of mine, found several people willing to gamble that he
couldn’t eat an orange peel in a minute. Once all the bets were in, he tried and failed. I was at
the height of my frat era, so an orange peel seemed like light work. I resolved to try the next day.

After finishing work, my mentor asked me where I was headed. I told him about the orange peel I was
about to eat. He came along to watch, and wagered a bunch of money on me. “I want to support you!”
he told me.

The peel was easy to swallow in a minute, as I had expected. But the huge number of people willing
to bet against me made me too conservative. I made about $250 from my wagers, my mentor about $350.

I tell you all this, and yet one of the most frequent topics of discussion around lunch in
Parsippany was ‘interns are so conservative with their gambling these days. Back when I was an
intern…’ The previous summer, a girl had made five figures betting on how many free throws she’d
make out of 50.

## Out of Your League

About 1/3 of the Jane Street internship is lectures and homework, and about 1/3 is spent on research
projects. But these are relatively unimportant. Interns can’t be given enough IP to work on valuable
research, though most mentors do a decent job convincing their mentees otherwise. The part of the
internship that matters most is mock trading.

If you want a taste of what mock trading games are like, I suggest you go to
[figgie.com](http://figgie.com). There you can learn and play a card game designed to teach Jane
Street interns how to trade. It will only take you ten minutes to learn the rules, but there’s
enough complexity in strategy to keep interns thinking for weeks.[^8]

Beyond figgie, the games grow closer and closer to what actually markets look like. You normally
play in a team of four on a simulated electronic exchange, with both bots and other Jane Street
teams trading against you. The rules are different every time, but usually you job is to figure out
which bot is making bad trades and transact with it before the other teams do.

Good mock traders can do math and game theory in their head. They shout whenever good opportunities
arise. They type at blazing fast speed. Their brains simultaneously process countless information
streams: order books, price graphs, two teammates’ unrelated mathematical arguments, a rule change
from over the loudspeaker, a sigh of displeasure from a coach. In the mock trading room there is a 3
foot diameter gong that the organizers strike as each round begins. In bad mock traders this sound,
and everything else about the experience, induces panic.

Many a genius has been humbled by mock. I once sat down excited to trade, until a teammate pointed
behind us and said “you see that team we’re up against? Three out of four won a gold medal at the
International Math Olympiad.”[^9] During my internship, someone looked up the top 100 eighth graders
at math when we were all in eighth grade, and discovered that 35 of them were current interns.

## Think Like a Streeter

When trading, you almost always think in terms of expected value, not risk. So I’ve never split the
bill at a dinner with Streeters. We pull straws[^10], and the loser pays. You pay the same amount as
you would splitting the bill, on average.

Trading requires paranoia. If a stock is trading below its true value, why hasn’t someone else
bought it already? And who is stupid enough to sell it to you below market value? Often you’re the
one making the mistake. We call this adverse selection. Once you’re aware of the effect, you see it
everywhere. An empty restaurant is adverse. So is the Citibike closest to the subway station. The
bar that let you in without a cover, the out-of-your-league girl you matched with on Hinge, the food
left over at the summer party. If these are good deals, why hasn’t someone taken them already?
You’ll make better decisions thinking this way, but you’ll also be cynical.
